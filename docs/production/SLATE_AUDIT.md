**TC-001 — Padmāvatī and the minister's son.**
- **Engine:** a cipher-courtship decoded by a clever adviser turns into a witchcraft frame-up, and her parents die of grief.
- **Sources:** KSS 1 (12.8.66–195), Ś 1, Hindi I (adds the kotwal, and the penalty is hell), J 1 (title only).
- **Q/A:** who bears the sin of the parents' deaths (186–187)? **The king**, who banished her without inquiry (190–195). The Vetāla objects with "crows and geese" (191). Reasoning: the adviser "served his master"; the lovers, "burned by love", are not to be judged.
- **Caste/class:** L–M. Padmāvatī is an ivory-carver's daughter, an artisan family used and discarded by royal schemers. **Keep her origin.** Making her a princess (as Platts's Hindi may) would erase the class asymmetry.
- **Gender:** H. From 12.8.153 on she is drugged, branded, defamed as a *ḍākinī* and removed. The scheme is told as cleverness.
- **Power:** the king's unexamined verdict, which the source itself condemns.
- **Roles:** prince E · adviser E (his cleverness is the engine) · Padmāvatī E · king E (ruler and judge is the point) · nurse A · magistrate I.
- **Adaptation:** keep the answer and the Vetāla's objection at full strength. **Drop the "love's arrows" and "served his master" exemptions** (user safeguard: accomplice guilt). The adviser and the prince did their own wrongs; the *deaths* belong to the one with the duty to inquire. Tell the branding as a violation, with the prince's hand visible. Give Padmāvatī a moment of **understanding**, not an invented choice, after the banishment; the source does not record her consent to leaving, and that silence stays felt. Keep the ambiguity over whether she poisoned anyone, so the same moral standard applies to her.
- **Must keep:** the signs (at least one decoded), the false story told to authority, the king's verdict, the parents' deaths, the objection.
- **May alter:** the number of signs, the dog, the Benares opening. Set up the parents early (now one verse).
- **Survives:** yes, and harder. **ADAPT**, M. *Open:* her poisoning intent (N1:534); Sanskritist check of 186–195.

**TC-002 — Three suitors revive Mandāravatī.**
- **Engine:** three devotions in parallel; the revival triggers rivalry, and the verdict sorts the claimants into kin roles.
- **Sources:** KSS 2 (12.9). Ś 2 differs materially: snakebite, a suitor dies on the pyre, the roles are father/brother/husband. Hindi follows KSS. J unread.
- **Q/A:** whose wife (36)? Reviver = father, bone-bearer = "counted" son, ash-keeper = "called" husband (38–40).
- **Caste (revised, rule 5):** the setting is a brahman settlement and a learned household, with suitors from Kānyakubja. The life-giving spell is in a householder's book, and the guest refuses food in what he takes for a "brahman-demon's house" (N1, 12.9.5–23). Their identity is **connected (5a)** to who holds learning and spells, so it is **kept** and portrayed straightforwardly: devoted, learned and grief-stricken. This also balances the slate (§5.3). *My first draft dropped it. Withdrawn.*
- **Gender: what the source actually gives her** (N1, 12.9.8–40):
  - **Choices:** none recorded. She does not speak or choose at any point.
  - **Constraints:** three suitors each declare they would sooner die than see her given to another; her father, fearing that two would die, gives her to none; the three stay on, gazing at her day and night.
  - **Wants:** unrecorded.
  - **After her revival:** three men immediately quarrel over her, "overcome with desire"; the text gives no reaction from her.
- **What was wrong with my first draft** (user's catch): "she declines to choose, knowing a refusal costs a life" made her **responsible for the men's threatened suicides**. The threat is the suitors'; the decision to withhold her is her father's. **Withdrawn.**
- **Smallest change that gives her real agency without making her answerable for men's choices:**
  1. **She has and voices a wish of her own** before her death. For example, she will not be the prize that settles their rivalry, or she is not ready to be given. The wish is *her own*, not a response to their threats.
  2. Her wish is **overruled**: her father acts on his fear of what *the men* have threatened. The telling assigns each piece of the pressure to the person who exerts it: the threats to the suitors, the decision to her father.
  3. **At her revival, she is a person in the room**: she wakes, she perceives, and her first moments of life are taken over by three men claiming her. The source's own abrupt turn from devotion to desire (12.9.33) is told from where she stands.
  4. **The verdict stays** (father, son, husband by act; "is counted", "is called"). Betal's question and Vikram's answer happen in the frame, over her head, and the reader is left with the question the source never asks: what did *she* want?
- **Honest limit:** within this dilemma she **cannot determine the outcome**. If she chose a husband, the classification riddle would collapse (gender reviewer). Her agency here is real in desire, voice and perception, but not decisive. I report that plainly. Deeper influence would need a different dilemma, and I do not recommend that.
- **Violence:** a householder's wife throws a child into fire "in anger" (*krudhā*). Keep the episode, and don't make it a statement about women.
- **Roles:** three suitors E (one act each) · Mandāravatī E · father A · householder couple A.
- **Must keep:** three acts, each mapping onto a kin role; the revival; the dispute.
- **May alter:** the means of getting the spell (risky), the fever, the setting.
- **Survives:** yes. **ADAPT**, M–H. *Open:* Ś 2 specialist check; the revival overlaps with 022.

**TC-008 — The three fastidious brothers.**
- **Engine:** a triple test of hyper-sensitivity (food, women, bed), each a mini-detective story.
- **Sources:** KSS 8 (12.15), Ś 23, Hindi XXIII, J 5 (careless per Emeneau).
- **Q/A:** most fastidious? → **the bed-connoisseur**: his mark was seen on his own body, while the other two could have been told in advance (51; KSS only).
- **Caste: H. Revision 2, applying the creative director's direction.**
  - **What the source connects** (N1 TC-008, 12.15.3–47): the setting is a brahman settlement. The father, Viṣṇusvāmin, is a wealthy sacrificer who has *begun a sacrifice* and needs a turtle. Each son refuses to touch the "slimy, smelly" turtle: the eldest because he is fastidious about food, the second about women, the youngest about beds. The **eldest warns that failing their father's sacrifice means hell, and the younger two laugh that he knows duty only for others** (6–14). They quarrel out of pride (*abhimāna*, 15) and go to the king. The food test is rice "smelling of corpse-smoke", grown beside a village cremation ground. They stay on happily, "having earned the sin of obstructing their father's sacrifice" (47).
  - **So the identity is connected (rule 5a).** Their being sons of a sacrificing household is what gives the comedy its point: they have refined ritual sensitivity until it defeats the ritual itself. **The brahman identity is kept and visible.** My draft (and the challenger) removed it. **Withdrawn**, and the caste reviewer's position is largely vindicated.
  - **The satire's target, named in the telling:** the brothers' **abhimāna**, refinement turned into a claim of rank, and the contradiction the source itself states. Their disgust is so exquisite that they abandon the duty it supposedly serves, and each knows duty only for others. The target is *these three men's* vanity, not brahmans.
  - **The rest of the tale's world counters it** (rule 8). Their father is an earnest sacrificer left with an unfinished rite. The king tests them coolly, and the courtesan turns the joke (below). The narration never implies that the vanity belongs to their birth group.
  - **The caste implication of the corpse-smoke test is not hidden** (5a/5b). The disgust at rice grown beside a cremation ground is the purity-pollution idea in small. The people who work and farm by the burning ground are present for a moment as people, so the brother's disgust visibly lands on *someone*, without a word of commentary. The reader sees the hierarchy the delicacy depends on.
  - The king's verdict still rests on evidence. The tale does not endorse any brother's purity claim.
- **Gender and class:** L–M. The courtesan is a test object, and the caste reviewer warns that "a low-status woman's body as the polluting object" mirrors untouchability's smell-and-proximity logic. The two reviewers **disagree**:
  - Caste reviewer: replace her with an object test.
  - Gender reviewer: that over-corrects, so keep her.
  - **Lead's reconciliation:** keep her and **give her the scene and the joke**. Her goat-milk upbringing is known around town (which plants Vikram's "could have been told" reasoning), and the disgust lands on the brother's vanity, not on her body.
  - The corpse-smoke rice must not invite aversion to people who work at cremation grounds.
- **Roles:** brothers E (sons of a sacrificing household: connected) · father E (his unfinished rite is the irony) · king A · courtesan A · cremation-ground farmers or workers I → made briefly visible.
- **Survives:** yes. **ADAPT** as satire of *abhimāna*, M–H. Sensitivity reader prioritised (§9.4). TC-011 (its rival) is rejected, so there is no slot conflict.

**TC-013 — Harisvāmin and the poisoned rice.**
- **Engine:** pure kindness, poison dropped by mindless agents; the verdict condemns whoever judges hastily.
- **Sources:** KSS 13 (12.20), Ś 12 (no hawk), Hindi XII (the wife fed him "at her husband's bidding"), J 12 unread.
- **Q/A:** whose is the sin of **brahman-murder** (*brahmahatyā*)? → **none of them; the hasty judge** (56–59).
- **Caste:** M. *Brahmahatyā* is a caste-weighted sin category: killing a brahman is ranked as the gravest. **The worth-weighting is removed from the narrated question (5b)**: it asks about the guilt of *this man's death*. The answer stays; the dilemma is untouched. Harisvāmin's and his host's brahman identity is **kept** (5a/balance): a sympathetic victim and a kind household, which counterweights the slate (§5.3). The caste weighting survives only in the dying man's own accusation, as his belief.
