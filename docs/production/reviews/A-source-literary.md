## Part 1: The 19 candidates

### TC-001 Padmāvatī and the minister's son (B-001; N1:90–103)
- **Engine:** a cipher-courtship run by a clever adviser grows into a framing for witchcraft, and the parents die. Then the question: who bears the sin of their deaths?
- **Events:**
  1. The prince sees a girl at a lake who speaks only in signs (12.8.66–76).
  2. The adviser decodes the signs (77–86).
  3. More signs come through the nurse; the couple make a secret *gāndharva* union, an informal marriage by mutual consent (87–131).
  4. The poisoned-food episode, followed by the adviser's accusation against her (132–149).
  5. She is made drunk and branded with a trident, and her jewels are taken (153–161).
  6. In disguise as an ascetic, the adviser tells the magistrate a false *yoginī* story (162–177).
  7. The king banishes her as a *ḍākinī*, a flesh-eating witch (178–181).
  8. The couple take her away (182–184).
  9. Her parents die of grief (185).
- **Cast (as given):** Prince Vajramukuṭa, son of King Pratāpamukuṭa of Vārāṇasī; Buddhiśarīra, "the minister's son"; Padmāvatī, daughter of the ivory-carver (*dantaghāṭaka*) Saṅgrāmavardhana; the old nurse, "motivated by gifts"; King Karṇotpala of Kaliṅga, "ruler and judge"; the city magistrate (N1:95, 99).
- **Traditions:**
  - KSS 1, BKM 1 (provisional), Ś 1, J 1 (title only), Hindi I, Ryder 1 (B-001:8).
  - The Hindi adds the kotwal (city police chief) as a fourth party and makes the penalty hell. Ś gives the same answer, compressed to *avicāreṇa*, "through lack of inquiry" (N1:102).
  - Jørgensen lists "the sign language in tale 1" as a feature the Newari shares with J (N3:144).
- **Question and answer:**
  - Q: what sin falls on the minister's son, the prince or Padmāvatī (12.8.186–187)? The penalty formula follows at 188.
  - A: none of them; the sin is King Karṇotpala's (190).
  - Betal objects: are crows blamed when geese eat the rice? (191)
  - Reasoning: the son did his master's business; the lovers, burned by love, are not to be judged; the king acted without inquiry (192–195).
- **Evidence:** moderate. "Padmāvatī's intent with the poisoned food is reported only through the dog's death and the minister's son's explanation" (N1:534). The king's dead son "is not otherwise introduced" (N1:103). Ś's and the Hindi's narrative bodies were not read (B-001:41).
- **[Critic]:**
  - Very high suspense in the first two-thirds: each sign is a small puzzle with a payoff, and the duel of wits with Padmāvatī is lively.
  - Structural weakness: the question's subject, the parents' deaths, takes one verse (N1:101). They must be set up earlier or the verdict lands cold.
  - The ambiguous poisoning is an asset: readers will half-suspect the adviser.
  - Memorable: the sign language, the crow-and-goose objection, and a surprising verdict that clears the people who did the deed.
  - Overlaps: blame form with 013, 022, 027; "the king is guilty" with 027; a schemer-helper deceiving elders with 026; a secret *gāndharva* union and ascetic disguise with 015.
- **Load-bearing:** the adviser's indispensable cleverness (at least one decoded sign); the false story told to authority; the king's unexamined verdict; the parents' deaths; Betal's objection.
- **Decorative:** the full list of signs, the nurse's slaps, the dog, the opening description of Benares, the rope-seat.

### TC-002 Three brahman youths revive a dead girl (B-002; N1:105–118)
- **Engine:** three devotions run in parallel and converge at the cremation ground; the revival turns at once into rivalry, and the verdict sorts the claimants into kin roles.
- **Events:**
  1. Three youths each want Mandāravatī; her father gives her to none (12.9.5–11).
  2. She dies of fever. One suitor keeps a vigil on her ashes, one takes her bones to the Ganges, one wanders as an ascetic (12–15).
  3. The ascetic sees a householder's wife throw a crying child into the fire, and the child revived by a spell (16–23).
  4. He steals the spell-book and revives her (24–32).
  5. The three quarrel (33–35).
- **Cast:** Agnisvāmin, a learned brahman; his daughter Mandāravatī, who "does not speak or choose" (B-002:15); three "equally accomplished" brahman youths; the householder of Vakrolaka and his wife.
- **Traditions:**
  - Ś 2 differs materially: she dies of snakebite; one suitor dies on her pyre and is revived with her; the roles become father, **brother** and husband. No bone-bearer, no son (N1:117).
  - The Hindi follows KSS's roles (N1:117). J has not been read.
- **Question and answer:**
  - Q: whose wife does she properly become (12.9.36)?
  - A: the reviver is her father; the bone-bearer is "counted (*mataḥ*)" her son; the ash-keeper "is called (*ucyate*)" her husband (38–40).
- **Evidence:** moderate. Ś 2 is flagged for a specialist check (B-002:32). Whether the wife burned the child in anger or as a demonstration: "the text says only *krudhā*, 'in anger' (18)" (N1:118).
- **[Critic]:**
  - Strong premise and a shocking hinge (the child in the fire), but the suitors are interchangeable and the woman is a prize with no voice.
  - The answer is a witty taxonomy rather than a moral struggle, which is good as a puzzle and weak in emotional consequence.
  - Overlaps: revival with 022 (and divine revivals in 006, 016, 021); allocation and kinship naming with 006, 015, 019, 024.
- **Load-bearing:** three acts that each map onto a kin role; one suitor obtains the means of revival; the revival; the claim dispute. If Ś's cast is used, the pyre-death replaces the Ganges journey.
- **Decorative:** the Yamunā setting, the fever, and the specific child-in-fire episode. Some means of obtaining the spell must remain, but this one is replaceable at the teller's risk.

### TC-003 The parrot and the mynah: are men or women worse? (B-003; N1:120–133)
- **Engine:** a wager between two birds, settled by two cautionary tales; then a generalising question.
- **Events:**
  1. The prince's learned parrot and the princess's mynah wager "slavery or wifehood" (12.10.4–15).
  2. **The mynah's tale:** the merchant's son Dhanadatta robs his wife Ratnāvalī and throws her into a pit; she protects him anyway; he later murders her (15–46).
  3. **The parrot's tale:** Vasudattā's lover is hanged; a Vetāla in his corpse bites off her nose; she blames her husband; a thief-witness clears him; she is mutilated and banished (47–88).
  4. The birds are freed from their curse; the dispute is left unresolved (89–91).
- **Cast:** two cursed celestials in bird form; their owners, a prince and a Magadha princess; a merchant's son and a merchant's daughter; a merchant's daughter, her husband and her lover; a thief; a king.
- **Traditions:**
  - Ś 3: King Rūpasena, the parrot Vidagdhacūḍāmaṇi, the mynah Madanamañjarī; the question is "whose sin is greater?"; a verse says women are to blame.
  - Hindi IV (order swapped with TC-004) gives an explicit reason: a depraved man keeps some sense of right and wrong, a woman gives it no thought (N1:132).
  - J has not been read.
- **Question and answer:**
  - Q: men or women, which are wicked (12.10.92)?
  - A: women. A man "of that kind" may occasionally be found, but women are so generally (93–94). "No further reasoning is given" (B-003:21).
- **Evidence:** moderate. "Why the husband in the parrot's tale stays silent is not explained (78)" (N1:133). The gendered answer is singled out for N9, the planned review of moralising and gendered passages (Rep:137).
- **[Critic]:**
  - The richest texture in the slate: a frame inside the frame, a Vetāla inside a tale told to Vikrama, macabre comedy.
  - The dilemma is thin, because the verdict does not follow from two balanced cases. That gap is its literary potential, but exploiting it departs from the recorded answer.
  - The two long embedded tales risk swamping the riddle.
  - Not in any comparison group; distinct in form.
- **Load-bearing:** the birds' wager; one tale of a man's betrayal and one of a woman's; the sweeping question.
- **Decorative:** the curse backstory, the names, the pit details. The Vetāla biting the nose is optional to the plot but highly memorable.

### TC-006 The transposed heads (B-006; N1:165–178)
- **Engine:** shock, then a miracle, then a mistake made in haste, which produces an identity puzzle.
- **Events:**
  1. The washerman Dhavala marries Madanasundarī (12.13.4–17).
  2. At Gaurī's temple he beheads himself "by fate" (18–31).
  3. Her brother does the same, "deluded" (32–33).
  4. She reproaches the goddess and prepares to hang herself; a voice from the sky tells her to rejoin the heads (34–45).
  5. She joins each head to the wrong body (46–50).
- **Cast:** washermen (*rajaka*, 12.13.7–8): Dhavala (husband), Madanasundarī (a washerman's daughter), her unnamed brother; the goddess Gaurī as a voice (N1:178).
- **Traditions:**
  - Ś 6: same answer, by a verse: "among all the limbs the head is chief".
  - Hindi VI: the same principle, cited from "the book of law" (N1:177).
  - J 8 has not been read.
- **Question and answer:**
  - Q: which man is her husband (12.13.51)?
  - A: the one with her husband's head (53).
  - Reasoning: the head is chief of the limbs, and recognition (*pratyabhijñā*) resides in it (53).
- **Evidence:** moderate to high for identity (B-006:80). The motives are only "fate" (*vidhipreraṇa*) and "delusion" (*vyāmohita*) (N1:535).
- **[Critic]:**
  - The cleanest and most iconic puzzle in the slate: compact, physical, arguable both ways. The woman is the agent.
  - Weakness: the self-beheadings come unmotivated, so a teller must supply an inner logic without changing the events.
  - Tone (horror turning to farce) is the main craft risk.
  - Overlaps: the woman-reproaches-goddess-then-revival pattern recurs in 016 and 021; the pool's only body-identity puzzle.
- **Load-bearing:** both self-beheadings at the temple; divine permission to rejoin the heads; her error made in haste; the head-as-identity principle.
- **Decorative:** the festival meeting, the invitation to her father's house, the rope.

### TC-007 Sattvaśīla and the Daitya princess (B-007; N1:180–193)
- **Engine:** a debt of gratitude that the master can repay only by repeating his servant's leap into the unknown.
- **Events:**
  1. Sattvaśīla, a poor southern prince working as a humble attendant (*kārpaṭika*), serves unrewarded (12.14.3–9).
  2. He gives the king, lost in the forest, two āmalaka fruits (10–31).
  3. At sea a golden banner rises, the ship sinks, and he leaps in (32–42).
  4. He finds undersea cities and a maiden, then surfaces in the palace tank (43–63).
  5. The king makes the same leap, coolly declines the Daitya princess, and she calls him "father" (64–102).
  6. The king gives her to Sattvaśīla: "one fruit repaid" (103–110).
- **Cast:** the king of Tāmraliptī (Caṇḍasena at 12.14.3, Caṇḍasiṃha afterwards: an inconsistency in the e-text, N1:525); Sattvaśīla, servant; the daughter of the Asura (demon) king Kālanemi; the goddess.
- **Traditions:**
  - All read versions agree on the answer (the servant) but **not on the reasoning**.
  - Ś 8: "the one who first does a kindness is greater".
  - Hindi VIII: serving another's interest beyond one's office is greater (N1:192; Rep:413).
  - J 7's answer is "per report; basis undocumented" (B-007:44).
- **Question and answer:**
  - Q: who showed more courage in leaping (12.14.110)?
  - A: Sattvaśīla (112).
  - Reasoning: he leapt without knowing and without expectation; the king knew (113a). 113b, that the king did not desire her, is ambiguous (N1:536).
- **Evidence:** moderate. Rep §8.6 calls the agreement a "checked non-difference", but it holds "for the **answer**, not for the **reasoning**" (Rep:413).
- **[Critic]:**
  - High wonder (the golden banner, the cities under the sea) and a warm, courteous resolution.
  - The first third is slow, and the dilemma is subtle: courage measured by knowledge.
  - Which tradition's reasoning is used changes what the tale means.
- **Load-bearing:** unrewarded service; the leap without knowledge; the king's leap with knowledge; the king's renunciation and gift of the bride; the fruit-debt as the measure (KSS).
- **Decorative:** the second city, the sword and the age-removing fruit, the details of the goddess's temple, the boar hunt.

### TC-008 The three fastidious brothers (B-008; N1:195–208)
- **Engine:** a triple test of sensitivity, each a small detective story with a hidden cause.
- **Events:**
  1. Three brahman brothers refuse to touch the turtle needed for their father's sacrifice and go to King Prasenajit (12.15.3–18).
  2. The rice smells of corpse-smoke: it was grown by a cremation ground (19–26).
  3. The courtesan smells of goat: she was reared on goat's milk (27–37).
  4. A hair under seven mattresses leaves a red mark on the third (37–45).
  5. They stay at court, "having earned the sin" of neglecting the sacrifice (46–47).
- **Cast:** the wealthy brahman sacrificer Viṣṇusvāmin; his three brahman sons; King Prasenajit; a courtesan.
- **Traditions:**
  - KSS 8, Ś 23, J 5, Hindi XXIII: position varies widely.
  - Ś and the Hindi give the same answer with no reason; the Hindi places the hair "in the seventh fold" (N1:207).
  - J 5 is one of the riddle solutions Emeneau calls careless (B-008:24).
- **Question and answer:**
  - Q: which is the most fastidious (12.15.49)?
  - A: the bed-connoisseur (50).
  - Reasoning: his mark was seen with one's own eyes; the other two could have learned their facts beforehand (51). This reasoning is KSS only.
- **Evidence:** moderate. Reading the final verse (47) as irony "is the compiler's" (N1:208).
- **[Critic]:**
  - Comic relief in a grave pool, with an elegant evidential answer.
  - Stakes are very low and the turtle errand is never closed, which could be used as a punchline.
  - Its rival TC-011 (CB group 6) is not in the slate, so the slot conflict is gone.
- **Load-bearing:** three domains of sensitivity; each verified by a hidden cause; the visible bodily mark versus knowledge that might be prepared.
- **Decorative:** the king's name, the number of mattresses (though "seven" is iconic), the courtesan's identity; the turtle frame is optional irony.

### TC-010 Madanasenā's rash promise (B-010; N1:225–238; Rep:339–356)
- **Engine:** a vow kept under pressure, and three successive releases on one night.
- **Events:**
  1. The betrothed Madanasenā is pursued by Dharmadatta (12.17.6–20).
  2. Fearing force, she swears to come to him after the wedding (21–26).
  3. She tells her husband on the wedding night; he releases her (27–41).
  4. A thief stops her and lets her go on her word (42–52).
  5. Dharmadatta refuses another man's wife (53–54).
  6. The thief lets her pass again; the husband receives her (55–61).
- **Cast:** a merchant's daughter, Madanasenā; the merchant Samudradatta (husband); Dharmadatta, her brother's friend (would-be lover); a thief (B-010:16).
- **Traditions:** this is the most divergent tale.
  - Ś 9: the question is who was *sattvādhika* (greatest in strength of character); answer the thief; the lover recites verses on women; the husband embraces her.
  - Hindi IX: answer the thief; the husband "evinced no affection".
  - J 10: adds a brahman-ogre; the husband rejects her ("I cannot now approve of you"); answer **the husband, Kandarpa**; the others' nobility is "conditioned" (Rep:349–352).
- **Question and answer:**
  - Q: which was truly generous (*kas tyāgī*) (12.17.62–63)?
  - A: the thief (64).
  - Reasoning: the husband could hardly keep a wife whose heart was elsewhere; the lover's ardour was "worn by time" (*kālajīrṇasaṃvegaḥ*) and he feared the husband would tell the king; the thief had no such constraint (65–67).
- **Evidence:** moderate; strong for KSS and J. The Hindi wording (*par us ke khāvind ne use pyār na kiyā*) "is **not** a specialist reading" (B-010:38). BKM was checked for names only.
- **[Critic]:**
  - The best suspense engine in the slate: a night road, out and back.
  - A woman's truthfulness drives it, and the answer inverts respectability. The variants are a gift: the same events support opposite verdicts.
  - Risk: the extracted oath and the husband's reception require a deliberate choice of version.
- **Load-bearing:** the oath extracted from fear of force; honesty with the husband; the three releases; the return past the thief; the question about generosity; the reasoning by constraints.
- **Decorative:** the names, the moonrise, the brother-friend link, the jewels' detail. The J ogre is an optional fourth.

### TC-013 Harisvāmin and the poisoned rice (B-013; N1:270–283)
- **Engine:** a quest that is abandoned, then a chain of innocent causes; the answer turns on the judge.
- **Events:**
  1. A Vidyādhara (a semi-divine sky being) abducts the brahman Harisvāmin's wife (12.20.3–9).
  2. He laments, gives everything away and wanders (10–28).
  3. A brahman's wife gives him milk-rice (29–40).
  4. A hawk carrying a snake drips poison into the bowl (41–46).
  5. He dies; the host expels his innocent wife (47–52).
  6. The court of Dharmarāja, god of death and justice, cannot decide (53).
- **Cast:** the brahman Harisvāmin; Lāvaṇyavatī (abducted, then dropped from the story); the brahman Padmanābha and his wife; the hawk and the snake.
- **Traditions:**
  - Ś 12: the snake lives in the banyan, no hawk; same answer.
  - Hindi XII: all are blameless, "whoever imputes guilt … is himself a sinner"; the wife gave "at the bidding of her husband" (N1:282).
  - J 12 has not been read.
- **Question and answer:**
  - Q: whose is the brahman-murder (12.20.53–54)?
  - A: not the snake, the hawk or the givers. It belongs to "the dull one who would say so without reflection" (*jaḍātmanaḥ … avicāryaiva yo brūyāt*) (56–59).
- **Evidence:** moderate. "The text does not say whether the hasty speaker is a member of the court" (N1:283). The abduction thread is unresolved (N1:501).
- **[Critic]:**
  - Compact and ironic; the only question in the slate with no culprit.
  - The self-referential answer catches out any reader who blamed someone.
  - Weakness: the dangling abduction and the abstract answer. The answer risks being a lecture unless the reader has already formed a judgement.
- **Load-bearing:** kindness given; poison through natural, mindless agents; the death; the wife blamed (blaming shown in action); even the gods failing to settle it; the turn onto the judge.
- **Decorative:** the abduction (it is only motivation for his wandering, and could be any bereavement), the hot-season imagery, the names.

### TC-015 The magic pill (Manaḥsvāmin as a woman) (B-015; N1:300–313; N3:92–112)
- **Engine:** a master-rogue's disguises produce two "marriages" to one princess; then a legal riddle.
- **Events:**
  1. The princess and the brahman youth fall in love; he rescues her from an elephant (12.22.3–19).
  2. Mūladeva's pills turn Mūladeva into an old brahman and the youth into a girl (20–26).
  3. The "girl" is lodged with the princess (27–33).
  4. A secret *gāndharva* union; a girl by day, a man by night (34–56).
  5. The minister's son is given the "girl"; the disguised youth seduces the son's bride Mṛgāṅkavatī and flees with her (57–99).
  6. Mūladeva returns with Śaśin and extracts the princess for Śaśin with full rites (100–105).
  7. The dispute (106–110).
- **Cast:** Princess Śaśiprabhā; the young brahman Manaḥsvāmin (a "bard's son" in J 13); the master-rogue Mūladeva and his friend Śaśin (Śaśideva in J); the king; the minister's son (Vijayasena in J) and his bride.
- **Traditions:**
  - Ś 14 and Hindi XIV add that she is pregnant by the lover; the child will perform Śaśin's funeral rites. Ś's reason is deceit (*chadmanā*); the Hindi's is "ten arbitrators" (N1:312).
  - J 13: a charm (*vidyā*), not a pill; same answer; the lover's act is "forbidden in this world and the next" (N3:96).
- **Question and answer:**
  - Q: whose wife is she properly (12.22.111)?
  - A: Śaśin's, given openly by her father; Manaḥsvāmin took her by stealth, and a thief has no right to another's property (113–114).
- **Evidence:** moderate to high for KSS and J. The lover's name in the Ś OCR is uncertain. "Śaśiprabhā's own wishes are not consulted" (N1:313).
- **[Critic]:**
  - The slate's best farce. Mūladeva is a star character, and the verdict deliberately goes against the lover the reader likes.
  - The plot is long and crowded; the Mṛgāṅkavatī subplot raises consent issues and is left unresolved.
- **Load-bearing:** her prior consent to the lover; the disguise that enables the secret union; Mūladeva's second trick, the public paternal gift; the dispute; the law-over-love answer.
- **Decorative:** the elephant rescue (any meeting would do), the embedded Ilā and Budha tale, the length of the minister's-son subplot (it must still end the disguise somehow).

### TC-016 Jīmūtavāhana (B-016; N1:315–328; Rep:369–378)
- **Engine:** a saint's habitual self-giving set against a minor figure's costly return.
- **Events:**
  1. The Bodhisattva-portioned Vidyādhara prince empties the family wishing-tree for the poor (12.23.3–29).
  2. He refuses war with his kin and goes into exile (30–38).
  3. He marries Malayavatī (39–93).
  4. The beach of bones: Garuḍa eats one Nāga (serpent being) a day by a pact with Vāsuki (94–112).
  5. Śaṅkhacūḍa and his mother lament; Śaṅkhacūḍa refuses the offered substitute and goes to worship (113–145).
  6. Jīmūtavāhana mounts the rock; Garuḍa eats him; his crest-jewel falls before his wife (146–158).
  7. Śaṅkhacūḍa runs back; Garuḍa is struck with remorse; the prince dies (159–180).
  8. Gaurī revives him and makes him emperor (181–201).
- **Cast:** the Vidyādhara prince Jīmūtavāhana; the Nāga youth Śaṅkhacūḍa and his mother; Garuḍa; Malayavatī and Mitrāvasu; his parents; the goddess Gaurī.
- **Traditions:**
  - Same answer in Ś 15 and Hindi XV; the reasoning shifts. KSS: "perfected over many births". Ś: practice (*abhyāsa*) across births, with Betal objecting. Hindi: practice grounded in Kshatriya caste (Rep:375–376).
  - J 24 has not been read; only its weeping-serpent-mother opening was seen (N3:218).
- **Question and answer:**
  - Q: which was greater in *sattva*, courage (12.23.203)?
  - A: Śaṅkhacūḍa (205–206).
  - Reasoning: Jīmūtavāhana's deed is no wonder, being *bahujanmasiddham* ("perfected over many births"); Śaṅkhacūḍa had escaped yet ran back and insisted.
- **Evidence:** moderate. The Hindi is "an AI reading of Barker p. 270, **not specialist**; N5 recommends a specialist" (B-016:28). N5 is the planned specialist Hindi/Braj reading.
- **[Critic]:**
  - The widest emotional range in the slate, and the answer favours the secondary character, which is excellent.
  - Weakness: at 207 verses it is the longest tale, the romance competes with the sacrifice, and Śaṅkhacūḍa is a minor figure until the end. He must be built earlier.
- **Load-bearing:** the prince's goodness shown as lifelong habit (the answer depends on it); the daily tribute; Śaṅkhacūḍa's refusal, then his return and insistence; Garuḍa's remorse (it reframes the act).
- **Decorative:** the wishing-tree episode (can be compressed), the kin war, the romance and wedding, the Kadrū–Vinatā backstory. The revival sets the tone but is not needed for the question.

### TC-018 Candrasvāmin and the lost science (B-018; N1:345–358)
- **Engine:** a rule-bound ordeal inside a whole illusory lifetime, failed by one moment of wavering.
- **Events:**
  - Frame episode: many identical corpses at the tree (12.25.3–7).
  1. A gambling brahman's son is beaten and left for dead (13–30).
  2. An ascetic conjures a golden city every night (31–47).
  3. The rules: recite under water, live an illusory life, and at twenty-four, when roused, remember and enter the fire; an unworthy pupil costs the teacher his science (48–58).
  4. The illusory life; he wavers before his weeping family, then enters; the fire is cold (59–76).
  5. Both lose the science (77–81).
- **Cast:** Candrasvāmin, a learned brahman's son; an ascetic teacher (*mahāvratī*, a sorcerer-ascetic); an illusory wife and children; gamblers.
- **Traditions:**
  - Ś 17: a *yakṣiṇī* (the science's presiding spirit) does not come; the pupil was "double-minded" (*dvidhācitta*); a closing verse ties the outcome to karma.
  - Hindi XVII: the same, plus fate. Fate is "not a Hindi-only addition" (N1:357).
  - J 15 has not been read; Emeneau calls it careless (B-018:8). The Nepali J manuscript W omits it.
- **Question and answer:**
  - Q: why was the science lost to both, although the rite was done as instructed (12.25.83)?
  - A: success needs a steady mind free of wavering (*vikalpa*); the pupil wavered; the teacher gave the science to an unworthy pupil (84–86).

- **[Critic]:**
  - The most imaginative device in the slate: a lifetime compressed into an instant, with a superb scene before the pyre.
  - Risks: nested reality is hard to tell, and the answer tends toward a maxim.
- **Load-bearing:** the rules stated in advance; the illusory life with family; the awakening and the wavering; the cold fire; the double loss.
- **Decorative:** the gambling backstory (motivation only), the golden city's luxuries. The many-corpses episode is frame material, not part of the tale.

### TC-019 The thief's son and the three hands (B-019; N1:360–373)
- **Engine:** a two-generation chain of bargains converging on one image.
- **Events:**

  - J 16 is careless per Emeneau and has not been read; Ryder omits the tale (B-019:23).

  - Q: into whose hand should the offering go (12.26.94)?

- **[Critic]:**
  - A stunning closing image, and the dilemma is genuinely three-sided.
  - But the logic rests on legal doctrine and paid sex; the middle is a travelogue; the tale is reflective rather than suspenseful.
  - Answer-pattern overlap with 010: both answers are "the thief".
- **Load-bearing:** the water-gift marriage and the thief's authorisation; payment to the begetter; the gold left as payment for rearing; the three hands; the reasoning that the other two claims were bought.

### TC-020 The boy who laughed (B-020; N1:375–388)
- **Engine:** a rash bargain with a demon builds to a frozen tableau; the riddle supplies the meaning.
- **Events:**
  1. King Candrāvaloka marries Indīvaraprabhā, daughter of the sage Kaṇva and the celestial nymph Menakā (12.27.4–50).
  2. The brahman-demon (*brahmarākṣasa*) Jvālāmukha demands a willing seven-year-old brahman boy, held down by his parents and killed by the king's own hand (51–82).
  3. A golden-statue proclamation; a boy volunteers and argues his parents round; they take the reward (83–110).
  4. As the sword is raised, he laughs; everyone freezes (111–125). **The outcome is not narrated in KSS.**
- **Cast:** a king; his minister Sumati; a brahman-demon; a seven-year-old brahman boy and his parents, who are rewarded.
- **Traditions:**
  - Ś 19: the parents refuse the eldest and the youngest; the middle son offers himself; verses on protectors turned killers.
  - Hindi XIX: the second son; he "first laughed, and then wept"; **the king beheads him**; a verse that woman is the source of misery (N1:387).
  - J 17 has not been read.
- **Question and answer:**
  - Q: why did the boy laugh at the point of death (12.27.127–128)?
  - A: a threatened creature cries to its parents, then the king, then the deity; here all three were reversed. Seeing such delusion about the body, and with his wish fulfilled, he laughed in wonder and joy (130–136).

- **[Critic]:**
  - The strongest single image in the slate; the question reads an image, not a rule.
  - The romance prelude is long and tonally mismatched; the ending choice (open or killed) is the key decision.
  - It is now the slate's only "why did X react so?" riddle, because 012, 014 and 023 are excluded.
- **Load-bearing:** the trespass and the demon's precise conditions (they create the triple betrayal the answer reads); the parents' consent for reward; the volunteering; the laugh at the sword-moment.
- **Decorative:** the Kaṇva and Menakā prelude, the hunting rebuke, the rain of flowers. The golden statue can be replaced by any proclamation.

### TC-021 Anaṅgamañjarī, Maṇivarman and Kamalākara (B-021; N1:390–403; Rep:380–392)
- **Engine:** three deaths from emotion in one night, then a divine reset.
- **Events:**
  1. The merchant's daughter loathes her loving husband (12.28.3–10).
  2. Love at a glance with the chaplain's son; a noose, cut by her friend (11–34).
  3. At the tryst she dies of joy; he dies of grief (35–78).
  4. Her husband returns and dies of love, not anger (79–84).
  5. Caṇḍī revives all three "with passion stilled" (*śāntānaṅgāḥ*) (85–91).
- **Cast:** the merchant Arthadatta; his daughter Anaṅgamañjarī; Maṇivarman, a merchant's son from Tāmraliptī (husband); Kamalākara, the king's chaplain's son; her friend and confidante Mālatikā; the goddess Caṇḍī.
- **Traditions:**
  - Same answer everywhere; the question's framing shifts. KSS "deluded by passion"; Ś 20 *kāmāndha*, "blind with desire"; Hindi XX *adhik kāmī*, "most full of desire", which the English translators render as love.
  - Whether Ś and the Hindi narrate the revival has not been checked (N1:402).
- **Question and answer:**
  - Q: whose delusion was greater (*adhiko moho 'nurāgamūḍheṣu*) (12.28.93)?
  - A: Maṇivarman (94). The lovers' passion was mutual and ripened; he had cause for anger yet died of love: "excessively deluded" (*atimūḍha*) (95–96).
- **Evidence:** moderate. The connotation of *kāmī* is "a question for a specialist" (Rep:390). The narrator's aside against women is at 12.28.81 (N1:401).
- **[Critic]:**
  - Vivid and quick, and the answer moves the reader's pity onto the unloved husband.
  - Risk of melodrama; the revival undercuts the tragedy. The question's wording steers the reading, which gives an editor useful latitude.
- **Load-bearing:** her aversion to a husband who loves her; the mutual glance; death of joy, then death of grief; the husband's choice of grief over anger.
- **Decorative:** the noose, Mālatikā's arrangements, the moon imagery. The revival is a choice of tone.

### TC-022 Four brothers revive a lion (B-022; N1:405–418)
- **Engine:** dispossession drives a quest for skill; four partial skills combine and the result is ruin.
- **Events:**
  1. Orphaned brahman brothers are dispossessed (12.29.3–7).
  2. Their maternal uncles treat them with contempt (8–11).
  3. The eldest's failed hanging; they resolve to learn skills (12–28).
  4. They learn four skills: flesh, skin and hair, limbs, life (29–35).
  5. They revive an unknown bone; the lion kills all four (36–41).
  6. The narrator on fate (42–44).

  - In the Hindi the bones come "from a Kanjar" (a caste-marked label).
  - The J tiger's basis is undocumented (B-022:23).
- **Question and answer:**
  - Q: which was guilty (*aparādhyati*) (12.29.45–46)?
  - Vikrama's aside: he knows answering will free the Vetāla (47).

- **Evidence:** moderate to high for KSS.
- **[Critic]:**
  - Compact, with a fable-like inevitability and the line "everyone helped, but one knew".
  - Weakness: half the tale is misfortune before the hook.
- **Load-bearing:** four skills in sequence; nobody asks what the bone is; the last one sees the shape and proceeds; the lion kills its makers; the question of guilt or folly.
- **Decorative:** the uncles, the eldest's suicide attempt (motivation only), the narrator's fate homily. Vikrama's aside is a gift for the frame.

### TC-024 The footprint marriages: the unanswered riddle (B-024; N1:435–448)
- **Engine:** a promise made over footprints produces cross-generational marriages and a kinship knot.
- **Events:**
  1. The deposed King Dharma flees; Bhils (*śabara*, a forest people) kill him (12.31.5–20).
  2. His widow and daughter wait by a pond (20–23).
  3. A local chief and his son see two lines of footprints: the son claims the small feet, the father the large; the son binds him with a verse of Mūladeva's and an oath (24–34).
  4. The queen accepts: "what can a helpless woman do…" (35–50).
  5. The mother has the smaller feet, so the son marries the mother and the father the daughter; both couples have children (51–56).
- **Cast:** King Dharma, a southern feudal king; Queen Candravatī of Mālava; Princess Lāvaṇyavatī; the local chief Caṇḍasiṃha and his son Siṃhaparākrama; the Bhil robbers.

  - Ś 24: "gave no reply"; Ś 25's opening may give a reason (provisional).
  - Hindi XXV: "held his tongue through ignorance"; the warning comes inside the same tale; the ascetic is named Shāntshīl.
  - J 25 "omits the key marriage detail" (Rep:206, 325).

  - Q: what are the children to each other (12.31.58–59)?
  - A: none. He "did not know" (*nājñāsīt*) (60). Betal laughs inwardly (61–62) and then warns the king (63–74).

- **[Critic]:**
  - A brilliant hinge: the only time the hero fails, and the failure saves him.
  - As a standalone tale it is thin. Its value is structural: it opens the frame's climax.
  - J cannot supply it.
- **Load-bearing:** the footprints and the oath; the inverted foot sizes; the two marriages and their offspring; the king's silence.
- **Decorative:** the deposition, the extent of the Bhil fight, the pond.

### TC-025 Four brothers and the courtesan (J 21) (B-025; N3:54–70)
- **Engine:** a contest in order of age won by inquiry, with a melancholy rebirth romance turned into the instrument of a trick.
- **Events:**
  1. A caravan merchant's four sons, each master of one art; three common jewels are left to the king's choice (Tr pp. 109–111).
  2. The test: two nights with Rūpavatī, who never takes the same man twice (111–113).

### TC-026 Mūladeva wins a bride for Śaśideva (J 22) (B-026; N3:74–112)
- **Engine:** a chain of deceptions, each enabling the next.
- **Events:**
  1. A merchant's wife is guarded through a twelve-year absence (Tr p. 125).
  2. Śaśideva falls for her; Mūladeva proposes the bawd Kāmikalpalatā (127).
  3. The bawd poses as an all-knowing ascetic, tells the parents the husband has remarried abroad, and prescribes a "charm" bower (127–129).

  5. Mūladeva, in Śaśideva's guise, makes a staged claim at the king's gate; a corpse is substituted and the house burned (129–133).
  6. A "lookalike" wife is presented to the king; the parents weep (133).

- **Load-bearing:** the lonely guarded wife; the false oracle and charm; the mistaken-identity claim planted early; the corpse and the fire; the lookalike presentation; the two-trickster question.
- **Decorative:** the hundred servants, the bones sent to pilgrimage places, the Arundhatī line, the festival.

### TC-027 The ogre and Arimaulimaṇi's kingdom (J 23) (B-027; N3:116–132)
- **Engine:** a cascade of causes: insult, revenge, tribute, rescue, ingratitude, release.
- **Events:**

- **Load-bearing:** the repeated insult; the ogre's revenge; the tribute pact; the weeping mother; the rescue; the ingratitude; the release; the question naming three candidates.
- **Decorative:** the girl in the belly (any bride would do), the ogress's bee and lamp episode, the restoration coda. Vidyādhara is named in the question but ignored by the answer.

- J: Vikramakesarin, also called Vikramāditya.
- Hindi: Vikram, younger brother of Bharthari, of a Dhārānagar royal family.
- BKM: the manuscripts say Vikramasena, the edition Trivikrama° (Rep:154, 257).

**Ascetic.**
- KSS: the monk (*bhikṣu*) Kṣāntiśīla.
- Ś: the yogī Kṣāntiśīla (the name varies by manuscript), called a *digambara* (a naked Jain-type ascetic) in the printed ending.
- J: the *kāpālika* Kṣāntiśīla.
- Hindi: a potter-devotee, named Shāntshīl in XXV (N1:447); he "has killed the oilman and made him the demon" (Rep:221; Cat:701).

**Gift motif.**
- KSS: a fruit daily for **ten** years (*daśasu*, 12.8.25); a monkey splits one and a gem falls out (12.8.23–27). Per Ryder, who follows KSS 12.8.21–32, the treasurer had stored the rest (Rev:117).
- Ryder says **twelve** years, and his base edition is unknown (Rep:246).
- Ś: the period varies, "twelve months or years in some manuscripts".
- J: a **bilva** fruit via a servant, for twelve years.
- Hindi: a long prehistory instead: the fruit of immortality and three men born at one moment (a king, an oilman's son, a potter-devotee) (Rep:258).
- Ś MS *a* prefixes a similar prehistory: Gandharvasena, a boy born three times, Vikramāditya's accession (Rep:186).

**Why the king carries the corpse.**
- KSS: he honours a request for help after receiving the gifts (12.8.28–49).
- Ś and J: "similar in outline (PROVISIONAL)".
- Hindi: the devotee requests it, and the Vetāla warns Vikram early, in the prehistory (Rep:259).

**Corpse and storytelling.**
- KSS: on a *śiṃśapā* tree in a cremation ground; it cries out, then laughs (12.8.50–55); stories are told "to beguile the road" (12.8.58).
- Ś: *śiṃśapā* tree.
- J: not checked.
- Hindi: a tree in a burning-ground (Rep:260–261).
- Newari J 21: the Vetāla goes "to his place", not back to the tree (N3:164).
- KSS 18 adds a unique frame episode of many identical corpses (12.25.3–7; N1:506).

**Silence and answer rule.**
- KSS: if Vikrama knows the answer and stays silent, his head bursts into a hundred pieces (12.8.188). When he speaks, the Vetāla returns to the tree.
- Hindi I: he "will be cast into hell" (Rep:262).
- Ś: the same return pattern. J: penalty not checked (Rep:681 lists the J and Ś penalty and cemetery details as unverified).
- The KSS penalty "applies only to one who knows and stays silent (59)" (N1:448).
- Frame texture inside tales:
  - The Vetāla contests Vikrama's answer in KSS 1 (12.8.191), 4 and 17; Ś and the Hindi add challenges elsewhere (N1:480).
  - In TC-022, Vikrama answers knowing it releases the Vetāla (12.29.47).
  - J's departure formulas say the Vetāla "deceived the king" into speaking (N3:83, 125).

**Climax.**
- KSS 24 / Ś 24 / J 25 / Hindi XXV: the father-daughter and son-mother riddle.
- KSS: "did not know" (*nājñāsīt*, 12.31.60). Betal resolves to outwit the monk (61–64) and warns that the monk will ask for an eight-limbed prostration in order to sacrifice him; Vikrama should ask to be shown how, then behead him. The warning already promises Vidyādhara power (12.31.65–73; at 72 per Rev:307). Betal leaves the corpse (74).
- Ś 25's opening may say the king fell silent because he had been deceived many times (OCR, PROVISIONAL; N1:447).
- Hindi: silence "through ignorance"; the warning is given inside XXV.

**Ascetic's true plan.**
- KSS: to sacrifice the king and gain Vidyādhara sovereignty (12.31.65–74).
- J (Emeneau's printed text): the Vetāla was an oil-miller (*tailika*); a wise man taught him and named Kṣāntiśīla, his disciple, "your brother"; Kṣāntiśīla plans to offer the king to **Devī** for magic power (Rep:263; Rev:203–206).
- **Contradiction:** Uhle 1881 calls the Vetāla an "oil-miller's *son*" and Kṣāntiśīla "the *king's* brother". This is unresolved; Vidyasagara 1873 would settle it (Rev:303). Emeneau argues the backstory is a Bengali interpolation, absent from W and the Newari (Rep:205).

**Ending.**
- KSS: Vikrama beheads the monk (12.32.1–19). Betal promises Vidyādhara overlordship (21) and offers a boon; Vikrama asks that the stories be famous (22–30). Śiva then appears, says the king was first created as Vikramāditya, gives him the sword Aparājita, and promises Vidyādhara sovereignty (31–38).
- Ś (printed text): blood offered to the Vetāla; the eight great *siddhis* (supernatural powers); the Gandharvas (celestial musicians) shower flowers and offer a boon, and the king asks that the collection be famous and that the Vetāla serve him; Brahmā, Viṣṇu and Śiva appear; Śiva names him Vikramāditya and grants Vidyādhara fortune.
  - **Mixed witness:** Uhle attributes vv. 2–4 to Kṣemendra.
  - Variants: Indra and the gods instead of the Gandharvas; Indra gives a sword; the Vetāla becomes an indestructible golden man (Rep:187, 263).
- J: the king beheads Kṣāntiśīla and offers him to Devī, who grants a hundred years of life, universal rule, and the magic power of Tāla and Vetāla.
- Hindi: Indra and the gods; the boon "this story concerning me become famous"; the two corpses go into a cauldron of oil; two *bīr* (heroes) appear and promise to come when called (Rep:263; Rev:211–213).

**Count.**
- KSS and Ś: 24 tales plus the conclusion counted as the 25th (12.32.24–27).
- J: 25 narrated tales plus the frame; Emeneau infers an original 27.
- Hindi: 25, with the conclusion inside XXV.
- Newari: the Vetāla is fetched 25 times and the conclusion is not counted as a tale (N3:144; Rep:274–279).
- The frame is excluded from the 25 (D-035; Cat:696).

**Internal tension to flag.** Cat:712 contrasts "the Hindi Vetāla's warning in the prehistory" with KSS's warning after TC-024. N1:447 also records a Hindi warning inside XXV. Whether the Hindi warns twice is not reconciled in the files.

**Not researched:** BKM's frame; J's frame beyond the opening and story 25; the penalty and cemetery details in Ś and J; the Newari, Tibetan and Bhaviṣya Purāṇa frames; the 1805 Hindi print and the Braj; why the king carries the corpse in Ś and J (provisional); the KSS embedding (Vikramakeśarin learning of the Vetāla from an old brahman) known only via Uhle (Rep:165); the source of Uhle's J summary (Cat:714; Rep:680–681).
