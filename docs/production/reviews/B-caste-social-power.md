# Caste, class and social-power review of the 19 Betal Pachisi candidates and the frame

## Summary
- **What the record contains.** I searched all six research docs and the 19 dossiers. None of them uses *caṇḍāla*, "outcaste", "untouchable" or an explicit purity-and-pollution rule. Śūdra, Vaiśya and an explicit varṇa-based verdict appear only in TC-009, which is not among the 19 (N1:217-218). This does not prove the 19 are free of such material. The documents summarise the KSS (Kathāsaritsāgara) only, and most of the Ś (Śivadāsa) and Hindi narratives are unread.
- **Which verdicts depend on birth.** Among the 19, Vikram's reasoning rests on birth or caste in two places:
  - Hindi XV (TC-016) grounds Jīmūtavāhana's courage in his being Kshatriya.
  - J 21 (TC-025) explains Rūpavatī's failure by her being "born into a courtesan's family" and becoming "very wicked".
- **Where the deeper problem lies.** It is less in caste labels than in how power is shown:
  - **Rulers paying with their subjects' lives:** TC-016 (Vāsuki's pact), TC-020 (a king buys his life with a child) and TC-027 (a daily human tribute chosen by lot).
  - **"Service" excusing harm:** TC-001.
  - **The frame:** it codes its villain and its monster by caste or sect, and rewards the king's own sacrificial killing.

## Per-tale review

### TC-001: Padmāvatī, the prince and the minister's son

**1. Markers in the sources**
- Prince Vajramukuṭa; Buddhiśarīra, the minister's son.
- Padmāvatī is "daughter of the ivory-carver (*dantaghāṭaka*) Saṅgrāmavardhana" (N1:95).
- A nurse bribed as go-between; a city magistrate; a false "ascetic"; *yoginīs* and a *ḍākinī* (a flesh-eating witch) (N1:95).
- The parents die of grief in a single verse (185). The mother "follows him in death" (N1:95).
- Hindi I adds the kotwal (city police chief), who "obeyed orders", and calls her "the princess" (N1:102). That is Platts's wording; the Hindi body is unread, so whether the Hindi raises her status is unknown.

**2. Does the verdict depend on birth?** Not on caste. It does depend on rank and service. The minister's son is sinless because "it was his master's business" (12.8.192). The lovers are "not to be judged" (193). The king is guilty for not using spies (194-195) (N1:97).

**3. Caste and class risk: low to moderate.** It is a class asymmetry: royal schemers use an artisan family and discard it, and the parents' deaths get one verse (B-001:55).

**4. Violence and power risk: high.**
- A woman is drugged and branded, and a false witchcraft charge gets her banished.
- The verdict excuses the minister's son because he was serving his master.
- Its strength is that the ruler is held to account.

**5. Roles**

| Character | Original role | Code | Proposed | Why | Effect |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Vajramukuṭa | Prince | E | Keep | The gap in power over an artisan family is the point | None |
| Buddhiśarīra | Minister's son, schemer | E | Keep the office; don't present it as hereditary or natural | His "service" defence is the crux of the verdict | Sharper |
| Padmāvatī | Ivory-carver's daughter | H | Keep her artisan origin | Shows who bears the cost | Class becomes visible |
| Her parents | Victims | E | Keep; give them presence earlier | The question is about their deaths | Adds weight |
| Nurse | Bribed go-between | I | Compress | — | — |
| King Karṇotpala | Ruler and judge | E | Keep | Ruler accountability | — |
| Magistrate / kotwal | Official | H | Keep the kotwal (Hindi) | Lets "obeyed orders" be weighed | Adds a party |
| *Yoginī* tale | A lie | E (as device) | Keep, but always clearly as a lie | Witch-branding must not read as credible or clever | — |

**6. Proposed adaptation**
- Keep "the king" as the answer and keep the Betal's crow-and-geese objection (191).
- Change the reasoning: the deceivers own the lie, but the banishment and the deaths belong to the one who had both the power and the duty to check.
- Remove "no sin because he served his master".
- Show the branding as cruelty, not as cleverness.

**7. Does the dilemma survive?** Yes, with a change to the reasoning.

**8. Recommendation: ADAPT** (moderate-high confidence). I disagree with B-001:59 ("cuts against the easy target"): the answer also clears the schemer on grounds of service. The dossier's §7 (B-001:72-73) misses both the class dimension and the service defence.

### TC-003: The parrot and the mynah (are men or women worse?)

**1. Markers in the sources**
- Merchant families in both embedded tales.
- The thief-witness is made chief of police.
- The king cuts off the wife's ears and banishes her.
- The birds' wager is "slavery or wifehood" (*dāsatva-bhāryātva*, 13) (N1:125, 133).

**2. Does the verdict depend on birth?** It rests on an essentialist claim about a group: women are bad "generally, everywhere and always" (*prāyaḥ sarvatra sadā*, 12.10.94). The Hindi adds that a woman "does not give a thought to right and wrong" (N1:132). This is not caste, but it has exactly the logical form the charter forbids for caste: moral worth fixed by birth into a group.

**3. Risk**
- Caste: none.
- Inherent group inferiority: high.

**4. Violence and power risk: moderate-high.** Mutilation by royal order, a near-execution on a father's accusation, and a wager of slavery.

**5. Roles**

| Character | Code | Proposed | Effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| Birds | E | Keep | — |
| Prince (judge) | H | Keep | — |
| Dhanadatta / Ratnāvalī; Vasudattā / husband | E | Keep as individuals | — |
| Thief-witness who becomes police chief | E | Keep | An outsider tells the truth |
| King who mutilates | H | Keep, but not as just deserts | — |

**6. Proposed adaptation**
- Vikram refuses the generalisation: each tale proves only its own culprit. This matches TC-013's turn of blaming the careless judge.
- This departs from KSS, Ś and the Hindi, which all answer "women", so it must be logged under D-034.

**7. Does the dilemma survive?** Yes, but only with a changed answer.

**8. Recommendation: MAJOR REWRITE** (moderate confidence). If the source answer must stay, **REJECT**. I disagree with B-003:70, which offers keeping the verdict "as a character's judgement". With Vikram as the collection's arbiter, that normalises the claim. The same option would never be offered for a caste-superiority verdict.

### TC-006: The exchanged heads

**1. Markers in the sources**
- Dhavala, "a young washerman"; Madanasundarī, "daughter of the washerman Śuddhapaṭa" (*rajaka*, 12.13.7-8) (N1:170, 178).
- Both families share the occupation (marriage within the community).
- Dhavala's reasoning before beheading himself: "people worship the goddess with offerings of living creatures, so why not offer himself" (N1:170).

**2. Does the verdict depend on birth?** Not on caste. KSS: identity is recognised in the head (*pratyabhijñā*, "recognition"). Ś: "among all the limbs the head is chief". Hindi: the principle is cited from "the book of law" (N1:177).
- My own inference (not in the docs): "the head is chief of the limbs", backed by a law-book, echoes the varṇa body hierarchy of the Puruṣasūkta.

**3. Caste and class risk: low to moderate.** This is the only named occupational caste among the protagonists of the 19. The risk is linking a stigmatised working caste with "fated" self-offering in blood (12.13.28, 33) and treating endogamy as natural.

**4. Violence and power risk: moderate** (self-beheading, an attempted hanging). None from power.

**5. Roles**

| Character | Code | Proposed | Why | Effect |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Madanasundarī | E | Keep as the agent | — | — |
| Dhavala, the brother | E | Give Dhavala a human motive (a vow, a desperate prayer) | Avoids "the lowly as irrational devotees" | Clearer |
| Washer occupation | H | Keep as work (cloth, river, festival), with no caste name and no "born to it" | Keeps working people at the centre with dignity | Texture without destiny |
| Fathers | I | — | — | — |
| Goddess | E | Keep | — | — |

**6. Proposed adaptation.** As above. For the verdict, use the KSS reasoning ("recognition lives in the face") rather than "the head rules the limbs" or the law-book.

**7. Does the dilemma survive?** Yes.

**8. Recommendation: ADAPT** (moderate-high confidence). See (C): dropping the occupation entirely risks erasure.

### TC-007: Sattvaśīla and the Daitya princess

**1. Markers in the sources**
- The king of Tāmraliptī.
- Sattvaśīla is "a poor southern prince working as a humble attendant (*kārpaṭika*)" (N1:185).
- Brahmans aboard the ship cry out.
- The Daitya princess, daughter of the Asura king Kālanemi (slain by Viṣṇu), "places herself and her cities in his power" and calls the king "father" (102). She is given to the servant (N1:185).

**2. Does the verdict depend on birth?** No. The servant is braver because he leapt without knowledge or expectation (113a). Ś: whoever is first to do a kindness is greater. Hindi: there is "no special merit in conferring favours" where that is one's office (N1:192).

**3. Caste and class risk: low to moderate.**
- The "servant" is noble by birth, and his reward (ruling cities) restores his rank. Service is honoured only because the servant is secretly royal.
- (Outside the docs) Some Adivasi and anti-caste traditions claim Asura figures as ancestors, so a slain Asura's daughter surrendering her realm needs care.

**4. Violence and power risk (servitude): moderate.** Ten unrewarded years living on fruit (7-31). The woman is transferred between men.

**5. Roles**

| Character | Code | Proposed | Effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| King | E | Keep; foreground his shame at his neglect | Ruler accountability |
| Sattvaśīla | E (servant); his royal birth is I | A genuine commoner attendant | "Servant braver than king" gains force; the reward becomes mobility, not restoration |
| Daitya princess | E; her Asura identity is H | A sovereign who chooses (12.14.104 already says "she agrees") | Removes the conquest-fantasy reading |
| Brahmans aboard | I | "Passengers" | — |

**6. Proposed adaptation.** As in the table. The Hindi reasoning (favours are a ruler's office, not a merit) supports accountability and can be used.

**7. Does the dilemma survive?** Yes.

**8. Recommendation: ADAPT** (moderate confidence). The dossier (B-007:69-70) does not analyse the servant's noble birth.

### TC-008: The three fastidious brothers

**1. Markers in the sources**
- "A wealthy brahman sacrificer", his three brahman sons, and a brahman settlement.
- The sacrifice needs a turtle (N1:200).
- The rice "smells of corpse-smoke" because it grew beside a cremation ground.
- The courtesan "smells of goat" because she was reared on goat's milk.
- The brothers "earned the sin of obstructing their father's sacrifice" (47).

**2. Does the verdict depend on birth?** No. It rests on evidence seen directly on the body (*pratyakṣa*, 51; N1:202).

**3. Caste and purity risk: moderate.**
- The whole comedy is a contest of purity-sensitivity among sons of a ritual elite.
- The food test is pollution by proximity to the dead.
- The courtesan test turns a low-status woman's body into the polluting object, which is the smell-and-proximity logic of untouchability in miniature (my inference).
- B-008:69-71 calls the courtesan test only "demeaning" and misses this structure.

**4. Violence and power risk: low to moderate.** A courtesan is sent by the king as a test subject.

**5. Roles**

| Character | Code | Proposed | Effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| Brothers | E; brahman label H | Keep only as satire of purity pride | The satire stays visible |
| Turtle errand and father | E (the irony) | Keep | — |
| King | E | Keep | — |
| Courtesan test | E as one of three tests; her body as the polluting object is I | An object test (perfume, garland), or let her turn the joke on him | Removes the class and gender disgust |

**6. Proposed adaptation.** Tell it as satire. The narrator's "sin" line closes it ironically. The corpse-smoke test may stay, but must not invite aversion to people who work at cremation grounds.

**7. Does the dilemma survive?** Yes.

**8. Recommendation: ADAPT** (moderate confidence).

### TC-015: The magic pill

**1. Markers in the sources**
- Manaḥsvāmin is "a young brahman" in KSS but "a bard's son" in J 13 (N3:96). The label varies across versions.
- Mūladeva disguises himself as an "old brahman".
- The king fears "a brahman's curse" (30, 102).
- In J 13 Mūladeva threatens to die "so that the guilt falls on the king" (N3:96).
- The council gives the "ward" to the minister's son to save the minister "and so the kingdom" (N1:305).

**2. Does the verdict depend on birth?** No. The father's public gift outranks the secret union, and "a thief can never have rightful ownership" (114). In Ś and the Hindi, the child will perform Śaśin's funeral rites. The verdict treats the woman as property. The plot engine is a ruler's deference to brahman impunity.

**3. Caste and class risk: moderate.** Told straight, it normalises caste privilege as beyond refusal. Told as tricksters exploiting the king's deference, it critiques it.

**4. Violence and power risk: moderate.** The state hands over a woman for reasons of state. Mṛgāṅkavatī is seduced under a disguise. The princess is never asked.

**5. Roles**

| Character | Code | Proposed | Effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| Princess, Śaśin, minister's son, Mṛgāṅkavatī | E | Keep | — |
| Manaḥsvāmin | E; brahman label I (J proves it) | Drop the label | None |
| Mūladeva's "old brahman" disguise | H | "A revered holy man whose curse kings fear" | Keeps the satire of sacred authority |
| King and council | E | Keep | The state's use of a woman becomes visible |

**6. Proposed adaptation.** Make the king's fear the comic weakness. Base the verdict on a witnessed public commitment, not on "theft of property". Let the Betal object that the princess was never asked.

**7. Does the dilemma survive?** Yes, with a change to the reasoning.

**8. Recommendation: ADAPT** (moderate confidence).

### TC-016: Jīmūtavāhana and Śaṅkhacūḍa

**1. Markers in the sources**
- The Vidyādhara prince is "born of a portion of a Bodhisattva" and remembers former births. He refuses to kill kin for a kingdom.
- The serpent king Vāsuki's pact gives Garuḍa one Nāga a day. The feud goes back to Kadrū's enslavement of Vinatā.
- Śaṅkhacūḍa refuses to "stain his family's name" (N1:320).

**2. Does the verdict depend on birth?**
- KSS: the act was "perfected over many births" (*bahujanmasiddham*, 205).
- Ś: practice "birth after birth".
- Hindi XV: "*jīmūtbāhan jāt kā chhatrī hai, use jī dene kā abhyās ho rahā hai*" ("a Kshatriya by caste; for him the practice of giving his life is ongoing"). Platts: "a Kshetri by caste … accustomed to holding his life in his hand" (CMP:375; REV:170).
- So the reasoning depends on caste in the Hindi only.

**3. Caste and class risk**
- Hindi XV: high. It is the only verdict among the 19 that rests on varṇa.
- KSS / Ś: low.
- I disagree with CMP:376 and B-016:57 ("not a simple shift from virtue to caste"). Grounding courage in a birth-group is exactly the claim the charter forbids.

**4. Violence and power risk: moderate-high.**
- A ruler, Vāsuki, pays Garuḍa with his own subjects. Jīmūtavāhana does blame Vāsuki and Garuḍa (106-112).
- A man is eaten alive; there is an enslavement backstory.
- The self-sacrifice is that of a free adult acting against an unjust pact, so it fits the charter.

**5. Roles**

| Character | Code | Proposed | Effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| Jīmūtavāhana | E (keep the prince: his refusal of war is political) | Keep | — |
| "Bodhisattva portion" | H | Keep, lightly | — |
| Śaṅkhacūḍa and his mother | E (ordinary subjects) | Keep | — |
| Vāsuki | H → make visible | Keep the ruler who agreed the pact | Accountability |
| Garuḍa, Gaurī | E | Keep | — |
| Malayavatī, Mitrāvasu | H | Compress | — |

**6. Proposed adaptation**
- Use the KSS/Ś reasoning; refuse Hindi XV.
- Render "many births" as a long practice he chose, not as his nature.
- Keep the anti-hierarchical heart: the ordinary victim's chosen courage outranks the prince's habitual virtue.

**7. Does the dilemma survive?** Yes.

**8. Recommendation: ADAPT** (high confidence). Reject the Hindi XV reasoning.

### TC-019: The three hands at Gayā

**1. Markers in the sources**
- Kinsmen seize the merchant's wealth "with royal backing" (N1:365).
- The thief is impaled. The mother pours water to give the bride away for 1,000 gold pieces.
- *Kṣetraja* ("born of his field": a son born to a man's wife by another man with his authority).
- A brahman begetter hired for 500 dīnāras; Śiva arranges the adoption in dreams.
- At the funeral offering, three hands rise: the thief's with an iron spike, a brahman's with a kuśa-grass ring, a king's with a signet ring (88-92).

**2. Does the verdict depend on birth?** No. It goes against hierarchy: the brahman "sold himself" (97), the king was paid for rearing (98-99), and the thief wins. Ś has the Vetāla object to both blood and rearing (N1:372).

**3. Caste and class risk: moderate.** The status emblems are essential to the image, and the verdict's subversion of them is a strength. I disagree with B-019:71, which reads this only as a risk.

**4. Coercion and power risk: high.** Dispossession backed by the court; impalement; a daughter sold by her mother; a woman as "field".

**5. Roles**

| Character | Code | Proposed | Effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| Candraprabha, the thief, the king | E | Keep | — |
| Begetter | E; brahman marker H | Keep the kuśa-ring hand | The verdict's bite against status survives |
| Dhanavatī | E | She accepts the bargain herself, knowing what it is | Gives her agency; the water-gift claim stays |
| Mother | E | Her greed set against the dispossession | — |
| Courtesan, Gayā priests | I | Compress | — |

**6. Proposed adaptation**
- Show the royal-backed robbery as the first injustice.
- Show the impalement as the state's cruelty, not as justice.
- Keep Ś's objection so the unease stays with the reader.

**7. Does the dilemma survive?** Yes, with changes.

**8. Recommendation: ADAPT** (moderate confidence).

### TC-020: The boy who laughed at his sacrifice

**1. Markers in the sources**
- A *brahmarākṣasa* (brahman-demon) demands "a brahman boy of about seven … who gives himself willingly … held down by mother and father while the king himself kills him" (74-82).
- The minister Sumati's proclamation offers a golden statue and a hundred villages (88-95).
- The boy is "devoted from former births" and argues that "the body is impure" (N1:380).
- The gods rain flowers.
- Hindi: the king beheads him, and a verse says woman is the source of misery (N1:387).
- TC-004 and TC-020 both sacrifice a brahman boy (N1:493).

**2. Does the verdict depend on birth?** No. The answer indicts the protectors: the parents (greed), the king ("ready to kill him to save himself") and the deity (130-133). The caste specification gives the victim's birth a ritual value.

**3. Caste and class risk: moderate.**

**4. Violence and power risk: central.**
- A king buys his life with a child.
- State machinery organises the search and the reward; the parents are paid.
- A child's "consent" is treated as meaningful.
- Divine flower-rain celebrates it.

**5. Roles**

| Character | Code | Proposed | Effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| Boy | E (caste I) | "A child" | — |
| Parents | E | Keep, with greed and persuasion, not poverty (see C) | — |
| King | E (keep the ruler; the answer indicts him) | Keep | — |
| Sumati and the proclamation | H | Keep | The state's complicity becomes visible |
| Demon | E (caste I) | "The demon of the tree" | — |
| Prelude (Kaṇva) | I | Compress | — |
| Flower-rain | I | Cut, or make it the crowd's projection | Removes the celebration |

**6. Proposed adaptation**
- Use the KSS open ending only.
- His willingness reads as a child carrying adults' burden, not as a model to follow.
- Mirror the frame: Vikram, a king, answers about a king.

**7. Does the dilemma survive?** Yes, with changes.

## (A) The frame

- **The ascetic.** His caste and sect vary by version (CAT:701; CMP:258, 263):
  - KSS: a *bhikṣu* ("monk", often a Buddhist term).
  - Ś: a *digambara* (a Jain naked ascetic) in the printed conclusion.
  - J: a *kāpālika* (a skull-bearing Tantric Śaiva ascetic).
  - Hindi: "a potter-devotee who has killed the oilman and made him the demon".
- **The Hindi birth triad.** The Hindi prehistory has three men born at one moment: "a king, an oilman's son, and a potter-devotee" (CMP:221). Birth-status maps onto destiny: the royal is the hero, and the artisan castes become the demon and the would-be killer of the king.
- **The Vetāla's origin.** In J the Vetāla "was a householder, an oil-miller by caste" (*tailika*) (REV:204).
- **The planned human sacrifice.** The ascetic plans to sacrifice Vikram for power. Vikram kills him in turn:
  - In J he offers him to Devī and is rewarded with universal rule (CMP:263).
  - In Ś he asks for the Vetāla as his servant (CMP:263).
  - In KSS Śiva confirms his divinely ordained kingship (CAT:707).
- **What works.** The penalty forces a king to judge and answer, which suits the charter. The frame also inverts the tales: here a renunciant would sacrifice a king, where the tales have kings sacrificing subjects.
- **Recommendations**
  - The ascetic has no caste and no sect; his motive is hunger for power.
  - Avoid the Hindi triad.
  - If the J oil-miller origin is kept, make it the story of a working man exploited by a teacher, not a monstrous origin.
  - Vikram kills in self-defence, with no offering to a deity, and no spirit made his servant.
  - Do not attach the cremation-ground horror to people who work there.

## (B) The five most serious problems, ranked

1. **The frame's coding.** Its villain and monster are marked by caste or sect (potter, oilman, oil-miller; *bhikṣu*, *digambara*), and the hero is rewarded for a sacrificial killing. This colours all 25 tales.
2. **TC-025's verdict.** Moral corruption from birth into a courtesan's family, plus pollution by contact.
3. **TC-020.** A king buys his life with a child, the state runs the search, and the narrative celebrates it.
4. **TC-016, Hindi XV.** Courage as Kshatriya practice. If Q-401 (choice of primary source) picks the Hindi lineage, it also brings in the Kanjar (XXI) and the frame triad.
5. **A hierarchy of the value of life, and stereotyped groups.** *Brahmahatyā* in TC-013 and TC-022 (and V's reading in TC-027); *śabara*/"Bhil" robbers (TC-024); the Kanjar (TC-022, Hindi).

Close behind: TC-001's "served his master" defence, and TC-027's restored king.

Three tales outside the 19 would outrank all of these if they were reinstated:
- TC-009: an explicit varṇa verdict (N1:217).
- TC-004: "as the threads, so the cloth" (N1:142).
- TC-017: sati in Ś and the Hindi (N1:342).

## (C) Where a fix could backfire

- **TC-006.** Dropping the washer-folk erases rare non-elite protagonists with dignity. Keep the occupation as work.
- **TC-019.** Removing the brahman's kuśa ring removes the verdict's subversion of status. Keep the emblems.
- **TC-008.** De-brahmanising the brothers kills the satire of purity pride.
- **TC-001.** Making Padmāvatī a princess (as Platts's Hindi may) erases the class asymmetry.
- **TC-020.** Making the victim a poor or lower-caste child implies such children are expendable. Drop caste and do not add poverty.
- **TC-022 and TC-024.** Do not swap in another named community, and do not make the coercing chief a tribal man.
- **TC-025.** Erasing her profession hides an injustice tied to a hereditary occupation. Removing the moral verdict is enough; do not "redeem" her by marriage.
- **The frame.** Making the ascetic a brahman to spare Buddhists, Jains and artisans creates a new anti-brahman stereotype. Leave him unmarked.
- **TC-016.** Replacing "many births" with "raised a prince" brings caste back in as upbringing. Use "chosen practice".
- **The slate as a whole.** Do not turn every thief into a noble outlaw, or every king into a commoner. The rulers in TC-001, 007, 015, 016, 020 and 027, and in the frame, must stay, because the accountability is theirs.
