## Preliminary findings that apply to the whole slate

- **N9 says** (`selection/RESEARCH_IMPACT_AUDIT.md:14, 87–98`): the work is "Targeted check only if a candidate is a serious contender."
  - The KSS layer of gendered material is already listed: TC-003's answer and thief's remark, TC-014, TC-021's aside, TC-009's caste reasoning, and TC-024's helpless-woman line. So is the J layer for TC-025–027.
  - Still unread in context: the Ś maxims in Ś 3, 9 and 13, and the Hindi maxims in XIII, XVII and XIX.
  - It finds "Eligibility: no", because difficult material is not a reason to exclude. It finds "Source choice: yes", because some moralising sits in only one version.
  - For this slate, that leaves four passages unread in context: Ś 3, Ś 9, Hindi XVII and Hindi XIX.
- **The dossiers misuse D-006.** Nearly every dossier ends its §7 with "Not a reason for exclusion (D-006)". But D-006 (`DECISIONS.md:43`) is about not forcing morals. It says nothing about whether difficult material is acceptable.
  - I found no decision in `DECISIONS.md` that records your gender, consent or agency rules. Until one exists, reviewers have nothing to cite, and D-006 is being stretched to cover the gap.
  - I recommend registering those rules as a decision.
- **The evidence itself frames one woman in a gendered way.** N1 lists Indīvaraprabhā as "the cause of the trespass" (`research/SOMADEVA_TALE_EVIDENCE.md:384`), and the TC-020 dossier repeats it as "through whom the trespass came about" (`TC-020.md:14`). But the recorded events show the demon threatening *the king* "for violating" the tree (`SOMADEVA:380`). The text, as summarised, doesn't support blaming her.
- **The frame already gives you a tool for this.** The frame lets a verdict be contested inside the story, with no narrator commentary:
  - The Vetāla objects to Vikrama's answer, and he defends it, in TC-001, 004 and 017 (`SOMADEVA:97, 469–480`). Ś and the Hindi add more objections.
  - Vikrama answers *so that the Vetāla will leave* in TC-022 (12.29.47, `SOMADEVA:411`).
  - In TC-024 he doesn't know the answer at all (12.31.60).
  - So a verdict is a character's judgement under pressure, not the narrator's. I rely on this throughout.

## Per-tale blocks

### TC-001 — The prince, the minister's son and Padmāvatī
1. **Women.**
   - Padmāvatī is the most active mind in the opening. She sets the signs, controls the timing ("ten nights", "three nights of my period") and arranges the way in. She may try to poison the adviser; that is reported only through a dog's death and his own account (`SOMADEVA:95, 103`).
   - From verse 153 on, she becomes an object. She is made drunk, branded on the thigh by her husband, robbed, falsely denounced as a *ḍākinī* (a flesh-eating witch), banished, and "taken" away on horseback. Her consent to leaving is not recorded (`SOMADEVA:95`, events 8–11).
   - Her mother dies after her father (185). The nurse is a bribed go-between who is publicly humiliated (102).
   - The dossier calls her "a participant, not a prize" (`TC-001.md:56`). That is true only up to verse 152; after that she is handled as a possession.
2. **Question and answer.**
   - The question asks who bears the sin of the *parents'* deaths (12.8.186–187). The answer is King Karṇotpala, for acting without inquiry (190–195).
   - The minister's son is cleared because it was "his master's business" (192). The lovers are "not to be judged" because they were "burned by love's arrows" (193).
   - There is no gendered generalisation. The double standard is structural: the question never asks about the harm done to Padmāvatī, and "love" and "orders" excuse the branding. Padmāvatī is cleared on the same grounds as the prince, which is at least even-handed. The narrator calls her *dhūrtā*, "crafty" (134).
3. **Consent: high.** She is drugged, branded, defamed and removed. The source presents the scheme as cleverness (`TC-001.md:72`).
4. **Men.** Buddhiśarīra is defined entirely as an instrument of his master. The king's dead son is a pretext and is never introduced (152). Padmāvatī's father dies offstage in one verse.
5. **Adaptation.**
   - Keep the uncertainty about the poisoning, told through the adviser's self-serving reading. The source supports this (`SOMADEVA:103`).
   - Tell the branding as what it is: no comic cleverness, and the prince's hand visible.
   - After the banishment, give Padmāvatī one moment where she understands the trident mark and a choice, even a bitter one, about going with them.
   - **Verdict.** The source answer is "the king", with the lovers excused by love. The problem is that the excuse makes passion a licence. Replace it: keep "the king" and the Vetāla's crow-and-goose objection (191) at full strength, but have Vikrama argue only that the deceivers did *their* wrongs while the *deaths* came from the one who had the duty to inquire. Drop the "love's arrows" exemption. The dilemma gets harder, not easier.
6. **Dilemma survives:** yes, with the change.
7. **Recommendation: ADAPT** (substantial), moderate confidence. The blame question is excellent; the branding needs reframing, not removal.

### TC-002 — Three brahmans who revived a dead girl
1. **Women.**
   - Mandāravatī never speaks or chooses (`TC-002.md:15`; `SOMADEVA:114`). Her father withholds her for the suitors' sake. When she is revived, the three men are "overcome with desire" and quarrel over her body (33–35).
   - A householder's wife throws a crying child into the fire "in anger" (*krudhā*, 18). The text gives no motive (`SOMADEVA:118`).
   - She is a prize in the purest sense.
2. **Question and answer.**
   - The question is "Whose wife does the girl properly become?" (12.9.36).
   - The answer: the reviver is her father, the bone-bearer her son, the one who kept vigil over her ashes her husband (38–40). Ś has father, *brother*, husband (`SOMADEVA:117`).
   - The verbs are *mataḥ* ("is counted") and *ucyate* ("is called") (`SOMADEVA:118`). That makes the answer a classification of what each man's act made him, not an award of the woman. There is no gendered generalisation.
3. **Consent: moderate.** She is allocated without being asked. The verdict at least strips two men of their claim.
4. **Men.** The three suitors are interchangeable and defined only by their acts.
5. **Adaptation.**
   - Before her death, give her a stake: she declines to choose because she knows refusing will cost a life. That converts the father's ban into her decision.
   - After the revival, she wakes among three men already arguing over her, and she is present when they make their claims.
   - Keep the verdict and its "is called" logic. Read that way, it says that giving someone life does not entitle you to them.
   - Don't make the child-burning a statement about women's rage; the text says only "in anger".
6. **Dilemma survives:** yes.
7. **Recommendation: ADAPT**, moderate-to-high confidence. The source verdict already pushes back against ownership; let the telling show it.

### TC-003 — The parrot and the mynah: are men or women worse?
1. **Women.**
   - The mynah Somikā argues, refuses the parrot's proposal, and wagers.
   - Ratnāvalī is thrown into a pit by her husband. She survives, shields him with a cover story, and is murdered sleeping in his lap (28–45).
   - Vasudattā takes a lover. A Vetāla in the lover's corpse bites off her nose, she falsely accuses her husband, and the king cuts off her ears and banishes her (`SOMADEVA:125`).
   - The wager terms are "slavery or wifehood" (*dāsatva-bhāryātva*, 13). Penzer reads it as: if the parrot wins, the mynah becomes his wife (`SOMADEVA:133`). So her refusal can be overridden by a bet.
2. **Question and answer.**
   - The question: "Are men wicked, or are women?" (12.10.92).
   - The answer is women, "generally, everywhere and always" (*prāyaḥ sarvatra sadā*, 93–94), with no reasoning (`SOMADEVA:127`).
   - The tale is reinforced by the thief's remark (72) and the parrot's closing line, "so women are by nature crooked" (88). In fairness, the narrator also remarks on wicked men at verse 27 (`SOMADEVA:130`).
   - Ś: "a man is rarely sinful." Hindi IV: "a woman does not give a thought to right and wrong" (`SOMADEVA:132`).
   - The source's own evidence applies unequal standards. Dhanadatta commits murder twice over and is never punished; Vasudattā is mutilated by the state. The verdict then condemns women.
3. **Consent: high** (the wifehood wager), plus victim harm (Ratnāvalī).
4. **Men.** The husband in the parrot's tale stays silent while under a death sentence, which is never explained (78).
5. **Adaptation.**
   - **Verdict.** The source answers "women, always". It is a collective judgement, it doesn't follow from the two cases, and it breaks your rules. Replace the *question* using Ś's and the Hindi's comparative form ("whose sin is greater?", `TC-003.md:43`), which D-034 permits: *which of these two did the worse thing?*
     - Vikrama answers Dhanadatta: he killed the one person who had already saved him.
     - The Vetāla objects: Vasudattā would have let an innocent man be executed to save her face.
     - The case is now genuinely arguable, and the dilemma becomes real where before it was empty.
   - Make the wager symmetric (the loser serves the winner for a season), not wifehood.
   - Cut the narrator's line at verse 88. The thief's remark (72) can stay as one character's prejudice.
   - Leave the unequal punishment visible and uncommented.
6. **Dilemma survives:** only with the change. As written, there is no dilemma.
7. **Recommendation: MAJOR REWRITE.** High confidence that the rewrite is needed; moderate that it earns a slot. I disagree with the dossier's view that the "two sexes' cases are both present" (`TC-003.md:70`); it misses the punishment asymmetry.

### TC-006 — The exchanged heads
1. **Women.**
   - Madanasundarī is the one who acts. She grieves, reproaches the goddess, prepares to hang herself, and is answered because of her courage (*sattvotkarṣa*, 44).
   - She joins the heads wrongly "in joyful haste" (46–47) and is left bewildered (`SOMADEVA:170, 175`).
   - She is not a prize, but the question is still about which man she belongs to.
2. **Question and answer.** "Who is her husband?" (51). Answer: the body with the husband's head, because the head is chief and recognition lives in it (53). No gendered reasoning.
3. **Consent: low to moderate.** Her own wish about which man she now lives with is never asked, and there is an unspoken question about bodies and intimacy.
4. **Men.** Two men behead themselves on "fate" and "delusion" alone (`SOMADEVA:178`); they are disposable and given no motive.
5. **Adaptation.**
   - Tell her haste as joy, darkness and blood, not as female flightiness (`TC-006.md:71` flags this framing).
   - Give the husband a motive for the offering (a vow for their happiness), so the self-sacrifice isn't empty.
   - Keep her present at the judgement: she is the one who has to live with the answer.
   - Keep the verdict.
6. **Dilemma survives:** yes.
7. **Recommendation: ADAPT** (light), high confidence. Already one of the slate's strongest female agents.

### TC-007 — Sattvaśīla and the Daitya princess
1. **Women.**
   - The Daitya princess has cities, power and the goddess's favour. She takes the king for the husband promised by her worship (87); he refuses her coolly.
   - She then calls him "father" and places herself and her cities in his power (102). He gives her to Sattvaśīla. "She agrees" (103–104).
   - He then says one āmalaka fruit is now repaid (106). She is literally half a debt.
   - The dossier's "a handling matter" (`TC-007.md:69`) understates this.
2. **Question and answer.** "Which of the two showed greater courage?" (110). Answer: Sattvaśīla, who leapt without knowledge or expectation (113a). No gendered reasoning, though verse 113b, on the king not desiring her, is unclear (`SOMADEVA:193`).
3. **Consent: moderate to high.** A woman used as a reward and payment, though assent is recorded.
4. **Men.** Sattvaśīla serves for years unrewarded and unnoticed; poverty and class make him invisible.
5. **Adaptation.**
   - Move the choice to her. She has seen Sattvaśīla in her city (she was the one who sent him back through the tank). She learns he dived without knowing what lay below, and she chooses him.
   - The king's repayment becomes the *dive itself* (risk for risk), not the woman.
   - The "father" line can stay as her own political move.
   - This sharpens the question: both men took the same leap, and only one knew what was down there.
6. **Dilemma survives:** yes.
7. **Recommendation: ADAPT**, high confidence.

### TC-008 — The three fastidious brothers
1. **Women.**
   - The courtesan is sent by the king as a test object. The man nearly faints and says she "smells of goat". She reveals she was separated from her mother and nurse as a baby and raised on goat's milk (27–37, `SOMADEVA:200`).
   - The category "fastidious about women" puts women alongside rice and mattresses. The dossier's "No gendered generalisation" (`TC-008.md:71`) is literally true but misses this objectification.
2. **Question and answer.** "Who is the most fastidious?" Answer: the bed-connoisseur, because only his evidence was seen on his own body; the other two "might have learned their facts beforehand" (12.15.51). Neutral.
3. **Consent: low to moderate** (objectification and public exposure, not coercion).
4. **Men.** Three vain brothers neglect their father's sacrifice; the joke is on them.
5. **Adaptation.**
   - Give the courtesan the scene, and the joke at the man's expense. Her story of being raised on goat's milk is something people in the city already know. That plants Vikrama's own reasoning (51) that the claim could have been learned in advance.
   - The verdict then gets more support, and she stops being the butt of the joke.
   - Don't replace "women" with "perfume" (see D).
6. **Dilemma survives:** yes.
7. **Recommendation: ADAPT**, moderate-to-high confidence.

### TC-010 — Madanasenā's promise
1. **Women.**
   - Madanasenā is the moral engine of the tale. Fearing rape (*balātkāra-bhaya*, 22), she bargains, delays and swears an oath. She keeps her word through the wedding night, the thief and the lover, out of her own principle of truthfulness (`SOMADEVA:230`).
   - But the men's "generosity" consists of giving *her* up. The thief let go "a jewel of a woman, ornaments and all" (67), putting her on a level with her jewels.
   - Her husband trusts her after "seeing her face unchanged and no sign of intercourse" (59–61): he inspects her body.
2. **Question and answer.**
   - The question: "Who was truly generous?" (*kas tyāgī*, 63). KSS answers the thief, judging each man by the pressures on him (65–67). Neutral as reasoning.
   - Ś 9 and Hindi IX add the lover's verses on women's untrustworthiness.
   - J 10 has the husband reject her as defiled, and calls *him* the noblest (`TC-010.md:27, 82`; `research/SOURCE_TRADITIONS_COMPARISON.md:349–354`). That verdict rewards punishing a woman for sexual "honour".
3. **Consent: high.** An oath extracted under threat of force, a wedding night, and an inspection of her body. KSS does not romanticise the threat.
4. **Men.** The lover's "love" is a threat that wears off with time and fear (66). The thief is the only one who gives up power over her.
5. **Adaptation.**
   - Use KSS. Exclude J's rejection ending and the Ś/Hindi verses (D-034 permits this).
   - Replace the inspection with the husband *taking her word*. The whole tale is about her word, so this strengthens its logic.
   - Reword the "jewel" line so the thief's renunciation is of his power over her, not of a jewel.
   - Keep the threat of force explicit: it is what makes her promise rational rather than foolish.
   - Keep the verdict.
6. **Dilemma survives:** yes.
7. **Recommendation: ADAPT**, high confidence. Potentially the slate's best woman-led tale.

### TC-013 — Harisvāmin and the poisoned rice
1. **Women.**
   - Lāvaṇyavatī is carried off by a Vidyādhara while she sleeps, then never mentioned again (`SOMADEVA:275`). An abducted woman used purely as a plot trigger.
   - Padmanābha's wife feeds a starving stranger out of pity. The dying man blames her; her husband throws her out; she goes off to do penance "falsely disgraced" (51–52).
   - The Hindi turns her compassion into "at the bidding of her husband" (`SOMADEVA:280`).
2. **Question and answer.**
   - The question: whose is the brahman-murder? The answer is no one in the chain. The guilt falls on the dull person who blames without reflection (56–59).
   - There is no gendered reasoning. In effect, the verdict condemns the husband who expelled her: he is exactly the hasty judge it describes. The dossier doesn't notice this.
3. **Consent: moderate.** An unresolved abduction, and in-text victim-blaming that the narrative presents as unjust.
4. **Men.** Harisvāmin is a decent man destroyed by accident.
5. **Adaptation.**
   - Use KSS, not the Hindi phrasing.
   - Make the host's wife a named person with an inner life, the emotional centre of the ending, so the verdict lands on Padmanābha without a word of commentary.
   - Let the abducted wife be felt as a real loss, not a launching pad. Don't invent a rescue arc (see D).
6. **Dilemma survives:** yes.
7. **Recommendation: ADAPT**, high confidence.

### TC-015 — The magic pill (Śaśiprabhā)
1. **Women.**
   - Śaśiprabhā loves her rescuer and consents to a *gāndharva* (mutual-consent) marriage *after* he reveals himself, with "shame and wonder" (54). She is then given by her deceived father to Śaśin, and her wishes are not consulted in the dispute (`SOMADEVA:305, 313`).
   - Mṛgāṅkavatī is seduced by deception (the Ilā story, the "man by night" claim), carried off, and then dropped (83–99).
2. **Question and answer.**
   - The question: "Whose wife is she properly?" (111). The answer is Śaśin: her father gave her openly, and "a thief can never have rightful ownership of what belongs to another" (113–114). This is explicitly woman-as-property.
   - Ś and the Hindi add that she is pregnant and the child will perform Śaśin's funeral rites, so his ownership extends to her child. Ś's reasoning is deceit (*chadmanā praviṣṭaḥ*, "entered by deceit"), not property (`SOMADEVA:312`). J calls the lover's act forbidden in this world and the next.
3. **Consent: central.** The riddle is about consent and property, and one woman's sexual consent is obtained by fraud.
4. **Men.** The minister's son is handed a "bride" (Manaḥsvāmin in disguise) to keep the state stable. The king is driven by fear of a brahman's curse.
5. **Adaptation.**
   - **Verdict.** The source rules for Śaśin on property grounds. That makes a woman stealable goods. Replace the reasoning with Ś's deceit rationale (attested), and make Śaśiprabhā's informed consent a factor the Vetāla raises against Vikrama.
     - Both claims should be compromised. Manaḥsvāmin has her consent but also deceived Mṛgāṅkavatī. Śaśin has the public rite but got it through Mūladeva's fraud.
     - Vikrama can still answer Śaśin ("the lie that came first") or answer Manaḥsvāmin ("a gift won by fraud was not the father's to give"). Either way the dilemma is harder than in KSS.
   - Mṛgāṅkavatī: her bridegroom fell for the disguised "girl" at his own wedding and then left on pilgrimage. Let her see through the Ilā story and choose to run. Deception becomes an escape, and the comedy survives.
6. **Dilemma survives:** yes, with the change.
7. **Recommendation: MAJOR REWRITE** of the verdict's reasoning and the Mṛgāṅkavatī strand, moderate confidence. I disagree with "Strong core contender" (`TC-015.md:86`) unless this rewrite is accepted.

### TC-016 — Jīmūtavāhana
1. **Women.**
   - Malayavatī falls in love and tries to hang herself (60–79). Later her reproach to Gaurī triggers the revival (181–190).
   - Śaṅkhacūḍa's mother refuses Jīmūtavāhana's offer to take her son's place and laments (`SOMADEVA:320`).
   - Background: Kadrū's enslavement of Vinatā.
   - Not a prize.
2. **Question and answer.** Greater courage: Śaṅkhacūḍa, because Jīmūtavāhana's act was "perfected over many births" (205–206). No gendered reasoning; the Hindi's caste grounding is a caste issue (`TC-016.md:28`).
3. **Consent: none to low.**
4. **Men.** Young Nāgas are handed over to be eaten by their own king's pact. Sons serve as tribute.
5. **Adaptation.**
   - Give the mother's refusal full weight as a scene.
   - Compress Malayavatī's noose, because it repeats a slate-wide motif (see C). Keep her reproach to the goddess as the active turn.
6. **Dilemma survives:** yes.
7. **Recommendation: ADAPT**, high confidence.

### TC-018 — The lost magic science
1. **Women.**
   - Women appear as amenities of the magic city: each night Candrasvāmin "spends the night with a beautiful woman" (37–43).
   - Inside the illusion there is a wife whose tears make him waver at the pyre (72–74) (`SOMADEVA:350`).
2. **Question and answer.** The answer is a wavering mind, and a teacher who gave the science to an unworthy pupil (85–86). Ś and the Hindi add fate. No gendered reasoning.
3. **Consent: low** (women furnished as a reward).
4. **Men.** The gambler is beaten and left for dead.
5. **Adaptation.**
   - Drop the nightly woman-as-reward.
   - Give the illusory wife enough reality that the reader feels the wavering: is it a failing to hesitate for someone who feels real? This sharpens the dilemma.
6. **Dilemma survives:** yes.
7. **Recommendation: ADAPT**, high confidence.

### TC-019 — The thief's son and the three hands
1. **Women.**
   - Dhanavatī's greedy mother gives her, for gold, to a dying thief on a stake (22–23).
   - Dhanavatī then *chooses* Manaḥsvāmin from her roof and pays him for one night (35–46): a woman buying a man.
   - On Śiva's instruction in a dream, she leaves her baby at the king's gate (`SOMADEVA:365`).
   - She has real agency, which the dossier underplays (`TC-019.md:53` calls the characters "functional").
2. **Question and answer.**
   - The offering goes to the thief. The begetter "sold himself" for money (97) and the king was paid for the rearing (98–99). The son belongs to the man who received the mother by water-gift and whose permission she conceived under (100–101).
   - The *kṣetraja* ("son of the field") doctrine makes the woman a field, owned land. Notice the even-handed point: the man paid for sex loses paternity.
   - The mother is absent from the question, which is structural to the funeral rite.
3. **Consent: high.** A bride sold by her mother; a woman's body as property in the verdict's logic.
4. **Men.** The son is a vehicle for rites. The thief's anxiety about having no son drives everything.
5. **Adaptation.**
   - Let Dhanavatī make the first bargain herself: two destitute women in flight, gold for survival, and her own hand pouring the water. It is still duress, but it becomes her decision.
   - Keep her choice of Manaḥsvāmin and her decision about the child.
   - **Verdict.** Keep "the thief" and the payment logic. Drop the explicit "field" metaphor in Vikrama's mouth, or leave it to the Vetāla to voice, so it stays a doctrine voiced by a character rather than the narrator's view.
   - Structurally, all three "fathers" now trace back to *her* decisions, which deepens the puzzle.
6. **Dilemma survives:** yes, with the change.
7. **Recommendation: ADAPT** (substantial), moderate confidence.

### TC-020 — The boy who laughed
1. **Women.**
   - The mother holds the boy's hands while the father holds his feet, both for wealth (123–125). The blame is even.
   - Ś has the mother refuse to give the youngest son, which is complex and humane.
   - The Hindi closes with a verse that "woman is the source of misery" (`SOMADEVA:387`; `TC-020.md:44, 75`).
   - The queen is wrongly cast as the trespass's cause in N1 and the dossier (see preliminary findings).
2. **Question and answer.** Why did he laugh? Because every protector had become a threat, and his own wish was fulfilled (130–136). No gendered reasoning in KSS.
3. **Consent: gender risk low.** The child-consent and child-sacrifice issue is grave but outside this review.
4. **Men.** A boy used as currency; a king who saves himself.
5. **Adaptation.**
   - Use KSS and omit the Hindi maxim.
   - Correct the queen's framing in the working notes.
   - Ś's protective mother can be borrowed (D-034) to make both parents individuals.
6. **Dilemma survives:** yes.
7. **Recommendation: ADAPT**, high confidence on gender. Child sacrifice is the real handling question, and outside this review.

### TC-021 — Anaṅgamañjarī
1. **Women.**
   - Anaṅgamañjarī is married by her father and kept at home. She finds her husband "hateful like bitter medicine" (9). She loves at a glance, tries to hang herself, and dies of joy in her lover's arms.
   - She is revived "with passion stilled" (*śāntānaṅgāḥ*) and returned to her husband while her father celebrates (85–91).
   - Mālatikā cuts the noose and arranges the meeting: capable and loyal (`SOMADEVA:395`).
2. **Question and answer.**
   - Most deluded: the husband (94–96). He is "excessively deluded" because, "at a time for anger", he died of love instead.
   - No generalisation, but the reasoning assumes anger is a husband's normal right.
   - The narrator's aside that bad women disgrace families (81) is unequal: Kamalākara gets no such line.
3. **Consent: moderate to high.** An unchosen marriage, and a goddess who *erases her desire* to restore the marriage, presented as a happy ending.
4. **Men.** Maṇivarman is loving and unloved. Kamalākara's heart bursts, then he goes home in shame.
5. **Adaptation.**
   - Cut aside 81.
   - Keep the revival but make "passion stilled" uncanny and costly, not a celebration. The father's feast can feel hollow.
   - Don't supply an invented reason for her aversion (compare N1's caution on Ratnavatī, `SOMADEVA:298`). The unchosen marriage is enough.
   - Keep the verdict. The phrase "a time for anger" can be the Vetāla's provocation rather than Vikrama's premise.
6. **Dilemma survives:** yes.
7. **Recommendation: ADAPT**, moderate-to-high confidence. I'd also drop the dossier's "a wife's contempt" (`TC-021.md:74`); "she could not love him" is closer to the text.

### TC-022 — Four brothers and the lion
1. **Women.** Only the mother, who "follows" her husband in death (3–7, `SOMADEVA:410`), a passing widow-death.
2. **Question and answer.** The one who gave the lion life, because he knew what it was (48–50). Neutral.
3. **Consent: none; low** for the widow-death detail.
4. **Men.** Four orphans are disposable. The eldest tried to hang himself.
5. **Adaptation.** Don't present the mother's death as a norm or a virtue; a death of grief is enough. Nothing else is needed on gender.
6. **Dilemma survives:** yes.
7. **Recommendation: ADAPT** (light), high confidence. A men-only tale is fine in a balanced slate.

### TC-024 — The final riddle (father and son marry daughter and mother)
1. **Women.**
   - Candravatī and Lāvaṇyavatī are refugees whose husband and father has just been killed. A father and son divide them by footprint size ("take whichever woman he likes").
   - The queen accepts "as if in another birth" with the line "what can a helpless woman do, fallen into misfortune in a foreign land?" (50). The daughter's consent is never recorded (`SOMADEVA:440, 445`).
   - These are prizes in the most literal sense.
2. **Question and answer.** What are the children to each other (58–59)? No answer: Vikrama "did not know" (60). No gendered reasoning, but line 50 is listed in N9 (`SOMADEVA:496`).
3. **Consent: central.** Coerced remarriage under exile; "rescue" turned into acquisition.
4. **Men.** The father is grieving and pushed into marriage by his son's verse and oath (24–34).
5. **Adaptation.**
   - The riddle depends on the marriages crossing generations, *not* on coercion. Keep the footprints as male presumption that misfires (the small feet are the mother's).
   - Give both women the decisive moment. Candravatī weighs protection against exile, with line 50 as *her own* clear-eyed sentence rather than the narrator's. Lāvaṇyavatī is asked, or answers.
   - Don't soften it into romance.
6. **Dilemma survives:** yes. The unanswerable kinship knot is untouched.
7. **Recommendation: ADAPT**, moderate-to-high confidence. See (A).

### TC-025 — Four brothers and the courtesan (J 21)
1. **Women.**
   - Rūpavatī is the slate's fullest woman. She remembers four lives of abandonment by the same husband and made her one-night rule from that memory (`research/JAMBHALADATTA_ADDITIONAL_TALES_EVIDENCE.md:61`, items 9–10).
   - She is the obstacle in a male inheritance contest. The female ascetic who is her teacher is used to spy on her.
   - Kanakadatta steals her story, then her wealth, and tells the king "secretly". She never learns (`J…EVIDENCE.md:66`).
2. **Question and answer.**
   - The question: why did she not recognise him? The answer: born a courtesan, she became "very wicked" (*pāpiṣṭhā*), and through union with other men she forgot (T p. 124, `J…EVIDENCE.md:63`).
   - This is victim-blaming with a double standard. The brothers slept with her too, and the man who abandoned her four times is not judged.
   - Her own verdict that men are cruel and loveless (Tr p. 121) is a character's generalisation. It mirrors TC-003.
3. **Consent: central.** Sex and wealth obtained by fraud; the victim blamed.
4. **Men.** Three elder brothers fail. Pratāpasena is a coward across four lives.
5. **Adaptation.**
   - **Verdict.** The source says her sin made her forget. That blames the fraud's victim for her sex work. Replace it with: her gift let her know a *body* in each life; this time she was given only *words*, the one story she had waited four lives to hear. Memory of past births tells you who you were, not who is lying.
   - The Vetāla can object with the source's own answer, keeping it on record as a voiced view.
   - Keep the king's secret, so the reader alone carries the irony.
6. **Dilemma survives:** yes, with the change. The question gets better.
7. **Recommendation: MAJOR REWRITE** of the verdict, moderate confidence, given J-only status.

### TC-026 — Mūladeva, the bawd and Anaṅgasenā (J 22)
1. **Women.**
   - Anaṅgasenā has been guarded for twelve years and is wasting away. A bawd posing as an ascetic falsely tells her parents the husband has married abroad and prescribes a sexual "charm".
   - She becomes Śaśideva's lover, "forgets" her husband, has her death faked, and is presented as a lookalike (`J…EVIDENCE.md:81`). Her agency is ambiguous in the text (`TC-026.md:79`).
   - The bawd Kāmikalpalatā is a powerful female schemer, able to "disturb even Arundhatī" (a byword for wifely fidelity).
2. **Question and answer.** Who had more ready wit? J answers Mūladeva. The Newari answers the bawd, because she was the occasion of all the cleverness (`J…EVIDENCE.md:83, 185`). No gendered reasoning.
3. **Consent: high.** Her parents are tricked into arranging sex for her, her consent is shaped by a lie, and her identity is erased.
4. **Men.** The absent husband's fate is never told. The king is deceived twice.
5. **Adaptation.**
   - Make Anaṅgasenā knowingly complicit, not the mastermind. A woman abandoned and guarded like property uses the bawd's "charm" as her cover to get out.
   - The parents' grief remains the real cost.
   - Use the Newari answer (the bawd). It is attested, and it credits the woman whose deception made everything else possible.
6. **Dilemma survives:** yes.
7. **Recommendation: ADAPT** (substantial), moderate confidence. Its overlap with TC-015 on Mūladeva matters for the slate.

### TC-027 — The ogre and Arimaulimaṇi (J 23)
1. **Women.**
   - Śobhāvatī is found alive in a creature's belly. She says her wish is fulfilled when the king resolves to marry her, then goes passive (`J…EVIDENCE.md:123, 127`).
   - The ogress Kumbhodarī poses as a courtesan and eats the men who come to her. She takes a lover and kills him out of jealousy when he stays with his wife "at her insistence". Her stated motive is revenge for her son.
   - The weeping mother's son is chosen by lot.
2. **Question and answer.** Who is guilty for the dead? The king, with no reasoning given. Neutral.
3. **Consent: low to moderate.** The sexualised man-eating woman is a trope, not coercion.
4. **Men.** Citizens handed over by lot as tribute.
5. **Adaptation.**
   - Keep Kumbhodarī frightening, with a *mother's* motive in front. Make the courtesan disguise a hunting tactic, not a comment on courtesans.
   - Give Śobhāvatī a beat in the flight.
6. **Dilemma survives:** yes.
7. **Recommendation: ADAPT**, low-to-moderate confidence, because the witnesses are thin.

## (A) Frame-narrative gender audit

- **There are no women in the documented frame.** The catalogue's frame table (`research/CANDIDATE_TALE_CATALOGUE.md:694–714`) and the report's §5 (`research/SOURCE_TRADITIONS_COMPARISON.md:251–268`) record only men and gods. King, monk/yogī/*kāpālika*, Vetāla, Śiva, the Gandharvas, Indra; Devī appears in J's ending.
  - The Hindi prehistory is recorded only as "the fruit of immortality; three men born at one moment" (`COMPARISON.md:221`).
  - *Not in the repository; verify before relying:* in common tellings, the Bharthari fruit story turns on a queen's infidelity and leads to the king's renunciation. If the Hindi frame is chosen (Q-002), that would put a misogynistic exemplum at the cycle's threshold. N9 should check it.
- **The judging structure is all-male.** One male judge, one male questioner, and women as the *subject matter* of the questions. N1 notes the questions "are mostly asked about the men" (`SOMADEVA:485`). This is the frame's real gender effect.
  - The fix is not a token woman added to the frame; that would be invention, and Q-005 leaves the narrator's identity open.
  - The fix is to use the frame's own mechanisms: the Vetāla's objections, Vikrama's fallibility, and his strategic answers. A verdict then becomes a judgement under pressure, never the collection's last word.
- **The final riddle (TC-024)** is the frame's hinge. Vikrama's silence (12.31.60) triggers the Vetāla's warning and the ending (61–74).
  - So the cycle's climax rests on two refugee women divided by footprints. If it is told as comedy alone, the collection *ends* on women as puzzle pieces.
  - Told with Candravatī's one sentence at full weight, the unanswerable kinship knot gains a second, unspoken edge (the women's position) without a lecture.
  - The riddle itself is not incest; the dossier is right on that (`TC-024.md:73`).

## (B) Five most serious problems, ranked

1. **TC-003's verdict is a collective judgement on women** ("everywhere and always", 12.10.93–94). It is reinforced by the narrator at verse 88 and given a reason in Hindi IV. It is the only answer in the pool that *is* a generalisation.
2. **TC-025 blames the victim.** A defrauded woman is "very wicked" for sex work (T p. 124), while the fraudster is made a minister and the brothers who slept with her are not judged.
3. **TC-015's woman-as-property verdict** ("a thief has no right to another's property", 12.22.114). Her consent is ignored, the pregnancy variant extends ownership to her child, and Mṛgāṅkavatī is seduced by deception and dropped.
4. **TC-024's coerced allocation of a refugee mother and daughter** (12.31.50), placed at the frame's climax.
5. **TC-001 presents the drugging, branding and defamation of Padmāvatī as a clever plan,** with a verdict that excuses the perpetrators by "love" and "service" (192–193).

Close behind: TC-019 (a bride sold by her mother; the woman as "field"), TC-026 (consent obtained by deception), TC-021 (desire erased by the goddess; the aside at 81), TC-010 (oath under threat; body inspection; J's rejection ending).

## (C) Patterns across the slate, and how to fix them without repeating the same fix

**Patterns:**
- **Allocation of women.** "Whose wife?" or "giving her" appears in TC-002, 006, 007, 010, 015, 024, and in TC-019's *kṣetra* logic. The slate already excludes the starkest suitor contests, TC-005 and TC-009 (the latter has caste reasoning). That is worth keeping in mind if the slate changes.
- **Women tie nooses or die at goddess shrines.** Madanasundarī (TC-006), Malayavatī (TC-016) and Anaṅgamañjarī (TC-021): three times on one slate.
- **Women's bodies used as evidence.** A thigh brand (001), a face inspected (010), a nose and ears cut (003), a goat smell (008), mixed bodies (006).
- **Courtesans as objects or predators.** TC-008, 019 (the courtesan Haṃsāvalī), 025, 027.
- **Mothers who are monstrous, greedy or only weeping.** TC-002, 019 and 020; TC-016 and 027.
- **Men are disposable too:** TC-006, 016, 020, 022, 027.
- **A strength to build on:** a network of women go-betweens and confidantes (the nurse, Mālatikā, the ascetic, the bawd), and women who confront goddesses and are answered (TC-006, 016).

**Strategy: assign a different tool to each tale, rather than applying "she speaks up" everywhere:**
1. **Choose the attested variant** (D-034): TC-010 KSS, TC-015 Ś's deceit reasoning, TC-020 KSS, TC-026 Newari.
2. **Let the Vetāla object:** TC-001, 003, 015, 025.
3. **Keep the answer, change the reasoning:** TC-001, 002, 015, 019.
4. **Move a decision to the woman:** TC-007, 019, 026, 024.
5. **Add interior life without changing events:** TC-013 host's wife, TC-018 illusory wife, TC-008 courtesan.
6. **Cut narrator maxims only:** TC-003 v. 88, TC-021 v. 81, Hindi XIX.
7. **Leave an injustice visible and unresolved:** TC-001's brand, TC-013's expulsion, TC-025's secret.
8. **Thin out the repeated noose motif:** keep it in TC-006, where it is the hinge and her courage is rewarded; compress it in TC-016 and 021.
9. **Hold women to the same standard as men.** Vasudattā, Padmāvatī's possible poisoning, the bawd, Hiraṇyavatī and the ogress remain capable of real wrong.

## (D) Where a fix would over-correct

- **TC-003:** don't flip the verdict to "men are worse" (the same generalisation reversed), and don't have Vikrama refuse to answer. He must answer if he knows, and a second silence would weaken TC-024's.
- **TC-024:** don't turn the remarriage into a love match, and don't let the women escape. Either removes the riddle or erases the grim truth of their position.
- **TC-010:** don't add Madanasenā as a candidate for the verdict, don't soften the threat of force, and don't make the lover a cartoon villain.
- **TC-006:** don't make the head-swap deliberate or erotic. Her mistake is the engine.
- **TC-002:** don't let her pick a husband at the end; the classification riddle would collapse. Presence, not choice.
- **TC-021:** don't make the husband a brute to excuse the affair; the verdict's sadness depends on his love. Don't cut the revival entirely; make it uncanny.
- **TC-025:** don't give her a "reformed courtesan" ending (the Newari's renunciation of her trade is itself moralising), and don't make her happily complicit in being deceived.
- **TC-019 / TC-008:** don't strip out the sexual transaction or the courtesan. The verdict's payment logic and the comic social edge depend on them.
- **TC-001:** don't clean Padmāvatī into pure innocence by cutting the poisoning. Equal standards include her capacity for wrong.
- **TC-013:** don't invent a rescue for Lāvaṇyavatī. The tale is "short" by the Vetāla's own word (12.20.2).
- **Frame:** no token woman, and no narrator speeches.

On the dossiers' own assessments: their labels are reasonable on the story merits. The gaps are that "a handling matter" understates TC-007, TC-015 and TC-019; TC-001's "not a prize" holds only for the first half; and the D-006 formula is doing work it can't do.
