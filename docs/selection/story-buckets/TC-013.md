# TC-013: Harisvāmin, who died of poisoned food

> **Selection dossier, story bucket.** Evidence summary and qualitative assessment for the owner. Not a selection, ranking or adaptation. Provisional assessment: **Strong core contender**. Sources: [catalogue record](../../research/CANDIDATE_TALE_CATALOGUE.md), [N1 record](../../research/SOMADEVA_TALE_EVIDENCE.md) (TC-013), [comparison report §7](../../research/SOURCE_TRADITIONS_COMPARISON.md), [N3](../../research/JAMBHALADATTA_ADDITIONAL_TALES_EVIDENCE.md) §6.1.

## 1. Identity and narrative summary

- **Working title:** Harisvāmin and the poisoned rice (a catalogue label).
- **Recorded in:** KSS 13 (12.20.1–60, Tawney ch. 87; the Vetāla calls it a short tale, 12.20.2) · BKM 13 (provisional) · Ś 12 · J 12 · Hindi XII · Ryder 12.
- **Essential events (KSS, N1 TC-013):**
  1. In Vārāṇasī, the brahman Harisvāmin has a beautiful wife, Lāvaṇyavatī. As they sleep on a roof, a Vidyādhara prince carries her off through the air (12.20.3–9).
  2. Harisvāmin searches, laments, is kept from suicide by kin, gives away his wealth and wanders to holy places (10–28).
  3. Starving in the hot season, he comes to the brahman Padmanābha's house; the host's wife, pitying him, gives him a bowl of rice cooked in milk (29–40).
  4. He leaves it under a banyan to wash. A hawk carrying a black snake settles in the tree; poison drips from the dead snake into the bowl; he eats it (41–46).
  5. He staggers back, tells the wife her food has poisoned him, and dies. The host drives out his innocent wife, who goes to a holy place for penance (47–52).
  6. In the court of Dharmarāja (god of death and justice) the question of whose is the brahman-murder is left undecided (53). The abducted wife is not mentioned again.
- **Central characters and relationships:** Harisvāmin (victim); his wife (absent after the abduction); the host and his wife; the hawk and snake.
- **What makes it recognisable as this family:** an accidental poisoning with no guilty party, and a blame question.

## 2. The central dilemma

- **Betal asks:** whose is the sin of brahman-murder (*kasya sā brahmahatyā*), among the snake, the hawk and the food-givers? (KSS 12.20.53–54) [Primary]
- **Vikrama answers:** not the snake's (helpless, eaten by its enemy), not the hawk's (hungry, eating what it found), not the couple's (blameless, acting from virtue). The sin belongs to the dull-witted person who, **without reflection**, would say it belongs to any one of them (12.20.56–59). [Primary]
- **Reasoning:** every actor was innocent; guilt falls on the hasty judge. [Primary]
- **Across versions:** Ś 12 and Hindi XII give the same answer (N1) [Primary (OCR) / Translation]. Ś 12 has a snake living in the banyan with no hawk. The Hindi narrative was not checked for a hawk.
- **What the reader is really asked to judge:** the surface question is who is guilty. The deeper tension is that a death needs someone to blame, and the answer refuses every candidate and turns the question on the one asking it. N1 calls it a self-referential turn. [Inference]

## 5. Storytelling potential

- **Hook:** a wife abducted from a sleeping roof, then a quest abandoned because of a bowl of rice. [Inference]
- **Story engine:** a false opening (abduction, quest) cut off by an accident; a chain of innocent causes (N1).
- **Stakes:** a man's life; an innocent woman's disgrace.
- **Characters:** a lamenting husband; a pitying wife who is wrongly blamed; no villain.
- **Emotional experience:** pathos and irony; injustice that nobody intended.
- **Distinctive device or turn:** the dispute is left unsettled even in the court of Dharmarāja, so Vikrama is asked to decide what the god of justice did not; the answer turns on the judge. N1 notes the gods cannot settle it, and the answer's last clause points at anyone answering carelessly.
- **Is the dilemma genuinely interesting?** Yes. It is the one question in the batch with no culprit.
- **Can reflection emerge without a lecture?** Yes, naturally, since the reader will be tempted to blame someone and then meet the answer.
- **What could make a retelling difficult?** The abducted wife and the abduction plot are left unresolved (N1); the text does not say whether the hasty speaker belongs to the court; the ending reasoning could read as a lecture. Whether the owner wants an unresolved thread is an editorial choice.

## 7. Editorial and sensitivity considerations

- A woman is abducted and then dropped from the story; a wife is blamed and expelled for an innocent act (12.20.51–52) [Primary]. The narrative treats her expulsion as unjust, but her outcome is not told.
- The sin at issue is **brahman-murder**, a religious category (N9 would need to examine how it is explained).
- The Hindi's phrase "at the bidding of her husband" for the wife is a detail of Platts's translation.
