# TC-019: The thief's son: to whom should the funeral offering go?

> **Selection dossier, story bucket.** Evidence summary and qualitative assessment for the owner. Not a selection, ranking or adaptation. Provisional assessment: **Viable core contender**. Sources: [catalogue record](../../research/CANDIDATE_TALE_CATALOGUE.md), [N1 record](../../research/SOMADEVA_TALE_EVIDENCE.md), [comparison report](../../research/SOURCE_TRADITIONS_COMPARISON.md).

## 1. Identity and narrative summary

- **Working title:** The thief's son: to whom should the funeral offering go? (Tawney's title as a catalogue label, not a project title.)
- **Recorded in:** KSS 19 (12.26.1–102, Tawney ch. 93) · BKM 19 (provisional) · Ś 18 · J 16 · Hindi XVIII · Ryder — (omitted; Ryder's reason is unverified, so the absence means nothing about the tale).
- **Essential events** (KSS base, N1 TC-019; verses as N1 gives them):
  1. A king, Sūryaprabha of Vakrolaka, has no son (3–6). Far away, a merchant dies and kinsmen seize his wealth; his widow and daughter Dhanavatī flee at night (7–12).
  2. In the dark they brush against a thief impaled on a stake who is still alive. He offers 1,000 gold pieces for the girl as his wife, because a sonless man cannot reach the higher worlds. He says that any son she later bears by another man at his bidding will be his own, a *kṣetraja* ("born of his field", a son born to a man's wife by another man with the husband's authority). The greedy mother accepts and pours water into his hand to seal the gift of the bride. He tells them where the gold is buried, asks to be cremated and his bones put in a holy river, and dies (13–29).
  3. In Vakrolaka a young brahman, Manaḥsvāmin, longs for a courtesan who demands 500 dīnāras. Dhanavatī, remembering the thief's permission, hires him for one night for that sum (32–46).
  4. She bears a son. Following Śiva's instruction in dreams, the baby is left with 1,000 gold pieces at King Sūryaprabha's gate and the king takes him as a gift from Śiva (47–58). He is named Candraprabha and, grown, becomes king (59–62).
  5. He goes on pilgrimage to perform his (adoptive) father's rites in person and ends at Gayā (63–87). As he offers the funeral rice-ball (*piṇḍa*), three hands rise to take it: a thief's with an iron spike, a brahman's with a kuśa-grass ring, and a king's with a signet ring. No one can say whose it is (88–92).
- **Central characters and relationships:** Candraprabha (the offerer); three candidate "fathers": the impaled thief (legal husband of his mother), Manaḥsvāmin (begetter), King Sūryaprabha (foster father); Dhanavatī and her mother; Śiva, who arranges the adoption.
- **What makes it recognisable as this family:** the three hands rising at the funeral offering, and the question to whom the offering belongs.

## 2. The central dilemma

- **Betal asks:** Into whose hand should the offering be given? (KSS 12.26.94; N1 TC-019).
- **Vikrama answers:** The thief's (96, 101).
- **Reasoning:** The begetter sold himself for money for one night, so he is not counted as father (97). The king might be father by sacraments and rearing, but the gold left with the child was payment for that rearing (98–99). So the son belongs to the man who received the mother by the water-gift, whose permission let her conceive, and whose wealth it all was (100–101). [Primary: KSS e-text, AI reading]
- **Across versions:** Ś 18: same answer; the Vetāla objects that the boy came from the brahman's seed and the king's rearing, and the king replies that both were bought [Primary, Uhle via OCR, N1]. Hindi XVIII: same answer and reasoning; the hands rise from the river Phalgu instead of a well at Gayā [Translation, Platts, N1]. J 16: not documented beyond its number; the catalogue notes that J 16 is among three J riddle solutions Emeneau calls careless (Emeneau Intro. §6, p. xix, as cited in N3 §4) [Secondary].
- **What the reader is really asked to judge:** On the surface, which of three claimants is the father. Underneath, whether fatherhood is made by blood, by care or by a prior legal bond, and what follows when the other two claims were literally paid for. The hard part is that each claim is partly right. [Inference]

## 5. Storytelling potential [Inference throughout]

- **Hook:** A vivid, strange image, three hands rising from the water to claim one offering, preceded by a dying thief who "buys" a bride.
- **Story engine:** A two-generation chain of bargains (bride-gift, a night's hire, a left-behind child) that converges on one moment.
- **Stakes:** Personal and quiet: a man's standing as son and the peace of the dead. Little physical danger after the opening.
- **Characters:** A resourceful dying thief, a mother driven by greed, a devout king who travels for his father's rites. The characters are functional; the situation is the interest.
- **Emotional experience:** Wry, strange and reflective more than suspenseful. N1 reads it as legal rather than gripping until the marvel at Gayā.
- **Distinctive device:** The final tableau of three hands, and a riddle decided by payment and law.
- **Is the dilemma genuinely interesting?** Yes, if the question is made felt: each father did something for the boy, and each was also paid.
- **Can reflection emerge without a lecture?** Plausibly, through the three hands themselves, but the answer depends on *kṣetraja* doctrine that a reader will not know.
- **Retelling difficulty:** The legal logic must be understood without being explained at length. The middle sections include a long pilgrimage description (76–87 in KSS). The tale hinges on a sexual transaction (section 7).

## 6. Contribution to the collection

- **Offers:** The pool's only inheritance-and-paternity question, in the "allocation" form (to whom?), a form it shares with the "whose wife?" tales but with a son's rites at stake. Also a rare child-of-bargains plot.
- **Nearest overlaps:** None as a story. The "allocation" question form is shared with TC-002, 005, 006, 009 and 015 (N1 §4.1): a similar form, a different dilemma. TC-004 and TC-020 also feature parents and children, as a theme only.
- **Variety:** Adds a legal-ritual dilemma and a supernatural-marvel ending. Slightly risks repeating "legal category decides" reasoning (TC-002, 009, 015; N1 §4.2).

## 7. Editorial and sensitivity considerations

- **Sexual transaction and surrogate paternity.** A daughter is given in marriage by her mother, for gold, to a dying thief, and later pays a brahman for one night; the child is a legal "son of the field" (KSS 12.26.17–46) [Primary]. A retelling must decide how much of this to show and how to treat the mother's greed (22) and the woman's lack of choice. Source-backed; needs later examination (N9).
- **Bride given by her mother to a dying stranger** (12.26.22–23). Source-backed.
- **Caste and religion.** A brahman begetter who "sold himself" (97) and a thief as legal father touch caste expectations; Śiva's dreams and the *piṇḍa* rite carry Hindu ritual meaning. Source-backed; later examination.
- **Violence at the start.** An impaled but living thief (13–16). No verdicts here; none of this is a reason for exclusion by itself (D-006).

## 8. Selection assessment (provisional, for discussion)

**Provisional label:** **Viable core contender**

- **Strongest reason to include:** A distinct, memorable dilemma and image, with good KSS evidence and a stable answer across KSS, Ś and the Hindi.
- **Strongest reason to hesitate:** The answer depends on a legal doctrine and a sexual bargain that are hard to make intuitive and sensitive to handle, and the tale is more reflective than suspenseful.
- **What could change this assessment:** Reading Ś 18 and Hindi XVIII's narrative bodies (to see whether they present the bargains more vividly); a reader test of how well the three-hands scene works; an editorial judgement on the sexual material.
- **Confidence:** Moderate. Question, answer and reasoning are well recorded in KSS; the other versions are read at the Q&A level only; J is unread.
