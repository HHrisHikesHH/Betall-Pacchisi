# TC-027: An ogre devastates Arimaulimaṇi's kingdom

> **Selection dossier, story bucket.** Evidence summary and qualitative assessment for the owner. Not a selection, ranking or adaptation. Provisional assessment: **Viable core contender**. Sources: [catalogue record](../../research/CANDIDATE_TALE_CATALOGUE.md), [N3 §4 and §5.1](../../research/JAMBHALADATTA_ADDITIONAL_TALES_EVIDENCE.md).

## 1. Identity and narrative summary

- **Working title:** An ogre devastates Arimaulimaṇi's kingdom (Emeneau's title, "How an Ogre devastated Arimaulimaṇi's Kingdom"; a catalogue label, not a project title).
- **Recorded in:** KSS — · BKM — · Ś — · J **23** (Emeneau T pp. 132–138, Tr pp. 133–139; **P and V only**, H and O lack it) · Hindi — · Ryder —. Also in W. **Not in the Newari version** (its tale 23 is identified by Emeneau with KSS 14, though Jørgensen says it differs substantially, p. 214). The absence from one manuscript of one recension is not evidence the tale never existed there (N3 §5.4).
- **Essential events** (J base, N3 §4):
  1. In Ekacakravarti on the Narmadā the townspeople kill a great creature in the river and find a living girl in its belly: Śobhāvatī, daughter of a king who had once proposed her to King Arimaulimaṇi. He resolves to marry her (Tr pp. 133–135).
  2. A disguised brahman-ogre (*brahmarākṣasa*) warns that he and his father mean to eat her and that the king should not marry her. The king insults the ogre's father repeatedly and dares him. The ogre, Tālajaṅgha, gathers the ogres from Laṅkā and they eat every man they catch. His mother, the ogress Kumbhodarī, takes a courtesan's form and eats the men who visit her (Tr p. 135).
  3. She takes as lover Vidyādhara, a teacher's son, and, jealous when he stays with his wife, comes as his mother and then as a bee. She kills him in his sleep and her husband eats him (Tr p. 137).
  4. The king accuses her; she names herself and says the killings avenge his insult to her son. The king seeks peace and agrees to give the ogre one man a day (Tr p. 137).
  5. The lot falls on a woman with a son, who weeps. A foreign king, Mahābala, promises to kill the ogres and save her son; the king promises him "this kingdom" if he succeeds. Mahābala beats the ogre, makes him swear to do no violence in the city and releases him (Tr pp. 137–139).
  6. The king treats Mahābala with little regard. Angered, Mahābala mocks the king's name, releases the ogre from his oath and leaves; the ogres eat the citizens. The king flees with Śobhāvatī to Mahābala's country, where he cannot explain himself; Mahābala smiles, makes the ogre swear again, gives him troops and restores him (Tr p. 139).
- **Central characters and relationships:** King Arimaulimaṇi; Śobhāvatī; the ogre family (father Kharjūrajaṅgha, mother Kumbhodarī, son Tālajaṅgha); Vidyādhara and his wife; the weeping mother; Mahābala.
- **What makes it recognisable as this family:** a king's insult to an ogre, a city devoured, a daily human tribute and a rescuer who is slighted.

## 2. The central dilemma

- **Betal asks:** Who is the slayer of the citizens and of the brahman Vidyādhara: Arimaulimaṇi, Mahābala or the ogre Tālajaṅgha? (J 23, T p. 138; Tr p. 139). V reads the third candidate differently (*atha vā rākṣasajaṅghaḥ*, T p. 138 n. 38), so the name Tālajaṅgha rests on P alone.
- **Vikrama answers:** The killing of the citizens rests on King Arimaulimaṇi and on no one else (T p. 138; Tr p. 139). [Primary: J Sanskrit in Emeneau; one manuscript, P, plus V]
- **Reasoning:** **None given**, and the answer does not mention Vidyādhara (N3 §4).
- **Across versions:** Not in the Newari. W: names only, plus one reading about Vidyādhara (N3 §4). The narrative links the deaths to several ogres (Tālajaṅgha, his mother and father) and does not assign them to one alone; the Sanskrit attaches the citizens' deaths after Mahābala's release to "that ogre", the father (T p. 138). N3 records this with the witness variant and does not resolve it.
- **What the reader is really asked to judge:** On the surface, who is guilty among king, rescuer and ogre. Underneath, whether responsibility lies with the one who ate, the one who unleashed, or the one whose pride began the chain; with no reasoning given, the basis (provocation, tribute, ingratitude) is left to the reader. ## 5. Storytelling potential [Inference throughout]

- **Hook:** A girl found alive in a river creature's belly, followed by a king's reckless insult to an ogre.
- **Story engine:** A cascade: insult, ogre raids, an ogress's seductive killings, tribute, rescue, snub, release and restoration. Each step causes the next.
- **Stakes:** A city eaten; individual lives (a weeping mother's son); a king's throne.
- **Characters:** A proud, ungrateful king; a jealous shape-shifting ogress; a foreign king who rescues, retaliates and then forgives.
- **Emotional experience:** Horror and the uncanny (N3 calls it the darkest of the three), then a closing generosity.
- **Distinctive device:** An ogress who becomes a courtesan, then a mother, then a bee; a double rescue with a reversal in the middle.
- **Is the dilemma genuinely interesting?** Yes in outline: guilt for a chain of causes with no obvious answer. But the answer comes with no reasoning, so the interest lies in the reader's own judgement.
- **Can reflection emerge without a lecture?** Plausibly; the chain makes the question feel natural.
- **Retelling difficulty:** The tale is long and crowded, with many figures. The text is imperfect (P and V disagree on the question; careless grammar at the end). The answer has no stated reasoning and does not mention Vidyādhara, so a retelling would need to decide how the question and answer fit the narrative. Mass death and cannibalism need handling.

## 7. Editorial and sensitivity considerations

- **Violence and cannibalism:** ogres eat citizens; the ogress eats her lovers (Tr pp. 135–137). Source-backed; the main issue for tone and audience.
- **A courtesan figure who is a man-eater** (Tr p. 135). A sexualised monster; needs later examination (N9).
- **A brahman-ogre** (the ogre is called *brahmarākṣasa*, Tr p. 135); a brahman, Vidyādhara, among the dead. Caste and religion implications need later examination.
- **Daily human tribute decided by lot** (Tr p. 137). Source-backed.
- **Insult based on a name:** Mahābala's pun on the king's name (Tr p. 139 n. 17). Needs a specialist reading.
- None is a reason for exclusion by itself (D-006).

## 8. Selection assessment (provisional, for discussion)

**Provisional label:** **Viable core contender**

- **Strongest reason to include:** A large-scale, dramatic and distinct story whose blame question grows naturally from its causal chain and whose moral weight rests on pride, gratitude and responsibility.
- **Strongest reason to hesitate:** The thinnest evidence of the three J-only tales (one manuscript plus a poor print; no Newari parallel; the answer has no reasoning, and the question's third candidate varies by witness), and a scene overlap with TC-016.
- **What could change this assessment:** A Sanskritist's check of T p. 138; W's narrative; the owner's view of the TC-016 overlap; the owner's weighing of J-only tales with thinner witnesses.
- **Confidence:** Low. The tale was read in full, but the witness base is minimal and the question-and-answer text is unsettled.
