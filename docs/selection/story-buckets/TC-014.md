# TC-014: The merchant's daughter and the thief who wept and laughed

> **Selection dossier, story bucket.** Evidence summary and qualitative assessment for the owner. Not a selection, ranking or adaptation. Provisional assessment: **Viable core contender**. Sources: [catalogue record](../../research/CANDIDATE_TALE_CATALOGUE.md), [N1 record](../../research/SOMADEVA_TALE_EVIDENCE.md) (TC-014), [comparison report §8.2](../../research/SOURCE_TRADITIONS_COMPARISON.md).

## 1. Identity and narrative summary

- **Working title:** The merchant's daughter and the thief who wept and laughed (a catalogue label; "KSS 14" in report §8.2).
- **Recorded in:** KSS 14 (12.21.1–61, Tawney ch. 88) · BKM 14 (provisional) · Ś 13 · J — (absent from the Bengali text; present in W and the Newari version, per Emeneau) · Hindi XIII · Ryder 13. A tale missing from J's Bengali text is not thereby missing from the wider tradition; W and Newari have it. N3 records that Emeneau identifies Newari tale 23 with KSS 14, but that Jørgensen says this tale differs substantially in content from KSS 14 and Ś 13 (Jørgensen p. 214; N3 §5.1); the Newari tale itself was not read by the repository (N3 read only Newari 21 and 22).
- **Essential events (KSS, N1 TC-014):**
  1. In Ayodhyā, the merchant Ratnadatta's daughter Ratnavatī hates men and refuses every suitor, merchants and kings alike; her father gives way (12.21.3–10). The text gives no reason (N1).
  2. A band of thieves plunders the city nightly. King Vīraketu goes out alone, calls himself a thief, and is led to a thief's underground house; a slave-woman warns him; he returns with his army and besieges it (11–26).
  3. The thief fights superhumanly; the king disarms and captures him and orders him impaled (27–32).
  4. Ratnavatī sees him led to execution, falls in love, tells her father she has chosen him and will die with him. Her father offers all his wealth; the king refuses (33–39).
  5. She goes to the execution ground. On the stake the thief sees her and her kin, weeps, then laughs, and dies (40–43).
  6. She mounts the pyre; the god Bhairava offers a boon; she asks for sons for her sonless father, then for her husband's life and righteousness. The thief revives and becomes the king's commander, named Ekavīra, gives up theft and marries her (44–56).
- **Central characters and relationships:** Ratnavatī; the thief (named only after his revival); her father; the king; a slave-woman; Bhairava.
- **What makes it recognisable as this family:** a thief on the stake who weeps, then laughs, at the sight of a girl who will die for him.

## 2. The central dilemma

- **Betal asks:** on seeing the merchant's daughter come with her father, why did the thief weep, and why laugh? (KSS 12.21.57–58) [Primary]
- **Vikrama answers:** he wept because he could not repay the merchant, "my friend without cause", and laughed in wonder that the girl, passing over kings, loved him: "strange indeed is a woman's heart" (12.21.59–60). [Primary]
- **Reasoning:** the answer is a reading of the man's inner state. The reasons are given inside the answer. [Primary]
- **Across versions:** Ś 13: he wept at how to repay the one who laid everything before the king for his sake, and laughed at women's obstinacy, loving even at the moment of death. Hindi XIII: wept at what return he could make for her giving up all; laughed because she loved him at his death and God's ways are inscrutable. In both the benefactor wept over is the girl; in KSS it is the merchant (N1; report §8.2). The report rates the differences as Low. [Primary (OCR) / Translation]
- **What the reader is really asked to judge:** surface: what two opposite reactions mean. Deeper: a condemned man's inner life, divided between gratitude and astonishment, is inferred from outside. [Inference]

## 4. Common story spine and variant differences

**Common spine:** a thief on the stake weeps then laughs at the sight of the merchant's daughter (as established for question and answer in Ś and Hindi; their narrative bodies were not read). The KSS body (the king's disguise, the siege, the boon) is not confirmed in the other versions.

**Variants:**

## 5. Storytelling potential

- **Hook:** a king who passes himself off as a thief; a man-hating girl who falls for the thief about to die. [Inference]
- **Story engine:** a night chase and duel, then a bride's public resolve, then a boon that revives him (N1).
- **Stakes:** a public execution, a father's whole fortune, the girl's own life.
- **Characters:** Ratnavatī is vivid and decisive; the thief's feelings are given only in the answer (N1); the king is clever and unyielding.
- **Emotional experience:** adventure, then pathos and wonder.
- **Distinctive device or turn:** two contrasting reactions in sequence (weeping, then laughing), a form N1 records otherwise only for TC-023 and in a related way TC-012, TC-020.
- **Is the dilemma genuinely interesting?** Moderately; the answer is a plausible reading but low in dilemma tension. [Inference]
- **Can reflection emerge without a lecture?** Yes, the weep-and-laugh reading invites a retelling to let the thief's expression speak first.
- **What could make a retelling difficult?** The thief's inner life is blank until the answer; Ratnavatī's hatred of men is unexplained; the generalisation about women is in Vikrama's answer.

## 6. Contribution to the collection

- **Offers:** an adventure-romance with a disguised king and a bold heroine; a happy resolution.

## 7. Editorial and sensitivity considerations

- The answer states a gendered generalisation, "strange is a woman's heart" (KSS 12.21.60); Ś 13's laugh is at "women's obstinacy", followed by gnomic verses [Primary, OCR]; the Hindi adds a proverb [Translation]. Recorded, not a reason for exclusion (D-006).
- A public impalement and a bride's self-immolation resolve (the pyre) are sources' events; both are reversed by a divine boon [Primary].
- Needing later examination: the Hindi's "God's ways are inscrutable" (N9).
