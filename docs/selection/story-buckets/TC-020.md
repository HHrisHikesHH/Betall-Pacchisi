# TC-020: The brahman boy who laughed at his sacrifice

> **Selection dossier, story bucket.** Evidence summary and qualitative assessment for the owner. Not a selection, ranking or adaptation. Provisional assessment: **Viable core contender**. Sources: [catalogue record](../../research/CANDIDATE_TALE_CATALOGUE.md), [N1 record](../../research/SOMADEVA_TALE_EVIDENCE.md), [comparison report](../../research/SOURCE_TRADITIONS_COMPARISON.md).

## 1. Identity and narrative summary

- **Working title:** The brahman boy who laughed at his sacrifice (Tawney's title as a catalogue label, not a project title).
- **Recorded in:** KSS 20 (12.27.1–137, Tawney ch. 94) · BKM 20 (provisional) · Ś 19 · J 17 · Hindi XIX · Ryder 18.
- **Essential events** (KSS base, N1 TC-020):
  1. King Candrāvaloka of Citrakūṭa lacks only a suitable wife. Carried off by his horse while hunting, he meets Indīvaraprabhā, daughter of the sage Kaṇva and the apsaras (celestial nymph) Menakā. Kaṇva rebukes him for hunting; the king gives it up and is allowed to marry her (4–50).
  2. On the way home they sleep under a fig tree. At dawn the brahman-demon (*brahmarākṣasa*) Jvālāmukha, whose dwelling it is, threatens to tear out the king's heart. Terrified, the king offers a human victim instead. The demon demands a brahman boy of about seven, brave and discerning, who gives himself willingly for the king, held down by mother and father while the king himself strikes with a sword, within seven days (51–82).
  3. The king's minister Sumati has a golden statue of such a boy made and paraded with a proclamation of a reward of the statue and a hundred villages. A heroic seven-year-old volunteers. His parents first think him mad; he argues that the body is impure and perishable and only worth what merit it earns; they accept the statue and the villages (83–110).
  4. At the tree the boy rejoices; gods rain flowers. His mother holds his hands and his father his feet; as the king raises the sword, the boy laughs, and everyone, the demon included, stops in amazement (111–125). **The tale ends there; whether he is killed is not narrated in KSS.**
- **Central characters and relationships:** the boy; his consenting, rewarded parents; King Candrāvaloka, who must wield the sword; the minister Sumati; the demon Jvālāmukha; the queen Indīvaraprabhā, through whom the trespass came about.
- **What makes it recognisable as this family:** a child about to be sacrificed, held by his parents, laughs.

## 2. The central dilemma

- **Betal asks:** Why did the boy laugh at the very point of death? (KSS 12.27.127–128).
- **Vikrama answers:** A threatened creature cries first to its parents, then to the king, then to the deity present. For the boy all three were reversed: the parents held him for wealth, the king would kill him to save himself, the deity (the demon) would eat him (130–133). Seeing such delusion about the body, and knowing his own wish fulfilled, he laughed in wonder and joy (134–136). [Primary: KSS e-text, AI reading]
- **Across versions:** Ś 19: the parents give the middle son after quarrelling over which child; the answer's verses say protectors had become killers [Primary, Uhle via OCR; the passage read stops at the laugh]. Hindi XIX: the parents give the second son; he "first laughed, and then wept"; **the king beheads him**; a verse follows that woman is the source of misery; the question asks only why he laughed; the answer matches Ś [Translation, Platts, N1]. J 17: not documented beyond its number.
- **What the reader is really asked to judge:** On the surface, what a laugh means at that moment. Underneath, what a child sees when every protector has become a threat, and whether his joy is courage, understanding or something stranger. [Inference]

## 4. Common story spine and variant differences

**Common spine:** a child offered for a king's rescue, parents handing him over for reward, a laugh at the moment of sacrifice and an answer in terms of betrayed protection. Established for the question and answer in all three read versions and for the sacrifice scene in KSS, Ś and (in part) the Hindi.

## 5. Storytelling potential [Inference throughout]

- **Hook:** A child laughs while a sword is raised over him, and everyone freezes.
- **Story engine:** A rash promise to a demon forces a search for a victim; a proclamation, a golden statue and a boy who volunteers. The tale builds to a held breath.
- **Stakes:** High and immediate (a child's life), though the opening romance (a hunt, a hermitage) is long relative to the sacrifice.
- **Characters:** The boy is the interest: calm, arguing his parents round. The parents' greed and the king's self-saving are the dark mirror.
- **Emotional experience:** Dread and wonder at once; suspended violence; unease at the parents' consent.
- **Distinctive device:** The tale stops at its climax and the riddle supplies the meaning.
- **Is the dilemma genuinely interesting?** Yes; it asks for a reading of a child's mind in a moment that is both horror and release.
- **Can reflection emerge without a lecture?** Yes, naturally, because the question is about an image rather than a rule.
- **Retelling difficulty:** The outcome is untold in KSS and told in the Hindi; a retelling must handle this and a child's death. The romantic prelude is long and sits oddly against the sacrifice. The boy's argument about the body (110) is philosophical.

## 7. Editorial and sensitivity considerations

- **Child sacrifice with parental consent for money** (KSS 12.27.76–82, 123–125). Source-backed; a central issue for audience, tone and any age guidance.
- **Violence by the king against a child** if the Hindi ending is followed (Hindi XIX, Platts) [Translation].
- **Gendered line:** the Hindi verse that woman is the source of misery (N1; N1 §4.4 lists Hindi XIX among Ś and Hindi maxims) [Translation]. Not in KSS; needs later examination (N9).
- **Animals and hunting:** the sage's rebuke of the king for killing animals (35–50); minor.
- **Religion:** a willing sacrifice to a brahman-demon, with the child's reflections on the body and rebirth (111–122).
- None of this is a reason for exclusion by itself (D-006).

## 8. Selection assessment (provisional, for discussion)

**Provisional label:** **Viable core contender**

- **Strongest reason to include:** A gripping, singular situation and a dilemma that is about a human moment rather than a rule.
- **Strongest reason to hesitate:** It sits close to TC-004 in situation and to three other tales in question form, so the owner's reading of D-035 for the sacrifice and reaction-riddle clusters matters. Its ending also differs sharply between sources.
- **What could change this assessment:** The owner's judgement on those clusters; a Hindi specialist's reading of Hindi XIX's death and the "laughed then wept" order (N1 §5.3); reading J 17.
- **Confidence:** Moderate. KSS and the Ś and Hindi answers are well documented; J is unread and the Ś and Hindi readings are passage-level.
