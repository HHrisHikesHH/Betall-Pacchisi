## 1. Identity and narrative summary

- **Working title:** The old ascetic who enters a dead youth's body (catalogue label, not a project title).
- **Recorded in:** KSS 23 (12.30.1–48, Tawney ch. 97) · BKM 23 (provisional) · Ś 22 (Uhle text pp. 56–58) · J 20 · Hindi **XXII** (Platts pp. 165–167; Barker pp. 338–343) and **XXIV** (Platts pp. 175–176; Barker pp. 357–360) · Ryder 21. Match **P**.
- **Essential events** (KSS base, N1 TC-023; the Hindi tellings are summarised in section 4):
  1. In Śobhāvatī in Kaliṅga, the brahman Yajñasoma has one son, Devasoma, who dies of fever at sixteen. The parents cling to the body until elders remind them that even kings end on the pyre; the kin carry him to the cremation ground (4–20).
  2. There lives an aged Pāśupata yogī, Vāmaśiva. His conceited disciple refuses an errand, calls him a decrepit fool, throws down his staff and leaves (21–30).
  3. Smiling, the ascetic goes to the body, sees its youth and, weighed down by old age, decides to enter it. Apart, he weeps aloud and dances. By yogic power he leaves his own body and enters the boy's "from desire for youth" (31–34).
  4. The boy rises, yawning. The kin rejoice. The ascetic, inside him, falsely says Śiva restored his life on condition he take the Pāśupata vow alone at once, sends them home torn between joy and grief, throws his old body into a pit and leaves as a young yogī (35–41).
- **Central characters and relationships:** the old ascetic; the dead youth and his parents; the insolent disciple; mourning kin.
- **What makes it recognisable as this family:** an aged man who knows the art of entering another body takes a younger one, and the riddle asks about a weeping and a laughing (or dancing) reaction.

## 2. The central dilemma

- **Betal asks (KSS):** Why did the yogī, about to enter another's body, first weep and then dance? (12.30.43; *parapure vasan* may mean "in another's city" or "dwelling in another's body", uncertain per N2 §7).
- **Vikrama answers (KSS):** He wept at leaving the body that had grown old with him, been the means of his spiritual attainment, and been fondled by his parents; attachment to the body is hard to give up. He danced for joy at entering a new body and accomplishing more, "for to whom is youth not desirable?" (45–47).
- **Reasoning:** The answer pairs attachment to the old body with joy in the new. [Primary: KSS e-text, AI reading]
- **Across versions:** Ś 22: Viśvapura, an aged brahman; he weeps (the body his mother protected, his father raised, he enjoyed in youth) and laughs ("again I have a young body") [Primary, Uhle via OCR, N2 §8]. **Hindi XXII:** the brahman Nārāyan weeps from his mother's love, the delight of youth and attachment (*moh*) to the body, and laughs at succeeding in his art and entering a new body [Translation + Hindi page image, AI reading]. **Hindi XXIV:** the riddle's subject is the boy's **father**, who laughs, then weeps: he laughed at seeing the yogī enter his son's body and so learning the art, and wept thinking he too must one day leave his own body [same basis]. J 20: not documented.
- **What the reader is really asked to judge:** On the surface, why one person feels two opposite things at once. Underneath, how attachment to a body (one's own, or a son's) sits alongside fascination with escaping it. In KSS and XXII the riddle is about the one who acts; in XXIV it is about a witness's own mortality. [Inference]

## 4. Common story spine and variant differences

**Common spine:** an aged man who knows the art takes a younger body; a weeping and a joyful reaction; an answer pairing attachment to the old body with joy at the new. A dead boy at a cremation ground is established only for KSS 23 and Hindi XXIV; in Ś 22 and Hindi XXII no death is mentioned (N2 §9.4).

| Story element | KSS 23 | Ś 22 | Hindi XXII | Hindi XXIV | Why the difference matters |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Setting | Kaliṅga, Śobhāvatī | Viśvapura, King Vidagdha | Biswapur, King Bidagdha | Kaliṅga | Two settings track two Sanskrit-type tellings |
| Entrant | Aged Pāśupata yogī Vāmaśiva | Aged brahman | Aged brahman householder (Nārāyan) who later says he is a yogī | A yogī | Householder vs ascetic changes the irony |
| Was the youth dead? | Yes, with funeral | Not mentioned | Not stated | Yes, with funeral | Whether there is a bereavement scene |
| Motive | Desire for youth (*yauvanecchayā*) | Enjoyment (*bhogān*) | Enjoyment (*bhog*) | Easy devotion (*sukh se jog*) | Changes the entrant's character |
| Riddle subject | The ascetic | The yogī | The brahman | The boy's father | Whose mind is opened |
| Reaction | Weeps, then dances (before entering) | Weeps, laughs | Weeps, then laughs (before entering) | Father laughs, then weeps (after entry) | Order and who reacts |
| Deception / aftermath | False vow story; sends kin home; old body into pit | Announces he is a yogī; sermon; pilgrimage | Tells kin he is a yogī; long sermon; announces a pilgrimage | Boy rises saying "Ram, Krishn"; the father "became a *bairāg*" (one clause) | Bereaved deceived (KSS) vs. sermon (XXII) vs. quiet conversion (XXIV) |
| Disciple scene | Yes (21–30) | Not recorded | No | No | A comic interlude |

Notes from N2: Uhle's manuscript B and recension *f* carry the **renunciation** (*vairāgya*) for the revived youth, whereas Hindi XXIV gives it to the father; and the sermon's anti-lust verses in XXII look like stock Sanskrit satire on yogīs (§8.1). Whether XXII's sermon is meant ironically is unestablished (N2 U4). How the father "learns the science" in XXIV is unclear (N2 U3). Neither version is preferred here (D-034 allows combination; N2 §13).

## 5. Storytelling potential [Inference throughout]

- **Hook:** An old man at a funeral sees a dead boy and decides to take his body.
- **Story engine:** Grief and a cremation-ground scene turn into a deception: the "revived" boy is a stranger.
- **Stakes:** The family's grief and trust; the dead boy's identity. No physical danger.
- **Characters:** The ascetic is the draw: comic (the disciple) and uncanny; in XXIV the father's quick conversion; in XXII a householder who preaches against enjoyment after taking a body to enjoy.
- **Emotional experience:** Macabre irony (N1); mixed joy and grief; in XXII possible satire.
- **Distinctive device:** A two-sided reaction that the riddle asks us to explain; a false resurrection.
- **Is the dilemma genuinely interesting?** Moderately: the answer is psychological and gentle rather than a hard choice.
- **Can reflection emerge without a lecture?** Yes; XXIV's thought ("I too must leave my body") surfaces mortality without comment. XXII's sermon is itself a lecture.
- **Retelling difficulty:** KSS's ascetic, the Hindi tellings and Ś differ in who weeps and why; a retelling must choose a riddle subject. The tellings are short and thin (N2 §10). The father's conversion in XXIV is one clause. The deception of bereaved parents is bleak.

## 6. Contribution to the collection

- **Offers:** A cremation-ground resurrection that is a deception, and a riddle about an inner contradiction.
- **Nearest overlaps:** **TC-012, TC-014 and TC-020** are reaction riddles (weeping, laughing or a burst heart). Shared question form; each concerns a different event, so a shared device rather than a repeated plot [Inference]. TC-020 and TC-023 both turn on a laugh or dance at a scene of death (N1 §4.6). **TC-002** (revival) is a different motif. The plot's repetition within the Hindi was settled by the owner.
- **Variety:** Adds body-entering and an ironic resurrection; risks repeating the reaction-riddle form.
- **Comparison bucket:** reaction riddles ([COMPARISON_BUCKETS.md](https://github.com/HHrisHikesHH/Betall-Pacchisi/blob/58d00606394f38801e94e4c258c431b02457f7aa/docs/selection/COMPARISON_BUCKETS.md) group 3). The Hindi XXII/XXIV question is settled (one slot) and is not a comparison group.

## 7. Editorial and sensitivity considerations

- **Deception of bereaved parents** (KSS 12.30.37–40) and the use of a dead child's body (KSS; Hindi XXIV). Source-backed.
- **Religion and asceticism:** a Pāśupata ascetic; a false claim that Śiva restored life (37); Hindi XXII's sermon against lustful, well-fed "yogīs" (Barker p. 342; N2 §8.1 suggests stock satire). Source-backed; needs later examination for tone toward ascetics (N9).
- **Mortality and bodily attachment** as themes. Source-backed.
- No gendered or caste material is recorded for this tale in the evidence documents; a retelling's choice of who is the riddle subject has no stated link to the gender of the characters. Not a reason for exclusion by itself (D-006).

## 8. Selection assessment (provisional, for discussion)

**Provisional label:** **Viable core contender**

- **Strongest reason to include:** A memorable, eerie premise with a gentle, psychological riddle, and two recorded versions offering different emotional registers.
- **Strongest reason to hesitate:** The riddle form recurs in three other tales, and the Hindi tellings are short and thinly developed; the shifts in riddle subject mean a retelling must choose among versions.
- **What could change this assessment:** The owner's view of the reaction-riddle cluster; a specialist Hindi/Braj reading of XXIV (*sīkhkar*, *bairāg*) and of XXII's sermon (N2 §14 U1, U3, U4); reading J 20.
- **Confidence:** Low to moderate. KSS and both Hindi tellings are well read, but the Hindi reading is AI and the comparison itself rests on literary judgement (N2 §13).
