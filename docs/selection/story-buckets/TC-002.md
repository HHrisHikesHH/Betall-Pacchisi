## 1. Identity and narrative summary

- **Working title:** Three young brahmans who restored a dead girl to life (a catalogue label, Tawney's title).
- **Recorded in:** KSS 2 (12.9.1–42, Tawney ch. 76) · BKM 2 (provisional) · Ś 2 · J 2 · Hindi II · Ryder 2. All columns record it (match E). KSS closes with a narrator's maxim at 12.9.42.
- **Essential events** (N1 TC-002, KSS 12.9.5–35):
  1. At a brahman settlement on the Yamunā, the learned Agnisvāmin has a beautiful daughter, Mandāravatī. Three equally accomplished youths each ask for her and would sooner die than see her given to another; her father, fearing for them, gives her to none (5–11).
  2. She dies suddenly of a burning fever. The three cremate her. One makes a hut on the cremation ground, sleeping on her ashes; one carries her bones to the Ganges; one becomes a wandering ascetic (12–15).
  3. The ascetic is a guest in a village house where a child will not stop crying and the mistress, in anger, throws it into the fire. He is horrified; the householder revives the child from the ashes with a spell from a book (16–23).
  4. That night the ascetic steals the book and goes back to the cremation ground, where the bone-bearer has also just returned. He reads the spell over the ashes and Mandāravatī rises alive, radiant (24–32).
  5. The three, overcome with desire, quarrel: each claims her by his own contribution (33–35).
- **Central characters and relationships:** Mandāravatī (the contested woman; she does not speak or choose, N1); her father Agnisvāmin; three rival suitors; the householder of Vakrolaka and his wife (holders of the spell).
- **What makes it recognisable as this family:** a woman revived by one of three suitors who each did something for her, and a verdict that gives the claimants kinship roles.

## 2. The central dilemma

- **Betal asks:** whose wife does the girl properly become? (KSS 12.9.36).
- **Vikrama answers:** the reviver is her father; the one who took the bones to the Ganges is counted her son; the one who kept her ashes and practised austerity out of love is called her husband (12.9.38–40).
- **Reasoning:** each is classified by the relationship his act resembles: giving life, performing a son's rite, devoted watch (12.9.38–40).
- **Across versions:** Ś 2 [Primary, OCR; death, cremation and answer read]: she dies of a snakebite; one suitor enters the pyre and dies with her; the reviver restores both. The answer: reviver is father, the one who died with her is her *brother*, the one who stayed at the place is her husband. No bone-bearer and no son. Hindi II [Translation, Platts, ending]: same roles as KSS (hut-keeper husband, bone-preserver "in the place of her son", reviver "as it were her father"); how she dies in the Hindi was not checked. J: not recorded in the repository evidence.
- **What the reader is really asked to judge:** the surface question is who gets the bride; the deeper conflict is whether sacrifice, labour or devotion gives a claim, and whether "wife" is a role earned or a category assigned. [Inference]

## 5. Storytelling potential [Inference throughout]

- **Hook:** a woman wanted by three, dying before she can choose.
- **Story engine:** three parallel paths converging on the cremation ground.
- **Stakes:** a life restored; the quarrel afterwards turns revival into rivalry.
- **Characters:** the three suitors are distinguished mainly by their acts, not personalities; the stolen-book ascetic is the actor.
- **Emotional experience:** grief, horror (the child in the fire), wonder, then dark irony as desire resurfaces at once.
- **Distinctive device or turn:** a shocking episode that supplies the means of revival; a verdict that reclassifies the claimants.
- **Is the dilemma genuinely interesting?** Yes: a classification puzzle where every answer is arguable, different from a merit contest.
- **Can reflection emerge without a lecture?** Plausibly, through what the woman does not get to say.
- **What could make a retelling difficult?** She has no voice or choice; the child-burning must be handled; the three suitors' answer is also a statement about which men are excluded.
