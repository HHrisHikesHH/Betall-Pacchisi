# TC-016: The sacrifice of Jīmūtavāhana

> **Selection dossier, story bucket.** Evidence summary and qualitative assessment for the owner. Not a selection, ranking or adaptation. Provisional assessment: **Strong core contender**. Sources: [catalogue record](../../research/CANDIDATE_TALE_CATALOGUE.md), [N1 record](../../research/SOMADEVA_TALE_EVIDENCE.md) (TC-016), [comparison report §8.3](../../research/SOURCE_TRADITIONS_COMPARISON.md), [N3](../../research/JAMBHALADATTA_ADDITIONAL_TALES_EVIDENCE.md) §4 and §6.2.

## 1. Identity and narrative summary

- **Working title:** The sacrifice of Jīmūtavāhana (Tawney's title via Penzer; a catalogue label).
- **Recorded in:** KSS 16 (12.23.1–207, Tawney ch. 90; question and answer 12.23.202–206; the longest KSS tale by verse range) · BKM 16 (provisional) · Ś 15 (Uhle text p. 43) · J 24 (not checked by the repository; N3 saw it only in passing, Emeneau Tr p. 141) · Hindi XV (Barker p. 270) · Ryder 15. Catalogue match code **P**: the answer is the same in KSS, Ś and the Hindi, but the reasoning differs (report §8.3).
- **Essential events (KSS, N1 TC-016):**
  1. In the Himalayan city of Kāñcanapura, the Vidyādhara prince Jīmūtavāhana, who remembers former births and is "born of a portion of a Bodhisattva", is compassionate to all. He asks the family wishing-tree to rid the earth of poverty; it rains wealth (12.23.3–29).
  2. Envious kinsmen prepare war; he refuses to kill kin for a kingdom and leaves with his parents for the Malaya mountain (30–38).
  3. He befriends Mitrāvasu, falls in love with his sister Malayavatī, and they marry after a night of lovesickness and a heavenly voice's promise (39–93).
  4. On the shore he sees mountains of bones: by a pact with the serpent king Vāsuki, Garuḍa eats one Nāga a day, a feud going back to Kadrū's enslavement of Vinatā. He resolves to give his body for one (94–112).
  5. He hears the young Nāga Śaṅkhacūḍa and his old mother lament; the youth is to be eaten that day. Jīmūtavāhana offers himself; the mother refuses; Śaṅkhacūḍa refuses to save himself at another's cost and goes to worship Śiva (113–145).
  6. Jīmūtavāhana mounts the execution rock; Garuḍa seizes him and tears out his crest-jewel; the jewel falls before Malayavatī (146–158).
  7. Śaṅkhacūḍa returns, follows the blood, and cries out that he is the Nāga. Garuḍa is struck by remorse; Jīmūtavāhana dies of his wounds as his family arrives (159–180).
  8. Malayavatī reproaches Gaurī; the goddess revives him and makes him emperor of the Vidyādharas; Garuḍa offers a boon and the bones revive; they reign with Malayavatī, Mitrāvasu and Śaṅkhacūḍa (181–201).
- **Central characters and relationships:** Jīmūtavāhana; Śaṅkhacūḍa and his mother; Garuḍa; Malayavatī, Mitrāvasu; Jīmūtaketu and Kanakavatī (parents); the goddess Gaurī.
- **What makes it recognisable as this family:** two self-sacrificers, a daily tribute to Garuḍa, and the question of whose courage is greater.

## 2. The central dilemma

- **Betal asks:** which was greater in courage (*sattva*), Śaṅkhacūḍa or Jīmūtavāhana? (KSS 12.23.203) [Primary]
- **Vikrama answers:** **Śaṅkhacūḍa** (12.23.205–206). [Primary]
- **Reasoning:** Jīmūtavāhana's deed is no wonder, as it was "perfected over many births" (*bahujanmasiddham*); Śaṅkhacūḍa, though he had escaped death, ran after Garuḍa and offered his own body insistently (12.23.205–206). [Primary]
- **Across versions:** same answer in Ś 15 and Hindi XV; the *reasoning* differs.
  - Ś 15: the Vetāla objects that the self-sacrificer must be greater; the king says Jīmūtavāhana had *practised* giving his life birth after birth (*abhyāsa*), with a verse on habits carried from birth to birth (Uhle text p. 43) [Primary, OCR].
  - Hindi XV: the printed Hindi reads roughly "Jīmūtavāhana is a Kshatriya by caste; for him the practice (*abhyās*) of giving his life is ongoing". The idea of practice is kept but grounded in **caste**, not births (report §8.3). Platts: a Kshatriya "accustomed to holding his life in his hand". [Translation; AI reading of Barker p. 270, **not specialist**; N5 recommends a specialist.]
  - J 24: not documented.
- **What the reader is really asked to judge:** surface: whose courage is greater. Deeper: whether an act counts for more when it is against one's nature and circumstances than when it comes from long habit, and whether a pure act and a costly act are the same kind of courage. [Inference]

## 4. Common story spine and variant differences

**Common spine:** Jīmūtavāhana saves a Nāga by offering himself to Garuḍa; the question compares him to Śaṅkhacūḍa; the answer is Śaṅkhacūḍa. Established in KSS in full; in Ś and Hindi only for the question, answer and reasoning. J 24 is unchecked.

**Variants:**

| Story element | KSS | Ś 15 | Hindi XV | J / other | Why the difference matters |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Question | greater in courage (*sattva*) | which was *sattvādhika* | "whose virtue was greatest?" (Platts); *sat* glossed "virtue" (Barker) | not documented | the same word, possibly different weight |
| Answer | Śaṅkhacūḍa | Śaṅkhacūḍa | Śaṅkhacūḍa | not documented | stable |
| Basis of reasoning | many births | practice across births (with verse) | practice grounded in Kshatriya caste | not documented | changes what Jīmūtavāhana's act says about his character: earned over lives, or belonging to birth-status |
| Vetāla's challenge | none recorded | Vetāla objects the self-sacrificer must be greater | not recorded | not documented | adds an argument |

The report notes the Hindi "keeps the idea of practice" but changes what it rests on; it is not a simple shift from virtue to caste. No version is preferred here.

## 5. Storytelling potential

- **Hook:** a prince gives away wealth, then his own body, to feed a feud he did not start. [Inference]
- **Story engine:** a long, nearly complete romance (love, exile, wedding) that gradually moves to the beach of bones, then to the sacrifice (N1: "a complete romance and martyrdom narrative").
- **Stakes:** life and death of a Nāga and a prince; emotional stakes of parents and a mother.
- **Characters:** Jīmūtavāhana is a saintly figure; Śaṅkhacūḍa is a minor figure for most of the tale, yet the question is about him (N1).
- **Emotional experience:** high pathos and devotional tone; the narrator calls it "very noble and full of feeling" (12.23.202). A man eaten alive who rejoices; a mother's lament; a miraculous revival.
- **Distinctive device or turn:** the victim's joy turns the devourer's perception (12.23.164–166); the answer favours the secondary character.
- **Is the dilemma genuinely interesting?** Yes, and it has an unexpected answer. [Inference] But the key evidence for the answer (Śaṅkhacūḍa running back) comes late.
- **Can reflection emerge without a lecture?** Yes, the two acts invite comparison; the reasoning, however, is theological ("births") in KSS and so would need care.
- **What could make a retelling difficult?** The length (207 verses, the longest); a Buddhist and Śaiva religious blend; a minor character carrying the answer; the hero's romance with Malayavatī competes with the sacrifice.

## 6. Contribution to the collection

- **Offers:** a heroic-devotional tale with a Nāga-Garuḍa myth, a different register from the trickster and romance tales.
- **Nearest overlaps:**
  - **TC-027 (J 23):** a shared motif: a daily human tribute by pact and a weeping mother whose son's turn has come (N3 §6.2). The dilemmas differ (courage against blame). N3: in J, J 24 begins right after J 23 with a weeping serpent-mother.
  - **TC-020:** self-sacrifice of a child for a king; a motif of willing sacrifice, with a different question (why did the boy laugh) and a different story.
  - **TC-004:** self-sacrifice for a king in a king-and-servant argument (a similar dilemma form).
- **Variety:** adds a mythic register and a self-sacrifice question.
- **Comparison buckets:** daily tribute and weeping mother, with TC-027 ([COMPARISON_BUCKETS.md](../COMPARISON_BUCKETS.md) group 8: shared motif only). Its willing self-offering also sits near TC-004 and TC-020 (groups 2 and 4), as a theme rather than a shared plot.

## 7. Editorial and sensitivity considerations

- **Violence and sacrifice:** a man is eaten alive; mountains of bones; a revival by nectar (12.23.94, 146–153, 190–196) [Primary]. Not a reason for exclusion.
- **Religious material:** Buddhist (*bodhisattvāṃśa*) within a Śaiva frame (Gaurī, Gokarṇa) (N1) [Primary]; the Garuḍa-Nāga feud arises from Kadrū's enslavement of Vinatā (12.23.97–105).
- **Caste reasoning (Hindi XV):** the Hindi answer grounds the hero's practice in Kshatriya caste [Translation; Hindi AI reading, N5 specialist needed]. Needs later examination.
- **Gendered:** Malayavatī's attempted hanging (12.23.60–79) and a mother's lament are in the text; no generalisation about women is recorded.

## 8. Selection assessment (provisional, for discussion)

**Provisional label:** **Strong core contender**

- **Strongest reason to include:** a rich, emotionally powerful mythic tale with an unexpected answer and a distinct dilemma about courage.
- **Strongest reason to hesitate:** length and a long romantic lead-in; the reasoning differs across versions and the Hindi version needs a specialist; J 24 is unread.
- **What could change this assessment:** the owner's view of how much devotional, mythic register the collection wants, and of the TC-027 motif link and the sacrifice theme (TC-004, TC-020). A specialist reading of Hindi XV and a reading of J 24 (N4) would affect source choice more than the label.
- **Why "strong" despite the length:** the dilemma is distinct (no other candidate asks whether habitual goodness is less courageous than goodness against one's situation), the answer is stable across KSS, Ś and the Hindi, and its emotional range (love, a mother's lament, a man eaten alive who rejoices, the devourer's remorse) is the widest in the pool.
- **Confidence:** moderate. KSS fully read; Ś and Hindi partial; J unread.
