# TC-007: The king who married his dependant to a Nereid (Sattvaśīla)

> **Selection dossier, story bucket.** Evidence summary and qualitative assessment for the owner. Not a selection, ranking or adaptation. Provisional assessment: **Viable core contender**. Sources: [catalogue record](../../research/CANDIDATE_TALE_CATALOGUE.md), [N1 record](../../research/SOMADEVA_TALE_EVIDENCE.md) (N1 TC-007), [report §8.6](../../research/SOURCE_TRADITIONS_COMPARISON.md).

## 1. Identity and narrative summary

- **Working title:** The king who married his dependant to a Nereid (Sattvaśīla) (a catalogue label; "Nereid" is Tawney's word for the Daitya, or demon-race, princess).
- **Recorded in:** KSS 7 (12.14.1–115, Tawney ch. 81) · BKM 7 (provisional) · Ś 8 · J 7 (identified in the report as the Pracaṇḍasiṃha–Sattvaśīla story) · Hindi VIII · Ryder 7. Match E.
- **Essential events** (N1 TC-007, KSS 12.14.3–110):
  1. In Tāmraliptī, a king is served for years without reward by Sattvaśīla, a poor southern prince working as a humble attendant (3–9).
  2. Hunting, the king is lost in a forest; only Sattvaśīla has followed on foot. He gives the king two āmalaka fruits (a tart fruit), saying he has lived on such for ten years. The king, ashamed, rewards him but does not feel the debt is repaid (10–31).
  3. Sent by sea to ask for a princess's hand for the king, Sattvaśīla sees a golden banner rise from mid-ocean; a storm drives the ship toward it and it begins to sink. He leaps in, sword in hand, to find the cause (32–42).
  4. Underwater he finds a divine city and a goddess's temple, follows a maiden into a richer second city and falls in love. Her attendants send him to bathe; he surfaces in a tank in the king's garden, lovesick (43–63).
  5. He tells the king; the king sails to the same spot and leaps in. The maiden, a Daitya princess, is drawn to the king and invites him; he declines coolly, then visits her second city. She says she is the daughter of the slain Asura king Kālanemi, offers herself and her cities, and calls him "father" (64–102).
  6. The king gives her in marriage to Sattvaśīla, saying one fruit is repaid and he is still in debt for the other. He returns with a sword and an age-removing fruit; Sattvaśīla rules her cities (103–110).
- **Central characters and relationships:** the king (master); Sattvaśīla (servant); the Daitya princess; the goddess Kātyāyanī/Pārvatī (presiding deity of the city).
- **What makes it recognisable as this family:** a servant's leap into the sea, then the master's repeat of it to repay him, ending with the master giving away the undersea bride.

## 2. The central dilemma

- **Betal asks:** which of the two showed greater courage in leaping into the sea? (KSS 12.14.110).
- **Vikrama answers:** Sattvaśīla (12.14.112).
- **Reasoning:** he leapt without knowing the truth and without expectation; the king knew the truth and entered with expectation (12.14.113a). Verse 113b adds that the king did not desire the princess, thinking her not to be won by desire. N1 notes it is not explicit whether 113b counts against the king's courage or only explains why he gave her away.
- **Across versions:** all checked versions give the same answer (servant) with **different reasoning**; record each. Ś 8 [Primary, OCR]: the Vetāla objects that the king won the divine woman and gave her to the servant; the king replies that the one who first does a kindness is greater, with a verse that goodness to benefactors is no merit while goodness to those who harm is true goodness. Hindi VIII [Translation, Platts]: there is no special merit in conferring favours where that is one's office; the one who promotes another's interests while having his own is greater. Report §8.6 qualifies its "checked non-difference" to the answer only (corrected from N1). J 7: the report's §7 says the answer is the servant "in all four checked" but §8.6 lists three traditions, so J's answer is not independently documented (Cat. TC-007).
- **What the reader is really asked to judge:** in KSS the surface question is who was braver; the deeper conflict is whether courage lies in facing the unknown or in knowingly paying a debt. Ś and the Hindi shift it toward who is the greater benefactor. [Inference]

## 5. Storytelling potential [Inference throughout]

- **Hook:** a golden banner rising from the open sea and a servant who dives after it.
- **Story engine:** a debt of gratitude that the master can only repay by repeating the servant's risk.
- **Stakes:** a drowning ship, a lovesick servant, a king's life; the romance is resolved by renunciation.
- **Characters:** the loyal, silent servant; a king whose shame at his own neglect drives the plot; a princess who first seeks the king as husband and then calls him "father" (12.14.87, 102).
- **Emotional experience:** wonder, longing, shame and gratitude; a restrained, courteous ending.
- **Distinctive device or turn:** a double structure (leap and repeat) with the two fruits as a measure of debt (N1).
- **Is the dilemma genuinely interesting?** In KSS, moderately: it compares the same act with and without knowledge. Where the traditions give a different reason, the dilemma changes character.
- **Can reflection emerge without a lecture?** Yes, through the fruit-debt and the king's cool refusal, shown rather than stated.
- **What could make a retelling difficult?** Length and many beats; a first third (the forest hunt, the fruit) that is slow; an ambiguous verse (113b) bearing on the answer.

## 7. Editorial and sensitivity considerations

- The princess is given in marriage by the king to the servant; her stated wishes shift from the king as husband to "father" (KSS 12.14.87, 102–104). Source-backed; a handling matter. No gendered generalisation recorded. N9 examination pending.
- Asura and Daitya figures (a slain demon-king's daughter) and the goddess's role are mythological content. Not a reason for exclusion (D-006).
