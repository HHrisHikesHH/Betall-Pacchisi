# TC-025: Four brothers and the harlot

> **Selection dossier, story bucket.** Evidence summary and qualitative assessment for the owner. Not a selection, ranking or adaptation. Provisional assessment: **Viable core contender**. Sources: [catalogue record](../../research/CANDIDATE_TALE_CATALOGUE.md), [N3 §2 and §5.2](../../research/JAMBHALADATTA_ADDITIONAL_TALES_EVIDENCE.md).

## 1. Identity and narrative summary

- **Working title:** Four brothers and the harlot (Emeneau's title, "How the Four Brothers fared with the Harlot"; a catalogue label, not a project title).
- **Recorded in:** KSS — · BKM — · Ś — · J **21** (Emeneau T pp. 108–124, Tr pp. 109–125) · Hindi — · Ryder —. Also Newari 21. The dashes mean only that the examined texts lack it; they are not evidence that no other tradition has it (Cat. §1.1). Manuscripts for J 21: H, O, P and V; also W.
- **Essential events** (J base, N3 §2):
  1. A caravan merchant of Puṣkaravatī has four sons, each master of one art: music and dance, arms, "all the sciences", and political wisdom (*nīti*). After their father's death the brothers separate because their wives quarrel. Three divine jewels are held in common; the father ordered that the king decide who gets them (Tr pp. 109–111).
  2. The king sets a test: in Kusumapura the courtesan Rūpavatī takes a man for one night only; whoever stays with her two nights wins the jewels (Tr pp. 111–113).
  3. The eldest three try with music, valour and learning; each gets one night and is refused a second (Tr pp. 113–115).
  4. The youngest, Kanakadatta, reasons that wealth and valour failed. He learns that she visits a female ascetic, Puṇyaśarīrā, serves her for six months under a false identity, and asks her to find out why Rūpavatī keeps her rule. Hidden in a corner of the ascetic's house, he overhears Rūpavatī's story (Tr pp. 115–119).
  5. In a former life she was a minister's daughter, married to a prince who loved her but abandoned her in battle. Reborn as a cow-elephant, a doe and a *cakravāka* bird, she recognised him each time in an animal of the same kind, and each time he fled and left her. Born finally as a courtesan, she vowed never to take a man twice, concluding that men are cruel (Tr pp. 119–121).
  6. Kanakadatta tells every courtesan the same story from the man's side, as if he were the husband who abandoned her and now pines for her alone, and has it painted on a cloth. Rūpavatī hears of it, brings him home, weeps in public recognition and gives up her vow, believing she has regained her husband. He takes her and her wealth home and tells the king everything in secret; the king gives him the jewels and makes him minister (Tr pp. 121–125).
- **Central characters and relationships:** the four brothers (Kanakadatta the youngest); the king Vikramabāhu; the courtesan Rūpavatī; the ascetic Puṇyaśarīrā; Rūpavatī's past-life husband Pratāpasena (never present in the story).
- **What makes it recognisable as this family:** the four brothers' contest, a courtesan who remembers former births, and a trick that retells her story against her.

## 2. The central dilemma

- **Betal asks:** In every birth she knew her husband; how, remembering her former births, did she not recognise Kanakadatta for what he was, since his story succeeded? (J 21, T p. 124; Tr p. 125). A causal "why did she fail to recognise?" question.
- **Vikrama answers:** In each earlier birth she had union with her own husband, and by that merit she recognised him. Born into a courtesan's family she became "very wicked" (*pāpiṣṭhā*); through union with other men she forgot her husband although she still remembered her births (T p. 124; Tr p. 125). [Primary: J Sanskrit in Emeneau; Translation: Emeneau]
- **Reasoning:** The merit-and-sin explanation above; nothing further recorded.
- **Across versions:** Newari 21 (Jørgensen pp. 226–232, German): the answer is the same in substance (her failure is due to the sin of having become a courtesan) [Translation]. The question is phrased as why she did not recognise her husband in this existence. W: names only; its narrative is not known (N3 §1.3).
- **What the reader is really asked to judge:** On the surface, why a woman who recognised her husband in animal bodies is fooled now. N3 notes it is "strictly an explanatory puzzle about the plot, not a choice between claimants." Underneath, the answer ties her failure to her life as a courtesan; it is the reader who must decide whether that explanation is satisfying or whether the trick succeeded because she was lonely and grieving. [Inference]

## 5. Storytelling potential [Inference throughout]

- **Hook:** A courtesan who will not take the same man twice, and three jewels for the one who does.
- **Story engine:** A four-step contest in order of age in which the youngest wins by inquiry, then a rebirth romance that becomes the means of the trick.
- **Stakes:** An inheritance for the brothers; a heart's recognition for Rūpavatī. The deception's stakes fall on her.
- **Characters:** The youngest brother is a patient schemer; Rūpavatī is a woman with a long memory and a grievance; the ascetic is a trusted teacher whose confidence is used.
- **Emotional experience:** Pathos (four abandonments) inside a trickster comedy; a recognition scene the reader knows to be false. N3: "a melancholy rebirth romance that becomes the instrument of the trick".
- **Distinctive device:** A story overheard and then retold falsely; a chain of animal rebirths.
- **Is the dilemma genuinely interesting?** The question is unusual (a failure of memory), though not a choice between options.
- **Can reflection emerge without a lecture?** Yes, through the contrast between her long fidelity to a memory and her trust in a stranger.
- **Retelling difficulty:** It is the longest tale in Emeneau's edition (N3 §2, no inference about quality). It has two stories in one (the contest and the rebirths). The answer is moralising about courtesans. The reader must accept a trick on a woman's grief.

## 6. Contribution to the collection

- **Offers:** The only candidate with a courtesan at its centre telling her own story; a chain of rebirths unique in the pool; a woman's voice against male abandonment (N3 §6.4).
- **Nearest overlaps:** **TC-022**: "four brothers" with different skills is a shared opening device only (TC-008 has a related device, three brothers each fastidious in one domain, but it is not a four-brothers tale); here the skills set up a failed contest and the riddle concerns the courtesan (N3 §6.2). **TC-018**: a similar causal question form ("why did it fail?"), a different dilemma. Shared motif, not a duplicate.
- **Variety:** Adds a rebirth romance, a trickster plot and a female narrator within a tale.
- **Comparison buckets:** [COMPARISON_BUCKETS.md](../COMPARISON_BUCKETS.md) group 11 (four-brothers opening, with TC-022) and group 14 (causal "why did it fail?" question, with TC-018). Both are shared device or form only.

## 7. Editorial and sensitivity considerations

- **Moralising about a courtesan:** Vikrama's answer calls her "very wicked" and blames her union with other men (T p. 124) [Primary]. Source-backed; needs later examination (N9).
- **Gendered content:** Rūpavatī's own verdict that men are cruel and loveless (Tr p. 121); a woman's grief used as a lever for deception (Tr pp. 121–123). Source-backed.
- **Deception for sexual and economic gain:** Kanakadatta passes as her husband; the text does not say whether she ever learns the truth (N3 §2).
- **Death and abandonment across four lives** (Tr pp. 119–121). Source-backed.
- None is a reason for exclusion by itself (D-006).

## 8. Selection assessment (provisional, for discussion)

**Provisional label:** **Viable core contender**

- **Strongest reason to include:** A rich, original tale with a strong engine, an unusual question and material found nowhere else in the pool; its manuscript base is the best of the three J-only tales.
- **Strongest reason to hesitate:** It is J-only (though with a Newari parallel), the answer is tied to moralising about courtesans, and it is the longest story with a dilemma that is explanatory rather than a choice.
- **What could change this assessment:** A specialist check of T p. 124; W's narrative; the owner's view on admitting J-only tales and on the courtesan material.
- **Confidence:** Low to moderate. The tale was read in full in Emeneau, but through one edition by an AI reader, and there is no specialist check.
