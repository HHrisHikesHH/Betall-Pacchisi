# TC-003: The king and the two wise birds: are men or women worse?

> **Selection dossier, story bucket.** Evidence summary and qualitative assessment for the owner. Not a selection, ranking or adaptation. Provisional assessment: **Viable core contender**. Sources: [catalogue record](../../research/CANDIDATE_TALE_CATALOGUE.md), [N1 record](../../research/SOMADEVA_TALE_EVIDENCE.md) (N1 TC-003).

## 1. Identity and narrative summary

- **Working title:** The king and the two wise birds: are men or women worse? (the report also calls it the parrot-and-mynah tale).
- **Recorded in:** KSS 3 (12.10.1–95, Tawney ch. 77) · BKM 3 (provisional) · Ś 3 · J 3 · Hindi IV (the Hindi swaps order with TC-004) · Ryder 3. Match E.
- **Essential events** (N1 TC-003, KSS 12.10.4–91):
  1. **Outer tale.** In Pāṭaliputra a prince owns a learned parrot, born on earth through a curse; on its advice he marries a Magadha princess who owns an equally learned mynah. The parrot proposes union to the mynah; she refuses because men are wicked and ungrateful; he retorts that women are wicked. They wager (the loser becomes the other's slave or wife) and bring the dispute to the prince (4–15).
  2. **The mynah's tale (men).** A merchant's son, Dhanadatta, squanders his inheritance and is taken in by a merchant, who gives him his daughter Ratnāvalī. He lures her and an old woman into a forest, steals her jewels and throws both into a pit. She survives, returns to her parents with a cover story, and later, when he returns for more money, protects him with it. One night he kills her in his lap and flees (15–46).
  3. **The parrot's tale (women).** Vasudattā, a merchant's daughter, takes a lover. She slips out at night to meet him and finds him hanged as a suspected thief. A Vetāla (a spirit entering corpses) in the body bites off her nose as she kisses it. She blames her husband; he is bound and silent. A thief who witnessed everything tells the king, who frees the husband, cuts off her ears and banishes her (47–88).
  4. **Close.** The birds are freed from their curse as celestial beings; the dispute is left undecided in the court (89–91).
- **Central characters and relationships:** two cursed celestials in bird form; the prince and princess who own them; in the mynah's tale a husband and wife (and her parents); in the parrot's tale an unfaithful wife, her husband, her lover, a thief-witness and the king.
- **What makes it recognisable as this family:** a debate between two birds, each told through a tale of betrayal in marriage, with the sexes' worth as the question.

## 2. The central dilemma

- **Betal asks:** are men wicked, or are women? (KSS 12.10.92).
- **Vikrama answers:** women. A man "of that kind" may occasionally be found, but women are generally, everywhere and always so (12.10.93–94).
- **Reasoning:** none beyond the generalisation (N1: "No further reasoning is given").
- **Across versions:** Ś 3 [Primary, OCR]: same answer; the outer pair is named differently (King Rūpasena with the parrot; a Magadha princess with the mynah Madanamañjarī); a verse that the greater sin lies in women and a man is rarely sinful. Hindi IV [Translation, Platts]: same answer, with an explicit reason: a depraved man keeps some sense of right and wrong, a woman gives it no thought. J: not recorded in the repository evidence.
- **What the reader is really asked to judge:** the surface question is a generalisation about two sexes; the two embedded tales are individual cases of cruelty, one by a husband and one by a wife, so the verdict does not follow from them. [Inference]

## 5. Storytelling potential [Inference throughout]

- **Hook:** two talking birds, formerly celestials, quarrelling about love.
- **Story engine:** a wager, then two cautionary tales told in rivalry.
- **Stakes:** the birds' wager is light; the embedded tales carry attempted murder, murder, a corpse and mutilation.
- **Characters:** the birds' banter is a potential strength; the human characters in the embedded tales are mostly types.
- **Emotional experience:** macabre and darkly comic; dread in the pit and the cremation scene, grim irony in the verdicts.
- **Distinctive device or turn:** a frame within the frame and a Vetāla (a spirit) entering a corpse within an embedded tale, echoing the outer cycle.
- **Is the dilemma genuinely interesting?** Weakly as written: the answer is a generalisation, not a verdict on the cases. It becomes interesting if the reader weighs the two tales against the verdict. [Inference]
- **Can reflection emerge without a lecture?** Possibly, by leaving the question open; this departs from the recorded answer.
- **What could make a retelling difficult?** The answer's gendered claim (see §7); two long embedded tales that can swamp the outer riddle; a silent husband whose silence is unexplained (N1, 12.10.78).

## 7. Editorial and sensitivity considerations

- **Gendered generalisation (D-006).** The answer at KSS 12.10.93–94 generalises about women as a class; the thief's remark at 12.10.72 and the closing line of the parrot's tale (12.10.88) repeat it; the Hindi gives an explicit reason (Hindi IV, Platts). Source-backed. Report §3 item 10 singles it out for reconciliation with D-006; N9 examination pending.
- Retelling implications, neutrally: the recorded verdict can be kept as a character's judgement, shown as contested, or left open. Each changes what the tale says; the choice is editorial and not made here. The first embedded tale (a man's cruelty to his wife) is equally part of the record, so the two sexes' cases are both present.
- Violence: murder, mutilation, a hanged corpse (KSS 12.10.28, 45, 65–70, 87). Source-backed. Not a reason for exclusion by itself.

## 8. Selection assessment (provisional, for discussion)

**Provisional label:** **Viable core contender**

- **Strongest reason to include:** a distinctive frame-in-frame structure and a macabre, funny tone unlike most of the pool.
- **Strongest reason to hesitate:** the verdict is a bare gendered generalisation with no reasoning in KSS, which makes the dilemma thin unless reworked, and the length of two nested tales.
- **What could change this assessment:** N9 examination of the gendered material; reading of J 3 and of the Ś/Hindi bodies; the owner's view on keeping or reframing the verdict.
- **Confidence:** moderate. The text is clear; the editorial question is open.
