# TC-001: The prince who won a wife with his minister's son's help (Padmāvatī)

## 1. Identity and narrative summary

- **Working title:** The prince who won a wife with his minister's son's help (Padmāvatī). A catalogue label (Tawney's title), not a project title.
- **Recorded in:** KSS 1 (12.8.59–196, Tawney ch. 75) · BKM 1 (provisional) · Ś 1 · J 1 · Hindi I · Ryder 1. All six columns record it (match E, Cat. §2.1). Ryder's first tale corresponds closely to KSS 12.8.59–60 (Cat. TC-001). It is the cycle's opener in KSS.
- **Essential events** (N1 TC-001, KSS 12.8.59–196):
  1. Prince Vajramukuṭa of Vārāṇasī, hunting with his friend Buddhiśarīra (the minister's son), meets a girl of divine beauty at a forest lake. She communicates only by silent signs (a lotus at the ear, a gesture with the teeth, and so on) and leaves; the prince cannot read them (59–76).
  2. Lovesick, the prince despairs; the minister's son decodes the signs: she is Padmāvatī, daughter of an ivory-carver in King Karṇotpala's Kaliṅga (77–86).
  3. The two travel there and lodge with her old nurse, who carries messages. Padmāvatī answers with two apparent "rebukes" and a rope-seat lowered from a window; the minister's son reads each as an instruction (a delay of ten, then three, nights; the route in). The prince enters; they marry by the *gāndharva* rite (informal union by mutual consent) and live in secret (87–131).
  4. Padmāvatī, learning the minister's son did the decoding, sends him food; he feeds some to a dog, which dies. He tells the prince she tried to poison him so as to have the prince to herself (132–149). Her intent is reported only through the dog's death and his explanation (N1 TC-001, uncertainty).
  5. When a cry is heard that a young son of the king has died (152), the minister's son devises a ruse: the prince brands her thigh with a trident while she is drunk and takes her jewels; disguised as an ascetic and pupil, they sell her necklace, are arrested, and the "ascetic" tells the city magistrate a false story that a *yoginī* circle in the cremation ground fed a prince's heart to Bhairava and that he marked one of them (153–177).
  6. The king, finding the brand and the necklace, decides she is the *ḍākinī* (a flesh-eating witch) who devoured his son, and banishes her. The two take her away to their own kingdom (178–184).
  7. Her parents, believing her eaten by beasts, die of grief (185).
- **Central characters and relationships:** the prince and his adviser-friend; Padmāvatī (secret wife); the nurse (paid go-between); King Karṇotpala (ruler and judge); Padmāvatī's parents (the victims).
- **What makes it recognisable as this family:** a prince's courtship carried by coded signs and a scheming companion, ending in a false witch-accusation and the parents' deaths, with the king judged at the end.

## 2. The central dilemma

- **Betal asks:** of the couple's deaths, what sin falls to the minister's son, the prince or Padmāvatī (KSS 12.8.186–187).
- **Vikrama answers:** none of the three; the sin is King Karṇotpala's (12.8.190). Betal objects that the three did the deed, as crows are not blamed when geese eat the rice (191).
- **Reasoning:** the minister's son served his master (no sin); the lovers, "burned by love's arrows", are not to be judged; the king did not investigate his subjects or read rogues' signs, and acted without inquiry (12.8.192–195).
- **Across versions:** Ś 1 [Primary, OCR]: same answer, the king's sin "through lack of inquiry", reasoning compressed. Hindi I [Translation, Platts]: the question names four parties (including the kotwal, the city police chief) and the penalty is hell; same answer, the king, for expelling her without thought; the kotwal "obeyed orders". J: not recorded in the repository evidence as to answer. Ryder: contents only.
- **What the reader is really asked to judge:** whether guilt follows the one who planned and deceived, or the one with authority who judged on a false story without checking. [Inference] The difficulty is that the deceivers also did the visible harm.

## 5. Storytelling potential [Inference throughout]

- **Hook:** a glimpse of a girl who speaks only in signs, and a prince who cannot read them.
- **Story engine:** decoding. Each sign leads to a plan, then the contest between Padmāvatī and the adviser, then a deliberate false story.
- **Stakes:** personal and compelling for the lovers; the parents' deaths are given in one verse at the end, so their weight depends on the teller.
- **Characters:** the adviser is both indispensable and sinister; Padmāvatī is called *dhūrtā*, "crafty" (12.8.134), and is a participant, not a prize.
- **Emotional experience:** cleverness and intrigue, a thrill of the cipher, then unease at the branding and the banishment.
- **Distinctive device or turn:** a false cremation-ground story that works because the king believes it; N1 records the false story as an embedded tale.
- **Is the dilemma genuinely interesting?** Yes: the answer cuts against the easy target and Betal's crow-and-goose objection is memorable.
- **Can reflection emerge without a lecture?** Plausibly, through the reader's own sense of who was wronged.
- **What could make a retelling difficult?** The poison episode is reported only through the dog's death and the adviser's explanation (N1: Padmāvatī's intent unconfirmed); the king's dead son is not otherwise introduced (12.8.152); a woman is drugged and branded.

## 7. Editorial and sensitivity considerations

- A woman is made drunk, branded and publicly denounced as a witch by deception (KSS 12.8.153–181). The text presents it as a clever plan; a retelling must decide how to frame it. Source-backed; later examination needed (N9).
- Witch-fear and a ruler's credulity are central, not incidental. Padmāvatī's poisoning attempt (146–149) is ambiguous in intent. No gendered generalisation is recorded in this tale. Not a reason for exclusion (D-006).

- **Confidence:** moderate. KSS read in full, by an AI and not a Sanskritist; other versions by question and answer only.
