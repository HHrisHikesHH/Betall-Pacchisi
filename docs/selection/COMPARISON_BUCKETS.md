# Comparison Buckets: Possible Overlaps Among the 27 Candidates

> **Selection dossier.** An analysis of candidates that might compete for the same place in the collection. **These are not selection decisions.** The owner decides which 25 tales form the collection (D-022, Q-411) and how D-035 applies. Each conclusion below is an analyst's reading of the existing evidence, offered for discussion. Compiled 2026-11-18 by an AI session (Claude Code); not independently reviewed.

## How to read this document

D-035 says a repeated underlying plot is **not automatically a separate tale**. It may count separately only if it has a **genuinely different central dilemma** or is **substantially different as a story**. The collection also needs **meaningful variety**.

Each group below tests a suspected overlap against the **actual events and dilemmas** recorded in the evidence, not against titles. The groups are hypotheses, not presumed duplicates. Every group ends with one of four conclusions:

| Conclusion | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Same underlying story / likely one family** | The stories repeat one plot, and neither D-035 route (different dilemma, substantially different story) is clearly met. |
| **Related stories, but distinct central dilemmas** | The stories share real narrative material, but they ask different questions. Under D-035 both could hold separate slots; the owner may still judge the collection better with only one. |
| **Shared motif or device only** | A figure, object, opening or device recurs, but the plots and dilemmas are different. Not a D-035 question. |
| **Insufficient evidence** | The evidence cannot settle it. |

A "distinct" conclusion is not a recommendation to include both stories. A "related" conclusion is not a recommendation to drop one. Variety is a judgement about the collection as a whole, made in [SELECTION_OVERVIEW.md](SELECTION_OVERVIEW.md).

**Sources.** Unless stated otherwise, event and question/answer details come from the N1 records in [SOMADEVA_TALE_EVIDENCE.md](../research/SOMADEVA_TALE_EVIDENCE.md) §3 (cited as "N1 TC-0NN", with KSS verses), from N3 in [JAMBHALADATTA_ADDITIONAL_TALES_EVIDENCE.md](../research/JAMBHALADATTA_ADDITIONAL_TALES_EVIDENCE.md) ("N3 §n"), and from N2 in [HINDI_XXII_XXIV_COMPARISON.md](../research/HINDI_XXII_XXIV_COMPARISON.md). The candidate map is the [catalogue](../research/CANDIDATE_TALE_CATALOGUE.md). All readings were made by AI models, not specialists.

## 1. TC-005 and TC-009: multiple suitors (with TC-002)

**What they share.** A marriageable woman; several suitors defined by special abilities; a father who must decide; Vikrama asked "to whom should she be given?" (KSS 12.12.46; 12.16.33). Both are allocation questions. TC-002 is a third allocation tale with three claimants (N1 §4.6 item 4).

**How the events and characters differ.**

| | TC-005 (Somaprabhā) | TC-009 (Anaṅgarati) | TC-002 (Mandāravatī) |
|---|---|---|---|
| The woman | Sets the condition (a hero, a seer or a craftsman); abducted by a Rākṣasa | Sets the condition (a handsome man with a unique skill); nothing happens to her | Does not speak or choose; dies and is revived |
| How the conflict arises | Three relatives each promise her separately to a different suitor (12.12.11–27) | Four suitors present themselves; the king hesitates (12.16.19–31) | Her father refuses all three; she dies (12.9.8–12) |
| Action | A joint rescue: seer locates her, craftsman builds a flying chariot, hero kills the Rākṣasa (12.12.30–38) | **None.** The tale ends at the moment of choice | Three devoted paths converge; the dead woman is revived by a stolen spell (12.9.13–32) |
| Answer | The hero, who risked his life; the others were instruments (12.12.48–49) | The Kṣatriya swordsman, of her own caste; the others are ruled out by caste or uselessness (12.16.35–38) | The vigil-keeper is husband; reviver = father; bone-bearer = son (12.9.38–40) |
| Length | 50 verses | 39 verses | 42 verses |

**What each asks Betal to judge.** TC-005: which contribution to a shared rescue earns the right to marry, and so whether risk of life outweighs knowledge and craft. TC-009: which of four gifted men is a fit husband, and the answer applies caste rules (*svakarma*). TC-002: what each devoted act makes a man in relation to the woman, a classification puzzle.

**Same or different dilemma.** **Different.** The surface question is shared, but each answer rests on a different principle: risk against service (005), caste and usefulness (009), kinship by function (002) (N1 §4.2). In the Ś and Hindi versions of TC-009, the suitor who revives the dead is absent, so TC-009's overlap with TC-002's revival theme is a KSS feature only (N1 TC-009).

**Experience.** TC-005 is an adventure with a monster and a flying chariot. TC-009 is static: a parade of self-presentations and a verdict (N1 TC-009, "short and static"). TC-002 moves from grief through horror (a child thrown into the fire) to wonder and an awkward quarrel.

**Could both hold separate slots under D-035?** Yes. Neither repeats the other's plot, and the dilemmas rest on different grounds. **The real issue is variety and handling:** three "whose wife?" or "to whom should she go?" questions (with TC-015 a fourth, see group 7) give the collection several allocation riddles in which the woman is the prize. TC-009's caste-based answer is the most difficult to retell without either endorsing or quietly erasing it (D-006; N9).

**Uncertain.** J's answer for TC-009 is given in report §7 without a documented reading (catalogue §1.4). Ś 7's third suitor rests on diacritic-less OCR (*śāstra*/*śastra*, N1 TC-009).

**Conclusion: Related stories, but distinct central dilemmas.** If the owner wants fewer suitor riddles, TC-009 is the thinnest as narrative and TC-005 the most eventful; that is a variety judgement, not a D-035 finding.

## 2. TC-004 and TC-017: king, subordinate, loyalty and duty (with TC-007)

**What they share.** A king and a devoted subordinate; an extreme act of self-giving by the subordinate; a question asking whose act was greater; the Vetāla **challenges** Vikrama's first answer and Vikrama justifies it (12.11.124–131; 12.24.49–60). In both, **the answer is the king, by the same argument:** a servant's (and his family's) vow is to give life for the master, so the servant only did his duty; the king acted against what kings usually do (N1 §4.2, §4.6 item 2). TC-007 asks the same kind of question (servant against king) but answers it differently.

**How the events and characters differ.**

| | TC-004 (Vīravara) | TC-017 (Unmādinī) | TC-007 (Sattvaśīla) |
|---|---|---|---|
| The subordinate's act | Sacrifices his son to the goddess to save the king's life; his daughter dies of grief, his wife enters the fire, he beheads himself (12.11.55–93) | Offers his own wife to the lovesick king; enters the fire when the king dies (12.24.35–45) | Leaps into the sea not knowing what lies below, in the king's service (12.14.39–41) |
| The king's act | Offers his own head to the goddess for the servant; all are revived (12.11.94–103) | Refuses the offered wife as against dharma and dies of lovesickness (12.24.38–44) | Makes the same leap, knowing what lies below, to repay the servant (12.14.64–74) |
| Ending | Miraculous restoration and reward | Tragedy: both die (Ś, Hindi: Unmādinī too) | Marvel and marriage: the servant weds the Daitya princess |
| What is sacrificed | Lives (a child's, a family's) | Desire, then life | Safety (a leap) |
| Answer | The king (12.11.123) | The king (12.24.48) | The servant (12.14.112) |
| What the answer rests on | Role expectation | Role expectation; extended verses on kings' vices (12.24.54–58) | Knowledge and expectation at the moment of risk |

**What each asks Betal to judge.** TC-004: whose courage is greatest when a whole family dies for a king and the king then offers himself. TC-017: who is greater in *sattva* (rendered in N1 as "courage"; "self-command" is this dossier's gloss), a servant who gives up his wife and life, or a king who dies rather than take her. TC-007: which leap took more courage, the blind one or the informed one.

**Same or different dilemma.** On the surface the same question type; at the level of the story, **different**. TC-004 is about devotion to the point of death and divine reversal; TC-017 is about restraint against desire, and the king's refusal is the moral event; TC-007 is about gratitude and debt. **But TC-004 and TC-017 resolve their questions with the same argument**, so a reader meeting both would hear Vikrama make the same case twice.

**Experience.** TC-004 is intense pathos with a miraculous release (and the hidden king watching every act). TC-017 is a slow tragedy of desire refused, with a deception at its root (the brahmans' false report). TC-007 is a sea-marvel romance.

**Could both hold separate slots under D-035?** Yes: the plots, stakes and endings are different, and D-035's "substantially different as a story" route is met. **The overlap is in the answer**, which matters for variety rather than for the D-035 count. D-034 allows a retelling to vary emphasis, but the answer itself is the tale's essential identity, so the repetition cannot simply be written away.

**Uncertain.** How strongly the shared argument would read as repetition depends on telling and placement. The Ś and Hindi TC-017 adds Unmādinī's sati and a three-way comparison (N1 TC-017), which changes both ending and question in those versions.

**Conclusion: Related stories, but distinct central dilemmas**; the pool's clearest repetition of **reasoning**. If both are chosen, the owner should know the Vetāla's challenge and Vikrama's reply will run on the same lines.

## 3. TC-012, TC-014, TC-020 and TC-023: reaction riddles

**What they share.** The question form "why did X react so?", which asks Vikrama to read a hidden inner state from a visible reaction (N1 §4.1). Two of them (TC-014 and TC-020) put the reaction at the moment of death; TC-020 and TC-023 put it at a scene of death (N1 §4.6 item 1). TC-018 (why was the science lost?) and TC-025 (why did she fail to recognise him?) ask causal questions close to this form (N1 §4.1; N3 §6.1; see group 14).

**How the events and characters differ.**

| | TC-012 | TC-014 | TC-020 | TC-023 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Who reacts | A loyal minister | A thief about to die on the stake | A seven-year-old brahman boy about to be sacrificed | An old ascetic about to take a dead youth's body (KSS); Hindi XXIV: the boy's father |
| Reaction | Heart bursts at the king's triumph (12.19.162) | Weeps, then laughs (12.21.43) | Laughs (12.27.125) | Weeps, then dances (12.30.33); Ś 22 and Hindi XXII: weeps then laughs; Hindi XXIV: father laughs then weeps |
| What the answer finds | Despair for a kingdom ruled by a pleasure-loving king (12.19.168–170) | Grief at an unrepayable debt; wonder at the girl's love (12.21.59–60) | Every protector has become a threat; joy at his own purpose (12.27.130–136) | Grief at leaving an old body; joy at youth (12.30.45–47); Hindi XXIV: learning the art; one's own coming death (N2 §6) |
| Story around it | A long sea romance with a Vidyādharī and a curse (171 verses) | An outlaw romance: a king in disguise, a duel, a girl who chooses a condemned man | A romance prelude, then a demon's demand and a child volunteer | A funeral and a body-snatching deception (KSS); short in all versions (N2 §10) |
| Ending | Death and mourning | Revival and marriage | **Not narrated in KSS**; the Hindi has the boy killed (N1 TC-020) | False resurrection; the ascetic walks away young |

**What each asks Betal to judge.** TC-012: was a loyal man's death envy, ambition or despair (a question about motive and loyalty). TC-014: what a dying stranger felt towards those who tried to save him (gratitude and wonder). TC-020: what a child understands about the people who should protect him (betrayal and transcendence). TC-023: what attachment to a body means to someone leaving it (age, youth and mortality).

**Same or different dilemma.** **Different situations and different inner states**; the **form** is the same. A reader would meet four riddles in which the answer is an interpretation of a feeling.

**Experience.** TC-012 is wonder and melancholy; TC-014 is adventure, then pathos; TC-020 is horror held in a frozen tableau; TC-023 is macabre irony. These are genuinely different emotional experiences.

**Could they hold separate slots under D-035?** Yes. None repeats another's plot. The pressure is on **variety of question form**: with all four in the 25, about one tale in six would end on "why did he react so?". The catalogue flagged this as "the clearest repetition risk in the pool" (catalogue §6.1), and N1 enlarged it from two to four tales.

**Uncertain.** TC-020's ending (killed or spared) is unresolved across versions, which changes its emotional experience substantially. TC-023's two Hindi variants change the subject of the riddle (agent or witness, N2 §6).

**Conclusion: Related by question form; distinct central dilemmas.** The owner's question is how many reaction riddles the collection can carry before the form feels repetitive, not which of them duplicates another.

## 4. TC-004 and TC-020: a child sacrificed for a king

**What they share.** A king's life or safety depends on a supernatural being's demand for a human victim; a **brahman boy** is offered **with his parents' consent**; the boy consents willingly (TC-004's Sattvavara says it repays the king's food, 12.11.55–63; TC-020's boy argues for it, 12.27.96–110) (N1 §4.4, "two tales sacrifice a brahman boy for a king with the parents' consent").

**How the events and characters differ.**

| | TC-004 | TC-020 |
|---|---|---|
| The demand | The Earth says only the son's sacrifice to Caṇḍikā will save Śūdraka (12.11.40–54) | A brahman-demon demands a willing boy, held by his parents, killed by the king's own hand (12.27.74–82) |
| Who offers the boy | His own father, the king's devoted servant | Parents who answer a public proclamation for a golden statue and a hundred villages (12.27.88–110) |
| Motive | Loyalty | Reward (Vikrama says "greed for wealth", 12.27.132) |
| Who kills | The father | The king is about to |
| Chain of deaths | Son, daughter (grief), wife (fire), father (self-beheading), then all revived | One death pending; the outcome is not narrated in KSS |
| Question | Who is the greatest hero? | Why did the boy laugh? |

**Same or different dilemma.** **Different.** TC-004 weighs a family's and a king's sacrifices against each other. TC-020 asks what the boy's laugh reveals: in Vikrama's reading, that every protector (parents, king, deity) has turned against him.

**Experience.** TC-004 builds intense devotion to a divine reversal. TC-020 is a moment of suspended horror whose meaning is a child's insight into betrayal. The moral situation is close to opposite: in TC-004 the parents give the boy out of loyalty; in TC-020 for payment.

**Could both hold separate slots?** Yes under D-035. The overlap is a **shared central image** (a consenting child victim for a king), which is strong enough that the owner may feel two such tales weigh heavily on one collection. Both also carry difficult handling questions (child sacrifice by parents; see each bucket's §7).

**Uncertain.** The Hindi narrates the boy's death in TC-020; KSS does not narrate the outcome (N1 TC-020). That choice (under D-034) would change how close the two tales feel.

**Conclusion: Related stories, but distinct central dilemmas.**

## 5. TC-002 and TC-022: restoration or revival of the dead

**What they share.** A skill or spell that brings the dead to life, and claimants or makers who each contribute part of an outcome (N1 §4.6 item 5).

**How they differ.** TC-002: three men's devotion and a stolen spell revive a dead woman; they then quarrel over her (12.9.13–35). TC-022: four dispossessed brothers each learn one part of creating life and together revive a lion's bone; it kills them (12.29.29–41). TC-002's revival restores; TC-022's revival destroys.

**What each asks.** TC-002: whose wife is she? (12.9.36), a kinship classification. TC-022: who is guilty of making the lion? (12.29.46); in Ś 21 and Hindi XXI, **"who is the fool?"** (N1 TC-022), a blame or folly question. The answer in TC-022 turns on knowledge of danger at the moment of acting (the life-giver saw it was a lion, 12.29.50).

**Experience.** TC-002 moves through grief, horror and wonder to a quarrel. TC-022 is a grim fable of misfortune, ambition and sudden violence; the narrator frames it as a lesson (12.29.42–44), which a retelling would need to handle under D-006.

**Conclusion: Shared motif or device only.** The revival device is shared, the stories and dilemmas are not.

## 6. TC-008 and TC-011: refinement, delicacy and physical tests

**What they share.** The same question form ("who is most fastidious / delicate?", 12.15.49; 12.18.36); a **triple escalating test** in which each case is a small marvel of sensitivity; a comic, courtly, hyperbolic tone; an answer that turns on **how the effect was produced** (direct visible evidence in TC-008; injury without contact in TC-011) (N1 TC-011, "close in kind to TC-008"; §4.6 item 6).

**How they differ.**

| | TC-008 | TC-011 |
|---|---|---|
| Who is tested | Three brahman brothers, proud of their fastidiousness | Three queens of one king |
| Setting and frame | Brothers sent to fetch a turtle for their father's sacrifice; they quarrel and seek a king's judgement (12.15.3–18) | A king's pleasures at the spring festival and on a moonlit terrace (12.18.6–28) |
| Tests | Rice that smells of the cremation ground; a courtesan who smells of goat's milk; a hair under seven mattresses (12.15.19–45) | A lotus wounds a thigh; moonbeams raise blisters; the sound of a pestle bruises a hand (12.18.6–28) |
| How the cases are verified | Each is a small detective inquiry revealing a hidden cause | Physical injuries appear |
| Irony | The brothers never fetch the turtle and "earn the sin" of obstructing the sacrifice (12.15.47) | The king reflects that refinement has become a fault (12.18.29–34) |
| Answer | The bed-connoisseur: his mark was seen with one's own eyes (12.15.50–51); Ś and Hindi give no reason (N1 TC-008) | The pestle-queen: hurt without contact (12.18.37–38) |
| Variants | Position in Ś and Hindi varies widely (Ś 23, Hindi XXIII) | Ś and Hindi add a Jaina prelude with Guṇaśekhara and the minister Abhayacandra (N1 TC-011) |

**Same or different dilemma.** The dilemma type is the same: compare three extreme sensitivities. The **grounds** differ (proof against contact). TC-008 has a longer frame (the turtle errand, the king's investigations, the ironic close); TC-011 is a shorter, concentrated court vignette. Neither is preferred here.

**Could both hold separate slots?** Under D-035's wording, they are not one plot repeated, so the count rule does not force one slot. But they are the **closest pair in the pool in form, tone and question**, and a reader would likely feel they had met the same joke twice.

**Uncertain.** The Ś/Hindi Jaina prelude to TC-011 (N1 TC-011) has not been examined for whether it gives the tale a different shape in those versions.

**Conclusion: Same type of story, likely competing for one slot on variety grounds, although not one underlying plot.** This is the comparison most likely to cost the collection a slot.

## 7. TC-015 and TC-026: Mūladeva and trickster material (with J 13)

**What they share.** The rogue pair **Mūladeva and his friend Śaśin (Śaśideva in J)**; a trick that removes a woman from her guardians; a disguised claim made before a king; the king deceived (N3 §3.1). J 13 is Jambhaladatta's telling of TC-015, so the comparison is within one recension as well as across the pool.

**How the events and characters differ** (N3 §3.1 table, summarised).

| | TC-015 (KSS 15; J 13) | TC-026 (J 22) |
|---|---|---|
| The woman | An unmarried princess, already in love with the lover | A merchant's wife, married twelve years, husband absent |
| The lover | Manaḥsvāmin, a brahman (KSS) or bard's son (J 13) | Śaśideva himself |
| Means | A sex-changing pill (KSS) or charm (J 13); disguise; a threat of a brahman's curse | A bawd posing as an all-knowing ascetic; a false oracle and a false charm; a staged mistaken identity; a substituted corpse and a fire |
| Who is deceived | The king and his minister | The parents, the king and (in absence) the husband |
| Second woman | The minister's son's bride Mṛgāṅkavatī, seduced and carried off; her fate untold | None |
| Question | **Whose wife is she?** (12.22.111) | **Who was cleverer, the bawd or Mūladeva?** (J T p. 132) |
| Answer | Śaśin, by the father's open gift; the secret lover is a "thief" (12.22.113–114); Ś and Hindi add the princess's pregnancy and the child's rites (N1 TC-015) | Mūladeva (J); **the bawd** (Newari 22) (N3 §5.3) |

**Same or different dilemma.** **Different.** TC-015 is a legal claim to a wife; TC-026 is a comparison of cleverness. N3 states that J 22 "is not a repetition of J 13 at the level of events or dilemma; it is a second, independent Mūladeva intrigue with overlapping devices" (N3 §3.1).

**Experience.** Both are comic intrigues. TC-026 has a darker undertow (a staged death, grieving parents) and celebrates the trick itself; TC-015 ends in a dispute between two men whom the trick made rivals.

**Could both hold separate slots?** Yes under D-035. The variety question is whether the collection wants **two Mūladeva intrigues**; as the pool's main source of comic trickery, they would also be the collection's main source of a particular kind of humour.

**Uncertain.** TC-026 rests on one Bengali manuscript (P) plus the 1873 print; W was collated for names only (N3 §1.3, §7). The Newari answer reverses J's (N3 §5.3).

**Conclusion: Related stories, but distinct central dilemmas.**

## 8. TC-016 and TC-027: daily tribute and the weeping mother

**What they share.** A pact by which a devourer receives **one victim a day**; a **mother weeping** for a son whose turn has come; a stranger who intervenes (N3 §6.2). In KSS 16, Garuḍa eats one Nāga a day by agreement with Vāsuki, and Śaṅkhacūḍa's mother laments (12.23.94–130). In J 23, the king agrees to give the ogre one man a day, and the lot falls on a woman's son; the foreign king Mahābala intervenes (N3 §4). In J's own sequence, J 24 (TC-016) opens right after J 23 with a weeping serpent-mother (Emeneau Tr p. 141; **seen in passing only, not read**, N3 §6.2).

**How they differ.**

| | TC-016 | TC-027 |
|---|---|---|
| Hero | Jīmūtavāhana, a compassionate Vidyādhara prince who gives his own body | Mahābala, a foreign king who beats the ogre, is snubbed, and releases it in anger |
| Shape | A long romance and martyrdom (207 verses): renunciation, love, self-offering, revival | A wonder opening (a girl in a river creature's belly), a king's insult, a revenge plague, a double rescue with a reversal |
| Tone | High pathos and devotion | Horror (a shape-shifting ogress who eats her lovers), pride and ingratitude, a generous close |
| Question | Who had greater courage, Jīmūtavāhana or Śaṅkhacūḍa? (12.23.203) | Who is the slayer of the citizens: the king, Mahābala or the ogre? (J T p. 138) |
| Answer | Śaṅkhacūḍa; reasoning varies (many births / practice / caste) (Report §8.3) | The king; **no reasoning given** (N3 §4) |

**Same or different dilemma.** **Different:** comparative courage against blame for a chain of causes.

**Conclusion: Shared motif or device only.** The tribute-and-mother setup is a strong shared image. If both are chosen, a retelling should be aware of it (and, if TC-020 is chosen too, of a third demon-demanding-a-victim tale; N3 §6.2).

## 9. TC-001 and TC-026: scheming helpers, deception and family consequences

**What they share.** A clever helper who schemes on a lover's behalf; deceived parents and a deceived king; a staged falsehood that removes a woman from her family (TC-001: a fake witchcraft accusation and the woman's banishment; TC-026: a fake death) (N3 §6.2).

**How they differ.** TC-001 is driven by a contest of wits between Padmāvatī (who speaks in coded signs) and the minister's son, and it ends with her parents dying of grief (12.8.185). TC-026 is driven by a bawd and Mūladeva, and the parents are left weeping over jewels, apparently deceived (N3 §3, uncertainty (a)). TC-001 asks **who bears the sin of the parents' deaths** (answer: the king who judged without inquiry, 12.8.190–195). TC-026 asks **who was cleverer**.

**Conclusion: Shared motif or device only.** The catalogue's title-level flag "is partly borne out at the level of devices, not of dilemma" (N3 §6.2).

## 10. TC-010 and TC-027: the limited ogre connection

**What they share.** A brahman-ogre (*brahmarākṣasa*) appears in **J's version** of TC-010 (J 10 adds an ogre who stops the bride on her way, Report §8.1) and in J 23 (Tālajaṅgha, N3 §4). That is all.

**How they differ.** TC-010 is about a promise and three men's restraint; the ogre in J 10 is one more encounter on the road and is not in KSS, Ś or the Hindi. TC-027 is about a city devoured and a king's guilt.

**Conclusion: Shared motif or device only** (confirmed by N3; catalogue §6.2).

## 11. TC-022 and TC-025: the four-brothers opening

**What they share.** Four brothers with different accomplishments open the story.

**How they differ.** In TC-022 the brothers' four skills combine to revive a lion, and the riddle is about them (12.29.29–50). In TC-025 the four brothers compete to win a courtesan's second night; three fail and the youngest succeeds by a trick, and the riddle is about **the courtesan** (why did she, who remembers her past lives, fail to recognise him?) (N3 §2). Emeneau links J 21's brothers to several tales with sets of differently skilled men (J 2, 5, 6, 9, 11, 19) (N3 §6.2).

**Conclusion: Shared motif or device only** (N3 §6.2: "a shared opening device, not a shared dilemma").

## 12. Additional comparison: blame questions (TC-001, TC-013, TC-022, TC-027)

**Why it is included.** Four candidates ask "who is guilty?", and two of them answer "the king". This affects variety of question form (lens A in the overview) more than D-035.

| | Question | Answer | What the answer rests on |
|---|---|---|---|
| TC-001 | Whose sin is the parents' death? (12.8.186–187) | King Karṇotpala | He acted without inquiry (12.8.194–195) |
| TC-013 | Whose is the brahman-murder? (12.20.54) | Whoever blames any party without reflection (12.20.59) | No participant is guilty; the hasty judge is |
| TC-022 | Who is guilty of making the lion? (12.29.46); Ś, Hindi: who is the fool? | The life-giver | He acted knowing the danger (12.29.50) |
| TC-027 | Who is the slayer of the citizens? (J T p. 138) | King Arimaulimaṇi | **No reasoning given** (N3 §4) |

**Same or different dilemma.** Different: N1 notes that the three core blame answers "all turn on knowledge and inquiry", each in a different way (N1 §4.1). TC-027's reasoning is left to the reader; its plot links guilt to pride, a daily tribute and ingratitude (N3 §6.1).

**Conclusion: Related by question form; distinct dilemmas.** TC-001 and TC-027 share the answer "the king is to blame" on different grounds.

## 13. Additional comparison: TC-007 and TC-012, the undersea journey made twice

**Why it is included.** N1 records the same narrative structure in both: a journey made first by a subordinate and then by the king, a marvellous undersea city, a supernatural woman, and a return through a garden tank (N1 §4.5).

**How they differ.** TC-007 is about a servant's unrewarded devotion and the king's repayment of a debt; the king gives the Daitya princess to the servant. TC-012 is about a pleasure-loving king who wins a Vidyādharī, and a loyal minister whose heart bursts at the king's success. The questions differ: which leap took more courage (TC-007); why the minister died (TC-012).

**Conclusion: Shared motif or device only.** But if both are chosen, the collection would contain two sea-voyage-to-an-undersea-city romances with the same portal device; that is a variety consideration for the owner.

## 14. Additional comparison: TC-018 and TC-025, the causal "why did it fail?" question

**Why it is included.** N3 places TC-025's question closest to TC-018's (N3 §6.1; catalogue §6.1).

**How they differ.** TC-018: a gambler learns a magic science and fails its final test by an instant of hesitation inside an illusory lifetime (12.25.59–81); the answer is a wavering mind (12.25.85–86). TC-025: a courtesan who remembers her past lives is tricked by a man who tells her own story back to her; the answer is that her life as a courtesan made her forget (J T p. 124). The stories share nothing beyond the question's shape; TC-025's answer carries moralising about courtesans that TC-018's does not (N3 §6.4).

**Conclusion: Shared motif or device only** (question form).
