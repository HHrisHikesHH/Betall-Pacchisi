# Narrator voice

Binding for the frame and every tale (D-078, D-079). Every brief, draft and critique reads this page.

## Who speaks

An elder storyteller, दादा की आवाज़, speaking to the reader as **"तुम"**. The reader is the grandchild. No child characters are added to the story, and the visible grandpa scene is optional and later (D-078). The voice still changes its tone with the moment: suspense, warmth, humour, quiet (D-007).

Reference passage: [voice-test.md](../stories/FRAME/hi/voice-test.md), version **A**.

## Where the rules come from

They come from a study of how Osho tells the frame story (tale 1), made from a Bengali rendering of his talk that the user shared on 2026-11-27. We study the *craft*. We never quote, paraphrase or imitate his lines, and we don't adopt his readings of the tales.

## Use

| # | Device | Example of the idea |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Promise a hidden layer before the story starts. | "Heard in a hurry it is a ghost story; heard slowly, something else comes out of it." |
| 2 | Give a human motive for every odd detail. | Why the king never asked; why the treasurer kept the fruit and didn't throw it away. |
| 3 | Grow wisdom out of the plot object itself. | The fruit is the daily chance nobody opens. |
| 4 | Move from event to character, then turn it on the reader: "and you too". | "Laugh at the king, then think what comes to you every day that you stopped looking at." |
| 5 | Keep asides short. Each is born from a character's feeling and closed with **"ख़ैर"** back into the story. | |
| 6 | Vikram is human and afraid. | |
| 7 | Send the reader there. | "Go to a cremation ground at midnight yourself…" |
| 8 | Use universal behaviour the reader nods at. | People hum or chant when they are afraid. |
| 9 | Mirror the reader's own absorption. | "Just as you are listening now…" |
| 10 | Hand the riddle to the reader before Vikram answers (the pause itself is the interface's job, D-071). | |
| 11 | Stage reversals of status. | The beggar's fruit holds a king's ransom. |

## Avoid

- **The long sermon tail.** No teaching paragraph at the end of a tale.
- **Bending the plot to fit the teaching.** Follow the source: the ascetic is a schemer, not a sage, and there are no "25 corpses".
- **Confident claims that aren't true:** about history, texts, science or people.
- **One-meaning allegory** (D-006). Leave room for more than one reading.
- **Spoken repetition in print.** What works aloud (repeated phrases, fillers) is cut on the page.

## Limits

- 1–3 "and you too" turns per tale.
- Asides under about 10% of a tale's words, and no more than 3 sentences each.
- No ending sermon. The tale ends on the story or on Betal's question.

## Chapter endings (D-090)

Every tale ends by pulling the reader into the next. Use a cliffhanger, a taunt, a turn, or an image: something creative and different each time. Build it from the frame situation, such as Betal's laugh and what he says as he goes back, the king's body and mind, the hour, or the walk to the tree. Never name the next tale's content or its number.
