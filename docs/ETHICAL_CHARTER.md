## 3. Ethical Editorial Charter

### 3.0 Standing and honesty rules
- The charter is **informed by** Dr B. R. Ambedkar's documented ideas. **It never claims, and no project text may imply, that he endorsed, reviewed or would approve this project.**
- Every specific claim about his thought cites a text and a location. The charter keeps **source evidence** (column A) apart from **our interpretation for storytelling** (column B).
- **No AI session administers this charter alone** on the highest-risk texts. A human sensitivity reader is recommended (D-046).
- The **formal citation** for a quotation is *Dr. Babasaheb Ambedkar: Writings and Speeches* (BAWS, Govt. of Maharashtra / Dr. Ambedkar Foundation). I read the passages below in online **reproductions and transcriptions**, not BAWS itself (links in Sources). Before any quotation goes into a doc, a research task checks the BAWS volume and page. Until then the charter paraphrases and cites by section.

### 3.1 Scope
The charter binds the 19 tales, the frame, interface copy and any reader-facing notes. Every blueprint, critique and verification cites charter clauses by number.

### 3.2 Caste (D-040)
**The creative director's instruction: keep caste visible when it matters, and make sure discrimination is called out and never normalised.**

1. Caste may appear where it is historically, socially or narratively relevant. When it appears, discrimination is **recognised as discrimination** through events, consequences or a character's clear-eyed perception. It is never normalised, romanticised, justified or presented as natural order.
2. No caste is superior or inferior. Intelligence, morality, purity, capability, dignity and rights are **never** set by birth, in the narration, the dialogue's endorsed views or the verdict.
3. Untouchability, exclusion, purity hierarchy and caste violence are never legitimate.
4. An occupation is never tied to birth as natural or obligatory (principle 3).
5. **Three-way label test, applied to every caste, community or occupation marker in a tale** (revised; it replaces my first draft's "drop when irrelevant" default):
   - **(a) Connected:** the identity is tied to the events, to the injustice shown, to the verdict's subversion, or to the practice being satirised. → **Keep it and make it visible.**
   - **(b) Encodes worth:** the marker states a hierarchy of human worth (a life valued by varṇa, a group as savages, virtue as caste nature). → Keep it **only as a character's belief or a demand made inside the story**, made visible as such. It is never narrated as truth and never carries the verdict.
   - **(c) An idle tag:** no function in the tale. → May be dropped, but **only after the slate-level pattern audit** (rule 9) confirms that dropping it does not skew how groups appear across the collection.

   Never relabel while keeping the hierarchy (a "coat of paint", principle 5). Never erase a real injustice.
6. A character may hold casteist beliefs. The narrative does not endorse them, and the tale's structure (reward, resolution, verdict) must not depend on them.
7. **Test:** if the tale's central reward, resolution or moral needs caste superiority to be legitimate, the tale gets a MAJOR REWRITE or is REJECTED.
8. **No new stereotypes, and no collective judgement of any group.** Do not make any community, **brahmans included**, collectively foolish, villainous or morally inferior. Where a character's identity is connected to the satire (rule 5a), the satire's **target is named in the telling**: a particular obsession, behaviour, contradiction or social practice, shown as *these people's*, never as inherent to their birth group. The world of the tale includes others of the same community who do not share it. The rule also covers **religious communities**. A Buddhist *bhikṣu* villain matters especially, because Ambedkarite Buddhists are a living community. Jain, Kāpālika and stock "evil tantrik" figures are covered too (see §8).
9. **Slate-level pattern audit.** This runs on the blueprints (before drafting) and again on the final texts, and it covers every group: brahmans, Kshatriya rulers, merchants, artisans and working people, forest peoples, courtesans, and Buddhist, Jain, Kāpālika and other religious figures. It asks **how each group appears across all 19 and the frame**: as fools, villains, sellers, victims, or full people. A pattern fails even if every tale passes alone. The working table is in §5.3.

### 3.3 Gender, consent and agency
1. Moral standards are the same for every gender. There are no collective judgements about men or women, and that includes the tale's **verdict and its reasoning**.
2. Women are full characters with desires, intelligence, motives and consequential choices. They are never prizes, property, rewards or devices.
3. Coercion, manipulation, sexual exploitation and forced marriage are never romanticised. Consent is treated as real. Victims are not blamed.
4. A misogynistic source verdict is recorded, analysed and replaced under D-041, never kept merely because the source has it.
5. Over-correction is also a defect. Do not turn every woman into a flawless heroine, and do not make men disposable or stupid as a group.
6. **No laundering of coercion into choice.** Where the source shows a woman sold, deceived or constrained, the adaptation keeps the constraint visible. "Agency" the plot cannot honour is decoration. Agency is given where the source already places a real decision, or through interiority, never by rewriting coercion as consent. The "she chooses" device is used sparingly across the slate, and the lead tracks how often it appears.

### 3.4 Human dignity and power
1. Poverty, occupation, gender, birth and rank do not set worth. Ordinary people (servants, guards, townsfolk, the daily "tribute") are never disposable scenery.
2. Children are never instruments of political or religious loyalty without the story examining that.
3. Abuse, servitude, sati, forced sacrifice and exploitation are never presented as unquestionable virtue.
4. The powerful are accountable for their choices, and so is Vikram.
5. Loyalty and sacrifice are examined, not assumed: who benefits, who suffers, was it free?

### 3.5 Storytelling stays storytelling
The charter shapes **characterisation, events, choices, consequences and endings**. It does not add speeches. Each review asks: *is the injustice made visible by what happens, or by what the narrator says?* If it is only said, the draft fails both the charter and the North Star §10. Depicting wrongdoing is not endorsing it. What counts is whether the narrative **rewards, excuses or normalises** it.

**Honest-difficulty test (D-041).** A fix must not settle a dilemma by making Vikram obviously right. Betal's objections are a genuine attested device (KSS 1, 4, 17), but they are **not the charter's mouthpiece**. When Betal voices a source position, it is argued at full strength and not set up to be knocked down. A fix that only works by having Vikram state a thesis fails.

### 3.6 Social-role adaptation rule
For each character, classify the role as **Essential** (to the dilemma), **Historically relevant, adaptable**, or **Incidental, replaceable**. For any change, record the original role, the new role, why, and its effect on the story. Keep kings where political power is the dilemma. Keep specialised knowledge where the supernatural or intellectual plot needs it. Never assign an occupation by caste, and never modernise the world out of its historical and fantastical register.
