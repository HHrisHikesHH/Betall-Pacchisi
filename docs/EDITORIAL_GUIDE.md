# Editorial Guide

This guide explains how stories should be written and judged. It applies decisions D-005 to D-009 and D-012 to D-013 in [DECISIONS.md](DECISIONS.md). The project's overall storytelling identity and a reviewer checklist are in [STORYTELLING_NORTH_STAR.md](STORYTELLING_NORTH_STAR.md) (D-036). The process (drafts, roles, approvals) is in [STORY_WORKFLOW.md](STORY_WORKFLOW.md). Rules on sources are in [SOURCE_AND_RESEARCH_POLICY.md](SOURCE_AND_RESEARCH_POLICY.md).

## 1. The narrator

**APPROVED (D-007, D-008, D-033)**

The narrator is **not a character standing outside the story to explain it**. The narrator is the way the story is told. Two things need to be kept apart:

| | Constant or changing? | What it is |
|---|---|---|
| **Underlying sensibility** (D-033) | Constant | Deeply wise, well-read, observant, introspective, and perceptive: a mature mind that notices what others pass over. |
| **Manner of telling** (D-007) | Changes with each story and moment | Suspenseful, humorous, quiet, warm, contemplative, dramatic, or whatever the moment deserves. |

The sensibility shows in **what the narration notices**: a revealing gesture, an irony in a character's choice, the detail that will matter later. It should not show in a solemn tone or in explanation. A funny passage can be just as perceptive as a contemplative one. Readers should *occasionally* find an insight they would have missed on an ordinary reading. They should never feel the narrator is announcing its wisdom or explaining every hidden meaning.

The narrator's gender, identity, biography, and whether it is presented as human or AI are **OPEN** (`Q-005`). Do not invent them.

### Manner of telling

| The moment calls for… | The narration may become… |
|---|---|
| A cremation ground at midnight | Quiet, close, and attentive to small sounds |
| A clever trick or absurd situation | Light, quick, and humorous |
| A reveal or approaching danger | Taut, with short sentences and withheld information |
| Grief or tenderness | Warm and unhurried |
| A choice that exposes a character | Challenging, turning the question back on the reader |
| An image that needs room | Spare, or silent |

The story decides the voice. A narrator who sounds the same in every tale, or who always reaches for profundity, has failed this guideline, even though the perceptive sensibility beneath it never changes.

### Direct address to the reader

Direct address is **allowed occasionally**, when it helps the reader understand a character's:

- situation or stakes,
- emotions or inner thoughts,
- dilemma, or the weight of a decision.

A good test is whether, after the aside, the reader feels *closer to the character*. If the reader feels closer to the narrator's opinions instead, cut the aside.

Direct address must **not** become:

- a lecture,
- philosophical commentary unconnected to the scene,
- a summary of what the reader should conclude,
- a habit that turns up in every story because it worked once.

## 2. Creative freedom

**APPROVED (D-009): Broad imagination, disciplined relevance.**

Writers have **substantial freedom** to deepen a story. They may develop:

- sensory description (sight, sound, smell, texture, temperature),
- character psychology and motivation,
- atmosphere and setting,
- dialogue,
- emotional tension,
- philosophical implications that the events already carry.

Every addition must stay faithful to the story's:

- **identity**: it is still recognisably this tale,
- **purpose**: what the tale is doing, including the riddle or dilemma it sets up,
- **emotional requirements**: what the reader must feel for the ending to land,
- **essential events**: the plot points that define the tale in its sources.

### Testing an addition

*PROPOSED checklist for applying D-009.*

Before keeping an invented passage, ask:

1. Does it make the reader see, feel, or understand something the story *needs*?
2. Would the story be weaker without it, or only shorter?
3. Does it point attention towards the story's centre, or away from it?
4. Is it in proportion? A small moment should stay small. It should not grow into an unrelated epic.
5. Have we recorded it as our addition and not as source material? (See [SOURCE_AND_RESEARCH_POLICY.md](SOURCE_AND_RESEARCH_POLICY.md).)

## 3. Depth and interpretation

**APPROVED (D-005, D-006)**

- **The surface story must work on its own.** A reader with no interest in philosophy should still enjoy it fully.
- **Depth should be discovered, not delivered.** Insight should arise from what characters do, what they choose, and what it costs them. It should not come from the narrator stating it.
- **Do not assume a single moral.** Many Betal tales end with a question, and a question can have more than one honest answer. Where the story allows it, keep several readings available.
- **Avoid complexity for its own sake.** Depth is not the same as difficulty.
- **Rereading should reward the reader.** Details that seem decorative the first time can carry meaning the second time.

## 4. Literary standards

*PROPOSED checklist. It expands the review criteria approved for Drafts 2 and 3 (D-019, [STORY_WORKFLOW.md](STORY_WORKFLOW.md) §3). The specific items have not been separately approved.*

A finished story should have:

- **A compelling opening** that makes the reader want to continue.
- **Coherent structure and chronology.** The reader is never confused unless confusion is the intended effect.
- **Believable motivation.** Characters act for reasons the reader can feel, even when the world is fantastical.
- **Living dialogue.** People speak as people in their situation would. Dialogue is not a vehicle for exposition.
- **Controlled pacing**, with suspense, release, and room to breathe.
- **Earned reveals**, prepared for without being given away.
- **Emotional truth.** The reader feels the stakes.
- **Precise, specific language.** Choose concrete images over vague adjectives.
- **A riddle or dilemma that feels genuinely difficult**, so the reader wants to answer before Vikram does.

### Hindi and English

**APPROVED (D-012, D-013)**

- Hindi is written first and should read as **natural, literary Hindi**. *What register of Hindi to use (for example, how Sanskritised or colloquial) is OPEN: see `Q-402` in [OPEN_QUESTIONS.md](OPEN_QUESTIONS.md).*
- **APPROVED (D-013):** English is a **natural literary adaptation** of the approved Hindi story, not a word-for-word translation. It must preserve the story's meaning, emotional impact, atmosphere, and depth. Like all writing for the project, it stays faithful to the story's identity and essential events (D-009).
- **PROPOSED (P-007), not yet approved:** the adapter has freedom to reshape sentences, imagery, idiom, rhythm, and emphasis so the story lives in English. As a working guideline, the English should not introduce new plot events or settle interpretations that the Hindi deliberately leaves open. Where the adaptation departs noticeably from the Hindi, record the departure and its reason so a reviewer can judge it. The aim is a story that works in English, not a line-by-line match.

## 5. What to avoid

The user explicitly asked to avoid preaching, forced morals, filler, generic prose, artificial dialogue, unrelated digressions, and forced philosophy (D-006, D-009). Uniform narration follows from D-007, and imitation from D-018. **Over-modernising is PROPOSED.** The "Instead" column is guidance, not decision.

| Avoid | What it looks like | Instead |
|---|---|---|
| **Preaching** | The narrator tells the reader what is right. | Let the consequences and the dilemma speak. |
| **Forced morals** | Every tale closes with a neat lesson. | Close on the question, the image, or the choice. |
| **Filler** | Passages that add length but not meaning. | Cut them. A shorter, sharper story is better. |
| **Generic prose** | "The night was dark and mysterious." | Choose specific images: what *this* night sounds and smells like. |
| **Artificial dialogue** | Characters explain things they would never say aloud. | Write subtext. Let what is unsaid carry weight. |
| **Unrelated digressions** | Tangents on history, philosophy, or description that pull away from the tale. | Keep only what deepens this story. |
| **Forced philosophy** | Profound-sounding statements attached to simple moments. | Trust the story. If the depth is there, readers will find it. |
| **Uniform narration** | The same voice and devices in every tale. | Let each story set its own voice (Section 1). |
| **Over-modernising** | Modern idiom or sensibilities that break the world of the tale. | Make it emotionally contemporary while keeping the world consistent. |
| **Imitation** | Writing "in the voice of" a real person, or borrowing their phrasing. | Learn the technique, then write in the project's own voice (D-018). |
