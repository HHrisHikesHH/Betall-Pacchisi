### R1. KSS states 24 question-tales plus a concluding 25th (12.32.24–27)

**CONFIRMED** in the GRETIL e-text [S02].

- 12.32.24 has Vikrama call "these first question-tales", "twenty-four", and "this twenty-fifth one, which concludes" (*ādyāḥ praśnakathā etā … caturviṃśatir eṣā ca pañcaviṃśī samāptigā*).
- 12.32.26–27 has the Vetāla repeat the division (*yāś caturviṃśatiḥ pūrvā yaiṣā caikā samāpinī*) and name the collection *vetālapañcaviṃśatikā*.
- The e-text's own tale markup agrees: `Vet_1` … `Vet_24`, then `Vet_25` for 12.32.1–41.

**COULD NOT CHECK** the Durgaprasad–Parab 1915 print. No scan of it was consulted.

### R2. Jambhaladatta's tale 10: ogre, rejection, and the husband as answer

**CONFIRMED** in Emeneau's text and translation [S07, story 10, pp. 66–73].

- A brahman-ogre (*brahmarākṣasa*) living in a banyan stops her after the thief and releases her (translation p. 71).
- On her return the husband, Kandarpa, tells her that he cannot now approve of her, and dismisses her (p. 71).
- Emeneau's note 40 (p. 71) states that in the other versions the husband is delighted and embraces the wife. *[Corrected in §7.6: the page image shows this is note **10**, not 40. "40" was an OCR misreading, which the report's R2 row repeats.]*
- The Vetāla asks which of Kandarpa, the thief, the ogre and Dharmadatta is noblest. Vikrama names **Kandarpa**: he first let her go to save Dharmadatta's life, then dismissed her as having been with another man, and the others' nobility was "conditioned and not absolute" (Sanskrit p. 72, translation p. 73).
- The names in the report (Vīrakeśarin, Anaṅgapura, Arthadatta, Dhanadatta, Lāvaṇyavatī, Kandarpa, Dharmadatta) all match (pp. 66–69).

Minor precision: the report cites "pp. 66–72" in §8.1 and "pp. 66–73" in R2. The story runs pp. 66–73, and the answer's translation is on p. 73.

### R3. KSS 10 and Ś 9: the husband's reception, and the thief as answer

**CONFIRMED.**

- **KSS** [S02]: she returns with her conduct intact and tells her husband everything (12.17.58–59). He sees that her character is unbroken, welcomes her, and lives happily with her (*tasthau … tayā saha yathāsukham*, 12.17.60–61). The question is which was the true *tyāgī* (12.17.63). The answer is the thief (*cauras tyāgī*, 12.17.64), with the reasons at 12.17.65–67.
  One addition: the king gives two reasons for the lover's restraint, not one. His passion had cooled with time, and he feared that her husband would report him to the king (12.17.66). The report's "feared exposure" covers only the second.
- **Ś 9** [S06, Sanskrit text pp. 27–28; colophon *navamaṃ kathānakam*]: names as the report gives them (Madanapura, King Madanavīra, Hiraṇyadatta, Madanasenā, Dharmadatta son of Somadatta). The lover delivers a run of verses against women (vv. 9–16). On her return the husband embraces her affectionately (*sasnehaṃ āliṅganaṃ kṛtam*), followed by the verse "a woman's beauty is fidelity" (v. 17). The question is *trayāṇāṃ madhye kaḥ sattvādhikaḥ*. The answer is the thief, because the husband let her go knowing her mind was elsewhere, and the lover let her go for fear of the king's punishment.

### R4. The 1805 introduction: who made the Hindi text

**CONFIRMED** in Barker's English translation [S10, "Introduction to the Calcutta edition of 1805", on the leaf before p. 4 of the translation].

- Rājā Jai Siṅgh Sawāī, lord of Jaipur, in the time of Muhammad Shāh, asked a poet named **Surat** to render the Sanskrit *Baitāl Pachīsī* in Braj Bhāshā.
- Under Shāh ʿĀlam and Wellesley, **Mazhar ʿAlī Khān, the poet surnamed Wilā**, at **John Gilchrist**'s order, "with the aid of the poet Shri Lallu Ji Lal", set it forth in easy language, keeping a considerable share of Braj.
- Afterwards, under the direction of **Captain James Mouat**, **Tāriṇī Charan Mitr** removed the rare Sanskrit and Bhāshā words for printing.

The report's account of Barker's own **preface** is also confirmed. It names "Surat Kabishwar", credits Lallū Jī Lāl with preparing the Hindi version in 1805 "in conjunction with other learned natives", and presumes the Sanskrit version "had ceased to exist". The tension the report marks as DISPUTED is real: within one book, the preface's attribution goes beyond the introduction it translates.

**COULD NOT CHECK** the 1805 Calcutta print, which was not located.

### R5. The Hindi order follows Śivadāsa's

**CONFIRMED, and the match is closer than the report states.** Each tale was identified from its opening (kings, cities, protagonists) in Platts [S11] and in Uhle's text [S06].

- Hindi I–XXIII = Ś 1–23 in the same order, except that **III and IV are swapped**. Hindi III is Rupsen and Vīravara (Ś 4); Hindi IV is the parrot and mynah (Ś 3).
- **Hindi XXIV** (Kaliṅga, the brahman Yagya Sharmā) has no counterpart at that point in Ś.
- **Hindi XXV** (the mixed-relationships riddle and the conclusion) = **Ś 24**, followed by Ś's conclusion, which Ś counts as its 25th.

Proper names often match closely too. For example: Ś 10 and Hindi X both have Guṇaśekhara and his Jaina minister Abhayacandra; Ś 22 and Hindi XXII both have Viśvapura and King Vidagdha; Ś 23 and Hindi XXIII both have the brahman Govinda's sons. The report's inference (PROVISIONAL) of a Śivadāsa-type source somewhere in the Hindi's ancestry is consistent with this evidence. This review did not test that inference further.

### R6. Ryder 1917 translates Somadeva's version

**CONFIRMED by comparison** [S09 against S02].

- **Frame.** Ryder's Introduction has the "Abiding Kingdom" on the Godāvarī, King "Triple-victory" son of King "Victory", and the monk "Patience". The fruit goes daily to the treasurer, a pet monkey splits one and a gem falls out, and the treasurer reports the others smashed in the treasury. This follows KSS 12.8.21–32 step by step (Trivikramasena, Vikramasena, Pratiṣṭhāna, Kṣāntiśīla).
- **Tale 1** opens with Benares, where Śiva lives, loved like the soil of Kailāsa, with the river of heaven shining like a pearl necklace. This corresponds to KSS 12.8.59–60.
- **Conclusion.** Śiva appears and gives the king a magic sword, as in KSS 12.32.31–38.
- **Order.** Ryder's contents list 22 tales in KSS order, omitting **KSS 10** (the promise tale; his 10 is the delicate wives, KSS 11) and **KSS 19** (the funeral-offering tale; his 18 is the boy who laughed, KSS 20). The report labels this PROVISIONAL. The contents themselves are clear enough to support it.
- The "twelve years" in Ryder against "ten" (*daśasu*, 12.8.25) in the e-text is confirmed.
- Ryder's title page says only "Translated from the Sanskrit". No statement naming his source text or edition was found in the front matter, which agrees with the report.

### R8. Emeneau's 27-tale hypothesis and the recensions' omissions

**CONFIRMED** [S07 §§3–4, pp. xii–xvi].

- Emeneau states that Śivadāsa and the Kashmirian versions have 24 stories apart from the frame, with the conclusion as the 25th, while his text of Jambhaladatta has the Vetāla tell 25.
- Stories 21–23 are found in no other Sanskrit version. Jambhaladatta's text therefore put **27** stories in the Vetāla's mouth (the 24 common ones plus 3 new ones).
- The **Nepali** recension omitted Bṛhatkathā 17 and 18 (= Ś 16, 17; = J 14, 15). The **Bengali** recension omitted Bṛhatkathā 12 and 14 (= Ś 11, 13), which the Nepali kept. Each reduced the count to 25 independently.
- **V** (Vidyasagara 1873) omits story 18, so the Vetāla tells 24 and the conclusion is the 25th.

### R10. The Hindi's two body-entering tales (XXII and XXIV)

**CONFIRMED** that both tales exist and both turn on an old ascetic entering a dead youth's body. **CORRECTED** on who the riddle in XXIV is about.

- **Hindi XXII** (Biswapur, King Bidagdha, the brahman Nārāyan; Platts, answer on p. 167): the ascetic himself weeps and laughs. The king explains that he wept recalling his mother's love in infancy, his youth's happiness and his long tenancy of the old body, and laughed at succeeding in his art. This matches the report.
- **Hindi XXIV** (Kaliṅga, the brahman Yagya Sharmā; Platts pp. 175–176): after the ascetic enters the boy's body, the person who "first laughed, then wept" is the **boy's father**. Platts's wording is compatible with this reading. Barker's free translation [S10, Story XXIV] is explicit: the father became an ascetic (*bairāg*), and the Baitāl asks why the **father** first laughed and then cried. He laughed at seeing the yogī enter his son's body and so learning the art. He wept from regret, thinking that one day he too must leave his own body.
- **Consequence.** XXIV shares the setting and the body-entering plot of KSS 23 (Kaliṅga; KSS 12.30.4–41), but its riddle concerns an onlooker. In KSS 23 it is the ascetic who weeps and then dances, and the reasons (grief at leaving the body his parents cherished in childhood, joy at youth; 12.30.45–47) are closer to **Hindi XXII** and **Ś 22**. In Ś 22 the yogī weeps because this body was protected by his mother and raised by his father in childhood and enjoyed in youth, and laughs at gaining a young body (Uhle text pp. 57–58).
- So "doublet" is accurate for the plot. But XXIV is a variant with a different riddle subject, not a duplicate. The report's §8.5 answer column for XXIV, and its claim that one Hindi tale "matches Somadeva's setting", should say this.
- **COULD NOT CHECK** the Hindi original.

### R11. KSS 16: cultivated virtue against caste

**CONFIRMED.**

- **KSS** [S02]: the question is which of Śaṅkhacūḍa and Jīmūtavāhana excelled in *sattva* (12.23.203). The answer is Śaṅkhacūḍa: Jīmūtavāhana's act was "perfected over many births" (*bahujanmasiddham*), while Śaṅkhacūḍa, already spared, ran after Garuḍa to give his body (12.23.205–206).
- **Ś 15** [S06, Sanskrit text p. 43]: the answer is Śaṅkhacūḍa, because Jīmūtavāhana had practised giving his life for others in birth after birth (*janmani janmani … prāṇatyāgābhyāsaḥ*), with a verse on habits carried from birth to birth. Same reasoning as KSS.
- **Hindi XV**: Platts (p. 129) has Jīmūtavāhana "a Kshetri by caste … accustomed to holding his life in his hand". Barker's interlinear gloss independently gives "Jīmūtavāhan of-the-Kshatriya-caste is, to-it life-giving a-practice is-prevailing". The caste reasoning is therefore attested in two translations.
- **COULD NOT CHECK** the Hindi original.

### R12. KSS 21: "most deluded" against "greatest love"

**CONFIRMED**, with one addition about Śivadāsa.

- **KSS** [S02]: the question is whose delusion was greatest among those "deluded by passion" (*adhiko moho 'nurāgamūḍheṣu*, 12.28.93). The answer is Maṇivarman, "excessively deluded" (*atimūḍhaḥ*), because at the moment for anger he instead died of grief for a wife who had died for love of another man (12.28.94–96).
- **Ś 20** [S06, Sanskrit text p. 55]: the report shows the evaluation only in the answer. In fact the **question itself** is framed the same way: "among the three, who was blind with desire?" (*kaḥ kāmāndhaḥ*). The answer is the husband, "exceedingly blind with desire". Both Sanskrit versions frame the case as delusion.
- **Hindi XX**: Platts (p. 161) asks "whose love, of these three, was greatest?", and the husband "was the deepest lover". Barker's interlinear has "out-of-these-three which most in-love was?" … "Her-husband most in-love was". Attested in two translations.
- Ryder's title for his tale 19 (= KSS 21) asks which "was the most foolish", consistent with the KSS framing.
- **COULD NOT CHECK** the Hindi original.

### R13. Hindi IX: the husband "evinced no affection"

**CONFIRMED in two translations. COULD NOT CHECK the Hindi original.**

- Platts (pp. 86–87): "her husband evinced no affection for her", followed by the verse on the beauty of the cuckoo and of a woman's fidelity.
- Barker's interlinear gloss of the same passage: "But her-husband for-her affection did-not-show". His free translation: "he had ceased to feel any affection for her".
- This makes it less likely that the cold reception is one translator's idiosyncrasy. The point needs a reader of the Hindi (Barker's Devanagari is unreadable in the OCR), because it is where the Hindi departs from Ś 9's *sasnehaṃ āliṅganam* while keeping Ś 9's verse.

### R14. The frame endings in §5

**KSS: CONFIRMED, with one citation correction** [S02].

- The rite in the cremation ground, the monk's request for a full prostration, Vikrama's request to be shown it first, and the beheading are at 12.32.1–19. The boon (that the tales be famous) is at 12.32.22–30. Śiva appears with the gods, says Vikrama was first created as Vikramāditya and now as Trivikramasena, gives the sword Aparājita, and promises Vidyādhara rule (12.32.31–38).
- **Correction**: the Vetāla's warning about the monk's plan comes at the end of tale 24, **12.31.61–74**, not within 12.32.1–19 as the §5 citation implies.
- **Addition**: the Vetāla himself also promises Vikrama the Vidyādhara overlordship that the monk sought (12.32.21), before Śiva does.

**Ś: CONFIRMED** [S06 p. XXIII]. Uhle lists the variants: flower-rain from Indra and the other gods instead of the Gandharvas, Indra's gift of a sword in one manuscript, the king asking for the Vetāla as servant in two, and the Vetāla turned into an indestructible golden man in another. Precision: in Uhle's base text the flower-rain comes from the **Gandharvas**, and Indra appears only as a variant. The report's §5 wording ("Indra and the gods showering flowers") reads as if this were the main text.

**J: CORRECTED.** The report says the Vetāla "reveals himself as an oilman's son and the ascetic as the king's brother, who intends to sacrifice the king to Devī [S06 p. XXII; S07 §4]".

- **What Emeneau's text says** [S07, story 25, translation pp. 145–155]:
  - The Vetāla says he was himself "a householder, an oil-miller by caste" (Sanskrit *tailika*). He is not an oil-miller's son.
  - A wise man (*jñānin*) who lodged with him taught him his knowledge. The wise man charged him to pass it on to his returning disciple, saying "he shall be your brother" (*sa ca tava bhrātā*). "Your" is addressed to the **oil-miller**, and the disciple is Kṣāntiśīla.
  - Kṣāntiśīla's plan, given to him by his teacher, is to offer the king to **Devī** to gain magic power. This part matches the report.
  - The ending continues: Vikrama turns the trick, beheads the ascetic, and offers him to Devī. Devī grants him a hundred years, universal rule, and the magic power of Tāla and Vetāla. The report's §5 does not record this resolution.
- **Where the report's wording comes from.** Uhle's 1881 summary (p. XXII) does say that Jambhaladatta's Vetāla reveals himself as an "oil-miller's son" (*Oelmüllerssohn*) and calls Kṣāntiśīla "the king's brother" (*des Königs Bruder*). Uhle wrote before Emeneau's edition, and the passage read does not say which manuscript or printing he used. Emeneau §4 discusses this backstory only as a Bengali interpolation and does not give these details.
- So the report conflated two sources. Uhle's summary and Emeneau's printed text disagree on both points.

**Hindi: CONFIRMED** [S11, Tale XXV, pp. 180–181]. Indra and the gods appear. Vikram throws the two corpses into the oil-cauldron. Two heroes appear, and he tells them to come when he remembers them.

**Addition**: in the Hindi, Indra offers a boon and Vikram asks that "this story concerning me become famous in the world". The same boon appears in KSS 12.32.24–25, a parallel the report does not note.

| §12.C | "Questions: KSS 21 'most deluded'; the Hindi 'greatest love' — **Opposite moral framing**" | **Not supported as worded** (A2). Barker's printed Hindi asks *in tīnoṃ meṃ se kaunsā adhik kāmī huā?* ("which of these three was most *kāmī*"), and answers *us kā khāvind adhik kāmī huā*. *Kāmī* means "full of desire, passionate, amorous", which is close to Ś's *kāmāndha* ("blind with desire") without its explicit "blind". Both English translators render it as "love". So part of the "flip" may be a **translation effect**, not a feature of the Hindi. The Hindi reasoning does add *prem* ("love"): the husband "immersed in love (*prem meṃ magan*) gave up his life". So the Hindi drops the explicit judgement of delusion and keeps the vocabulary of desire. That is an intermediate position, not an opposite. A Hindi specialist should judge the connotation of *kāmī* in this register (§7.7). |

### 7.8 Is the evidence sufficient to begin deciding Q-401 and Q-410?

**Q-410 (how "25 tales" is counted): sufficient to begin.** The counting facts are verified directly in the sources:

- **KSS**: 24 + a concluding 25th, stated in the text.
- **Ś**: 24 + the conclusion counted as the 25th, from Uhle's colophons, with one extra 25th story in MS *c*.
- **J**: 25 tales plus the frame, with Emeneau's 27-tale hypothesis explicitly his own inference.
- **Hindi**: XXV contains the final riddle and the conclusion, and XXIV is an added second body-entering tale.

Kṣemendra's internal count is not verified beyond its 25 headings.

**Q-401 (primary foundation): sufficient to begin a discussion, not to conclude one.**

The factual differences between traditions that would drive the choice are now verified, at least in part, against primary texts or page images:

- frames and endings (§5);
- the J 10 reversal;
- tale order;
- what is missing from or added to each tradition.

Three things are not yet established:

1. **The Hindi lineage's distinctive meanings are less settled than the report states.** Two of the three "Hindi shifts" (XV, XX) turned out to be partly translation effects (A1, A2). Any option that turns on the Hindi's character should wait for, or explicitly accept the absence of, a specialist reading.
2. **Kṣemendra is essentially unread.** Any claim about its priority or literary value is unsupported (A6).
3. **The report's evaluative language in §§9, 12.D and 13** ("best-verifiable", "lowest burden", "richest", "familiar to Hindi readers") is **not neutral evidence**. The user should read the options against the factual tables in §§5–8, not against those adjectives (A10).

This review does not rank the traditions or recommend an option.
