### 1.2 Method

1. Each tale was read in the Sanskrit from its first verse to the Vetāla's return to the tree, before its record was written. The essential events follow the text's order and cite verses.
2. The question and answer are given in close paraphrase or short transliterated phrases with verse numbers. Quotation is limited to what identifies the passage.
3. **Fact and interpretation are separated.** Everything in "Essential events", "Vetāla's question" and "Vikrama's answer" is a statement of what the text says. Interpretation, where offered, is labelled "*Interpretation*" and kept to the "Stakes and emotional character", "Narrative features" and "Evidence and uncertainty" rows.
4. Variants record only what was actually read in Ś (Uhle) and the Hindi (Platts). A tale not examined in a tradition is described as not examined, never as agreeing.
5. No tale is rated, ranked or recommended. Observations of repetition (§4) are prompts for the user's D-035 judgement, not findings under D-035.

### 1.3 Reading limitations that apply to every record

- The Sanskrit was read by an **AI model, not a credentialed Sanskritist**. The e-text was **not** collated with the 1915 print.
- Uhle and Platts were read through **OCR**. Uhle's romanised Sanskrit loses diacritics, so names in the Ś column are given in normalised form where certain and flagged where not.
- "Same answer" in a variant row means the same person or principle is chosen in the passage read; it does not mean the tellings are identical.

## 3. Evidence records, TC-001 to TC-024

### TC-001 — KSS 1: Vajramukuṭa, Buddhiśarīra and Padmāvatī

| | |
|---|---|
| **Identification** | KSS 12.8.59–196 (Tawney/Penzer ch. 75). Tale opens 12.8.59; question 12.8.186–188; answer 12.8.190–195; Vetāla departs by illusion at 12.8.196; Vikrama goes to the śiṃśapā tree to retrieve him at 12.9.1. |
| **Essential events** | (1) In Vārāṇasī, King Pratāpamukuṭa's son Vajramukuṭa hunts with his friend Buddhiśarīra, the minister's son (59–65). (2) At a lake in a forest he sees a girl of divine beauty; they are mutually smitten; she makes silent signs (a lotus at her ear, a gesture with her teeth, a lotus on her head, a hand on her heart) and leaves; the prince does not understand them (66–76). (3) Lovesick at home, he despairs; the minister's son decodes the signs: she lives in King Karṇotpala's kingdom (Kaliṅga), is the daughter of the ivory-carver (*dantaghāṭaka*) Saṅgrāmavardhana, is named Padmāvatī, and her life is his (77–86). (4) The two ride to Kaliṅga and lodge with an old woman who was Padmāvatī's nurse; bribed, she carries the prince's message (87–99). (5) Padmāvatī twice "rebukes" the go-between: slapping her cheeks with ten camphor-white fingers, then striking her breast with three lac-red fingers; the minister's son reads these as "wait ten moonlit nights" and "wait three nights of my period" (100–114). (6) On a third visit Padmāvatī lowers the nurse from a window into the garden on a rope-seat on the pretext of a rampaging elephant; the minister's son reads this as the route (115–124). The prince enters by it, and they marry by the *gāndharva* rite and live together secretly (125–131). (7) When Padmāvatī learns that the minister's son, not the prince, read her signs, she sends him food; he feeds some to a dog, which dies. He tells the prince she tried to poison him so as to have the prince to herself (132–149). (8) A cry is heard that a young son of the king has died (152). The minister's son devises a plan: the prince makes Padmāvatī drunk, brands her thigh with a heated trident and takes her jewels (153–161). (9) Disguised as an ascetic and his pupil, the two have the pupil try to sell her necklace; the police arrest him; the "ascetic" tells the city magistrate that at night in the cremation ground he saw a circle of *yoginīs* offer a prince's heart to Bhairava, and that he marked one who tried to snatch his rosary with his trident and took her necklace (162–177). (10) King Karṇotpala, finding the trident mark and the necklace, concludes she is the *ḍākinī* (e-text *ḍakinyā*, 180) who devoured his son, and banishes her from the city (178–181). (11) She stays alive in the forest; at dusk the two, now undisguised, take her on horseback to their own kingdom, where the prince lives happily with her (182–184). (12) Her father, believing her eaten by wild beasts, dies of grief, and her mother follows him in death (185). |
| **Vetāla's question** | Of the couple's death (*daṃpatyor etayor vadhāt*), what sin falls to the minister's son, what to the prince, or what to Padmāvatī (12.8.186–187)? The penalty formula follows: if Vikrama knows and does not answer, his head will burst into a hundred pieces (12.8.188). |
| **Vikrama's answer** | None of the three is guilty; the sin is King Karṇotpala's (12.8.190). The Vetāla objects that the three did the deed: are crows to blame when geese eat the rice? (12.8.191). Vikrama explains: for the minister's son it was his master's business and so no sin (192); Padmāvatī and the prince, burned by the fire of love's arrows, were acting for their own ends and are not to be judged (*avicārārhāv adoṣau*, 193); Karṇotpala, untrained in political science, did not investigate his subjects by spies and could not read rogues' conduct or signs, so the sin is his for acting without inquiry (194–195). |
| **Dilemma (plain language)** | Two innocent parents die because of a deception that ruined their daughter's name. Who bears the guilt: the schemer who planned it, the lover who carried it out, the daughter who consented to elope, or the ruler who condemned her without investigation? |
| **Characters and relationships** | Prince Vajramukuṭa and his friend and counsellor Buddhiśarīra (minister's son; prince and adviser); Padmāvatī (lover, then secret wife); the old nurse (go-between, motivated by gifts); King Karṇotpala of Kaliṅga (ruler and judge); Padmāvatī's parents, the ivory-carver Saṅgrāmavardhana and his wife (the victims). |
| **Stakes and emotional character** | Textual: lovesickness (77–79), the nurse's humiliation (102), an apparent poisoning (146–149), a woman branded and banished (160, 181), parents dying of grief (185). The narrator calls Padmāvatī *dhūrtā*, "crafty" (134). Interpretation: the tone is largely one of cleverness and intrigue; the parents' deaths come in a single verse at the end. |
| **Narrative features** | Coded signs and their decoding drive the plot (signs at 72–74, 101, 111, 118–121; decoding at 81–85, 104–105, 113, 123). A contest of wits between Padmāvatī and the minister's son (the poisoned food, 142–149). A false story within the story (the cremation-ground *yoginī* tale told by the disguised minister's son, 172–177). The question concerns an outcome (the parents' deaths) that occupies only the last verse of the narrative. The Vetāla challenges the first answer and Vikrama justifies it (191–195), an exchange that recurs in TC-004 and TC-017. |
| **Variants (Ś and Hindi)** | **Ś 1** (Uhle, question and answer through OCR): "whose is the sin?"; answer: the king's, "through lack of inquiry" (*avicāreṇa*). Same answer, reasoning compressed. **Hindi I** (Platts): the sprite asks "to which of those **four** does guilt attach?" (the four include the kotwal) and the penalty is to be "cast into hell"; answer: the king, for expelling her inconsiderately; the minister's son did his duty, the kotwal obeyed orders, the princess attained her object. Same answer; a fourth party is added. |
| **Evidence and uncertainty** | Textual fact: all events above, read in the GRETIL Sanskrit. Translation aid: Penzer vol. 6 ch. 75, for the question only. *Uncertainty:* whether Padmāvatī actually poisoned the food is reported only through the dog's death (146) and the minister's son's explanation (148–149); the narrator does not independently confirm her intent. The king's son who died (152) is not otherwise introduced; it is not stated how he died. |

### TC-002 — KSS 2: Mandāravatī and the three brahman youths

| | |
|---|---|
| **Identification** | KSS 12.9.1–42 (ch. 76). Return to the tree and recapture 1–5; tale 5–35; question 36–37; answer 38–40; Vetāla returns 41; narrator's maxim 42. |
| **Essential events** | (1) At the brahman settlement Brahmasthala on the Kālindī (Yamunā), the learned brahman Agnisvāmin has a surpassingly beautiful daughter, Mandāravatī (5–7). (2) Three equally accomplished brahman youths from Kānyakubja each ask for her and each would sooner die than see her given to another; her father, fearing that two would die, gives her to none, and the three stay on, gazing at her day and night (8–11). (3) She dies suddenly of a burning fever (12). The three, grief-stricken, cremate her (13). One builds a hut on the cremation ground, makes her ashes his bed and lives on alms; the second takes her bones to the Ganges; the third becomes an ascetic and wanders (14–15). (4) At the village Vakrolaka the wandering ascetic is a guest in a brahman's house. A child cries without stopping; the mistress of the house angrily throws it into the fire, where it is burned to ashes (16–19). The guest, horrified, refuses to eat in what he thinks a brahman-demon's house; the householder revives the child from the ashes with a life-giving spell from a book (20–23). (5) That night the ascetic steals the book and travels back to the cremation ground, where the bone-bearer has also just returned (24–28). He opens the hut, reads the spell over the ashes, and Mandāravatī rises alive, more radiant than before, "as if made of gold" after passing through fire (29–32). (6) All three, overcome with desire, quarrel: one claims her as won by the power of his spell, another by the power of the sacred bathing-place, the third because he guarded the ashes by his austerity (33–35). |
| **Vetāla's question** | Settle their dispute: whose wife does the girl properly become (*kasyaiṣā kanyā bhāryopapadyate*, 12.9.36)? Penalty formula 37. |
| **Vikrama's answer** | The one who revived her by the spell, after hardship, is her **father**, because he did a father's work (38); the one who took her bones to the Ganges is counted her **son** (39); the one who embraced her bed of ashes and practised austerity in the cremation ground out of love is called her **husband**, since he acted as a deeply devoted lover would (39–40). |
| **Dilemma (plain language)** | Three men each did something essential to bring a dead woman back. Whose claim to her as wife is valid? The answer reclassifies each act by the relationship it resembles (giving life = father, carrying bones to the Ganges = son, devoted watch = husband). |
| **Characters and relationships** | Mandāravatī (the contested woman; she does not speak or choose); her father Agnisvāmin; the three suitors (rivals, then claimants); the householder of Vakrolaka and his wife (owners of the reviving spell). |
| **Stakes and emotional character** | Textual: unrequited longing (11), sudden death and grief (12–13), a horrifying act of a mother burning a crying child (18–19), the guest's revulsion (20), wonder at revival (23, 31–32), then a quarrel driven by desire (33). Interpretation: the tale moves from devotion to an abrupt shift into rivalry at the moment of revival. |
| **Narrative features** | Three parallel paths (stay, pilgrimage, wandering) that converge at the cremation ground. An embedded shocking episode (the child thrown into the fire) supplies the means of revival. Revival through a stolen book. The answer depends on mapping actions onto kinship roles; it is a classification puzzle rather than a merit comparison. The tale closes with a narrator's maxim on the king's steadfastness (42). |
| **Variants (Ś and Hindi)** | **Ś 2** (Uhle, OCR; the death, cremation and answer were read): the girl dies of a **snakebite**, with verses on fatal times and body-places of snakebite; at the cremation **one suitor enters the pyre and dies with her**, the second builds a hut on the cremation ground, the third becomes an ascetic and obtains the reviving book as in KSS (the child thrown into the fire); the reviver restores the girl **and the suitor who died with her**. Answer: the reviver is her father; **the one who died with her is her brother** ("born together with her"); the one who stayed at the place is her husband. **Material variant:** there is no Ganges bone-bearer and no "son"; the roles are father, brother, husband. **Hindi II** (Platts, ending): answer as in KSS: the one who built the hut and stayed with the ashes is the husband; the one who preserved the bones "occupied the place of her son"; the reviver is "as it were, her father". How she died in the Hindi was not checked. |
| **Evidence and uncertainty** | Textual fact: as above. *Uncertainty:* "is counted (*mataḥ*) her son" and "is called (*ucyate*) her husband" are Vikrama's classifications; the text gives no reaction from the woman or the claimants. Whether the householder's wife acted in a fit of rage or as a deliberate demonstration is not stated; the text says only *krudhā*, "in anger" (18). |

### TC-003 — KSS 3: the parrot and the mynah (are men or women worse?)

| | |
|---|---|
| **Identification** | KSS 12.10.1–95 (ch. 77). Frame 1–4; outer tale 4–15 and 89–91; the mynah's tale 15–46; the parrot's tale 47–88; question 92; answer 93–94; Vetāla returns 95. |
| **Essential events** | **Outer tale.** In Pāṭaliputra, Vikramakesarin (called *nṛpati* at 5 and *nṛpātmaja*/*rājaputra* at 7 and 13–14) owns a learned parrot, Vidagdhacūḍāmaṇi, born on earth through a curse; on its advice he marries the Magadha princess Candraprabhā, who owns an equally learned mynah, Somikā (5–9). The parrot proposes union to the mynah; she refuses because men are wicked and ungrateful; he retorts that women are wicked and cruel. They wager (the loser becomes the other's slave, or wife) and take the dispute to the prince in his father's court (10–14). **The mynah's tale (men).** Dhanadatta, a merchant's son of Kāmandikā, squanders his inheritance through gambling and bad company and leaves home in shame (16–19). In Candanapura a merchant takes him in and gives him his only daughter Ratnāvalī with wealth (20–22). Restless, Dhanadatta leaves with her and an old woman; in a forest, pleading fear of robbers, he takes her jewels and throws both women into a pit (23–28). The old woman dies; Ratnāvalī, caught in creepers, survives, climbs out wounded and returns to her parents, telling them robbers attacked and carried off her husband (29–35). When Dhanadatta, having gambled away the money, comes back to extract more from his father-in-law, she falls at his feet and tells him the cover story she gave; he is welcomed (36–43). One night he kills her as she sleeps in his lap, takes her jewels and flees (44–45). **The parrot's tale (women).** Vasudattā, a merchant's daughter of Harṣavatī, married to Samudradatta of Tāmraliptī, takes a secret lover while staying at her father's house (48–54). When her husband arrives, she feigns sleep and goes out at night to meet her lover; a thief who has broken in follows her (55–62). In a garden she finds the lover hanging from a tree, executed by the city guards who took him for a thief (63–64). She grieves, takes him down, adorns and kisses the corpse; a Vetāla entering the corpse bites off her nose (65–68). She returns home, cries that her husband has cut off her nose; her father has the husband bound, and he stays silent (74–78). The king orders the husband's execution; the thief, who saw everything, tells the king, offering the nose still in the corpse's mouth as proof (79–85). The king frees the husband, cuts off her ears and banishes her, fines her father, and makes the thief chief of police (86–88). **Close.** The parrot becomes the Gandharva Citraratha and the mynah the apsaras Tilottamā, their curses ended; the dispute is left undecided in the court (89–91). |
| **Vetāla's question** | Tell me: are men wicked, or are women? (*kiṃ pāpāḥ puruṣāḥ kimuta striyaḥ*, 12.10.92), with the penalty formula. |
| **Vikrama's answer** | **Women** are wicked (93). A man of that kind may occasionally be of bad conduct somewhere, but women are generally, everywhere and always, of that kind (*prāyaḥ sarvatra sadā*, 94). No further reasoning is given. |
| **Dilemma (plain language)** | Two stories of betrayal within marriage, one by a husband, one by a wife: which sex is worse? The answer is a categorical generalisation, not a reasoned verdict on the two cases. |
| **Characters and relationships** | Two cursed celestials as parrot and mynah (disputing a would-be couple); Vikramakesarin and Candraprabhā (their owners and judge); in the mynah's tale, Dhanadatta and his wife Ratnāvalī, and her parents; in the parrot's tale, Vasudattā, her husband Samudradatta, her lover, her father, the thief-witness and the king. |
| **Stakes and emotional character** | Textual: attempted murder and murder of a devoted wife (28, 45); grief and horror at a corpse (65–70); a husband falsely condemned to death (81); judicial mutilation and banishment (87). The narrator comments on wicked men (27) and the thief on women's hearts (72); the parrot's tale closes "so women are by nature crooked" (88). Interpretation: the tale is morally harsh and macabre, with explicit gendered moralising (see N9). |
| **Narrative features** | A frame within the frame: a debate between two birds, each telling an exemplary tale (two embedded stories). The supernatural intrudes in the parrot's tale (a Vetāla in the hanged man's corpse, 68), an echo of the outer frame. | **Variants (Ś and Hindi)** | **Ś 3** (OCR): the outer pair is King Rūpasena of Bhogavatī with the parrot Vidagdhacūḍāmaṇi and a Magadha princess with the mynah Madanamañjarī; the birds become Vidyādharas at the end. This section summarises the KSS evidence across the 24 records. It describes; it does not evaluate. Repetitions are noted as prompts for the user's judgement under D-035, not as rulings.

### 4.3 Characters and relationships

- **Master and servant:** TC-004, 007, 012, 017. In 004 and 017 the king is judged greater because his act exceeds his role; in 007 the servant is judged greater.
- **Spouses, lovers and rival suitors:** TC-001, 002, 003, 005, 006, 009, 010, 012, 014, 015, 021, 024. Women are the occasion of the question in most of these. Women speak and act in several (Padmāvatī's signs, 001; Madanasenā's promise, 010; Ratnavatī's choice, 014; Anaṅgamañjarī's suicide attempt, 021), but the questions are mostly asked about the men.
- **Parents and children:** TC-004 (a father sacrifices his son), 019 (three fathers for one son), 020 (parents hand over their son), 023 (bereaved parents deceived), 024 (cross-generational marriages).
- **Siblings and kin:** TC-006 (husband and brother), 008 and 022 (brothers).
- **Supernatural figures:** goddesses who stop a suicide or restore the dead (004, 006, 016, 021), Bhairava (014), Śiva in dreams (019), Vidyādharas (012, 013, 016), a Daitya princess (007), Rākṣasas and brahman-demons (005, 012, 020), Garuḍa and the Nāgas (016), cursed celestials (003, 012).

### 4.4 Stakes and emotional character (as present in the text)

- **Deaths and revivals.** Most tales put life at stake. Divine or magical revival reverses deaths in TC-002, 004, 006, 014, 016 and 021; in 022 the revival itself kills.
- **Self-sacrifice and sacrifice of others:** TC-004 (a son, a daughter's death of grief, a wife's immolation, self-beheading), 006 (two self-beheadings), 016 (self-offering to Garuḍa), 020 (a willing child victim held down by his parents). **Two tales sacrifice a brahman boy for a king with the parents' consent (004 and 020).**
- **Suicide attempts and deaths of grief:** attempts or near-attempts in 006, 012 (the merchant, stopped by a heavenly voice), 013 (Harisvāmin, dissuaded by kin), 016, 021, 022; deaths of grief or heartbreak in 001 (the parents), 004 (the daughter), 012 (the minister), 017 (the king, of lovesickness) and 021 (the lover and the husband; Anaṅgamañjarī dies of joy).
- **Humour and irony** (*interpretation*): the comic tests of 008 and 011, the trickery of 015, the insolent disciple in 023, and the footprint promise in 024. Report §9's note of humour in 008 and 015 is supported by the events.
- **Gendered and moralising material** relevant to N9: the answer of 003; the thief's remark in 003 (12.10.72); the answer of 014 (12.21.60); the narrator's aside in 021 (12.28.81); caste reasoning in 009 (12.16.35–38); the line on a helpless woman in 024 (12.31.50). Ś and the Hindi add further maxims (Ś 3, 9, 13; Hindi XIX).

### 4.5 Narrative structures

- **Embedded tales:** TC-003 (two exemplary tales inside a debate), 001 (a false cremation-ground story told by the disguised minister's son), 012 (a curse-story told by its victim), 015 (Ilā and Budha), 016 (Kadrū and Vinatā).
- **Unresolved threads in KSS:** TC-013 (the abducted wife is never found), 015 (Mṛgāṅkavatī's fate after her flight), **020 (whether the boy is killed is not narrated)**.
- **Triple or repeated structures:** 008 and 011 (three tests), 010 (the road out and back), 007 and 012 (the same journey made twice, the second time by the king).
- **Marvellous places reached through water:** 007 and 012 (undersea cities, return through a tank).
- **Disguise and trickery:** 001, 014 (the king calls himself a thief), 015.
- **Illusion and nested lives:** 018.
- **Frame-level events inside tale chapters:** 018 (many corpses, 12.25.3–7); 024 (the Vetāla's warning and the move to the frame's climax, 12.31.61–75).

### 4.6 Observations bearing on repetition (for the user's D-035 judgement)

These are observations from the KSS evidence, not decisions.

1. **Reaction riddles:** four tales (TC-012, 014, 020, 023), plus the causal riddle of 018. TC-020 and TC-023 both turn on a laugh or dance at a scene of death; TC-014 and TC-020 both on a laugh at the moment of death.
2. **King against servant:** TC-004 and TC-017 use the same argument (a servant's duty against a king's exceptional act); TC-007 asks the same kind of question with a different argument.
3. **Child sacrifice for a king:** TC-004 and TC-020.
4. **Suitors:** TC-002, 005 and 009 each have several claimants with different contributions or skills. Their bases differ: kinship roles (002), risk of life (005), caste and usefulness (009). The catalogue's §6.2 pair (005 and 009) is now comparable at the level of events.
5. **Skill that revives the dead:** TC-002 (revives a girl) and TC-022 (revives a lion that kills its makers).
6. **Refinement contests:** TC-008 and TC-011 are close in form and tone.
