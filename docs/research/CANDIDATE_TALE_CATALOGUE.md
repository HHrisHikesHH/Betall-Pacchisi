## 1. How to read this catalogue

### 1.1 What the pool is, and is not

The pool is **the candidate set identified by the traditions and evidence the report examined**: Somadeva (KSS), Kṣemendra (BKM), Śivadāsa (Ś), Jambhaladatta (J), the Braj/Hindi lineage, and Ryder's English translation. **It is not historically exhaustive.** Other witnesses (Newari, Tibetan, Bhaviṣya Purāṇa, other vernaculars) were not read, or were read only in part (report §4.F, §15), and a tale's absence from an examined edition is **not** evidence that it never existed in that tradition (for example, J's Bengali text lacks two tales that its Nepali manuscript has).

### 1.2 Candidate IDs

`TC-###` identifiers are **catalogue labels only**. They are not final tale numbers and imply no order, rank or preference. TC-001 to TC-024 follow Somadeva's order **only because it is the one tradition read tale by tale in a verse-numbered e-text** (report §7). That is an indexing convenience, not a source hierarchy (Q-408 is open). TC-025 to TC-027 are the three Jambhaladatta-only tales. **The frame has no TC number** (§8).

### 1.3 Evidence labels

The report's labels are used as they stand: **VERIFIED — primary text**, **VERIFIED — scholarly source**, **PROVISIONAL**, **UNVERIFIED**, **DISPUTED**, **NOT ACCESSIBLE** (report §1). "VERIFIED — primary text" means the specific passage was read, **never** that the whole text was. No label has been upgraded.

### 1.4 Standard evidence basis (applies to every record unless a record says otherwise)

| Column | What was actually done (report §2.2, §7, §15) |
|---|---|
| **KSS** | Each tale's verse range, opening, the Vetāla's question and Vikrama's answer were read in the GRETIL e-text (by an AI model, not a credentialed Sanskritist). Most of each tale's narrative body was **not** read. The e-text was **not** checked against the 1915 print. *VERIFIED — primary text, passage-level.* |
| **BKM** | **PROVISIONAL.** Same-number correspondence with KSS is assumed from Emeneau's shared numbering and the 1901 contents (25 *Vetālakathā* headings). Only two tales were checked, on page images by the reviewer: BKM 10 (matches KSS 10's names) and the Unmādinī story (KSS 17) at about the 17th heading. |
| **Ś** | Identified from openings, questions, answers and colophons in Uhle's text, through OCR. *VERIFIED — primary text (OCR).* Most narrative bodies not read. |
| **J** | Titles from Emeneau's contents. Content was read **only** for the frame, J 10 and J 25. *VERIFIED — scholarly edition (titles); content mostly unread.* **N3:** J 13 and J 21–23 have since been read in full ([JAMBHALADATTA_ADDITIONAL_TALES_EVIDENCE.md](JAMBHALADATTA_ADDITIONAL_TALES_EVIDENCE.md)). |
| **Hindi** | Platts's English translation: openings and endings of all 25 tales read. The Hindi itself was read only in four passages of Barker 1855 (IX, XV, XX, XXIV), on page images, by AI readers. *VERIFIED — translation; Hindi wording PROVISIONAL.* |
| **Ryder** | Contents compared with KSS. *VERIFIED — primary text (contents).* |

A record's **Evidence** row therefore only names *deviations* from this standard.

**Report inconsistencies on J coverage (not resolved here).** Report §2.2 lists J's frame and story 10 as read, §7 says J content was read "only for J 10", and §15 says "J 10 and J 25". The report also gives J's answers for KSS 7 and KSS 9 (§7) without a documented reading. The catalogue treats J answers other than J 10 and J 25 as per-report and undocumented.

### 1.5 Titles and descriptions

The **working title** of each record is the English title given in Penzer's contents for the Somadeva tale (the "identifying description" in report §7), shortened where noted. It is a **label for identification, not a project title** and not a synopsis. Where the report establishes no more than a title, the record says so. **Nothing has been inferred from a title** except where a record states it as a flagged observation.

### 1.6 Match codes (report §7)

**E** = same story (same core plot and riddle). **P** = partial (same core, different setting, names, or reasoning; the report applies P where an ending or answer differs, as in KSS 10). **U** = uncertain.

### 2.3 What the pool size means for Q-411 (preliminary)

The pool has 27 families, against a target of 25 tales. That leaves **a margin of two**, so the choice is not wide, and it is narrowed by whatever the user decides about the J-only tales and any tale that research shows to be unsuitable. Two other facts bear on the count and are for the user's judgement: in both Somadeva and Śivadāsa the 25th *unit* is the frame's conclusion, not a narrated tale, and TC-024 is a riddle that is unanswered by design (§6.4).

## 4. Jambhaladatta-only candidates (TC-025 to TC-027)

**These three are recorded because the comparison found them in no other Sanskrit version examined. That is a fact about the examined editions, not a reason to select them.** The report did **not read their content**: Emeneau's contents list supplied the titles, and the records below were written from titles only. **N3 (2026-11-18) has since read all three in full**, with the Newari parallels: see [JAMBHALADATTA_ADDITIONAL_TALES_EVIDENCE.md](JAMBHALADATTA_ADDITIONAL_TALES_EVIDENCE.md). The rows marked "N3" below summarise that evidence; nothing in them selects or ranks a tale.

What is established about all three:

- They are numbered **J 21, 22 and 23** in Emeneau's printed (Bengali-recension) text. *VERIFIED — scholarly edition (titles).*
- Emeneau hypothesises that Jambhaladatta's original had **27** tales: the 24 common ones plus these 3. The Bengali and Nepali recensions each cut back to 25, differently. This is an *attributed scholarly hypothesis*, not an established fact (report §4.D, §6).
- Emeneau notes that the Newari version is "obviously based on Jambhaladatta's version" because it contains **J 21 and 22**. *VERIFIED — scholarly source.* **N3:** J 23 is **not** in the Newari version, whose tale 23 Emeneau identifies with KSS 14 (TC-014) (pp. xv–xvi); Jørgensen says that tale differs substantially in content from *f* 14 and Ś 13 (p. 214), so the identification is not a close-parallel claim.
- **N3:** **J 22 and J 23 are missing from the Bengali manuscripts H and O** (a long lacuna); Emeneau's text of them rests on **P alone, with the 1873 print V**. J 21 is in H, O and P. All three are in the Nepali manuscript W, but Emeneau reports only W's proper names for them (Intro. §3, pp. xii–xiv).
- Emeneau's judgement of the J style is "monotonously bald" in places, with careless riddle details; Aufrecht (via Uhle) judged it more elegant than Śivadāsa. The style judgement is *DISPUTED* (report §4.D); the riddle carelessness is Emeneau's report (§6). Neither judgement was tested against these three tales.
- The edition carries a 1934 copyright notice. Rights status is **RESEARCH REQUIRED**, and his translation must not be reproduced (report §11).

## 5. Special cases

### 5.A The Hindi body-entering plot: XXII and XXIV (TC-023)

**Status: SETTLED by the user's editorial decision.** **User's editorial decision (2026-11-17, an application of D-035 to TC-023; not a new decision and not a change to D-035):** the Hindi tellings XXII and XXIV count as **one candidate family and one slot, TC-023**, with **two distinct recorded source variants**. This is **not** a selection of TC-023 for the final collection; **Q-411 remains open**. The analysis below is preserved as the record of the evidence. The two variants are (a) **Hindi XXII** (Ś-type: the aged brahman Nārāyan of Biswapur, motive enjoyment, announces he has become a yogī, sermon and pilgrimage, no death stated; he himself weeps and laughs) and (b) **Hindi XXIV** (KSS/Kaliṅga-type: the dead prodigy, the cremation ground, the ascetic's entry, the father who becomes a *bairāg*; the father laughs and weeps). All their documented differences are in the comparison document §§3–9 and in the table below.

**What the question was.** The Hindi has 25 numbered tales, and two of them (XXII and XXIV) tell a body-entering plot: an aged man who knows the art takes a younger body. D-035 says a repeated plot is a separate tale only if it has a **genuinely different central dilemma** or is **substantially different as a story**. The report establishes some, not all, of what is needed to apply that test. **This catalogue does not decide it.**

**What is known** (report §3 item 7, §6, §8.5; Platts and Barker; labels as in the report).

| | Hindi XXII | Hindi XXIV |
|---|---|---|
| Setting | Biswapur (Viśvapura), King Bidagdha, the brahman Nārāyan | Kaliṅga, the brahman Yajña Sharmā and his son |
| Whose reaction is the riddle | The **brahman** (not yet an ascetic) weeps and laughs | The boy's **father**, an onlooker, laughs and then weeps |
| Order of the reaction | Weeps, then laughs | Laughs first, then weeps |
| The riddle's question | "Why did he weep, and why did he laugh?" | Why did the father first laugh and then weep? |
| Answer | Wept recalling his mother's love and his youth; laughed at succeeding in his art (Platts p. 167) | The father laughed at seeing the yogī enter his son's body, and so learning the art; he wept, thinking that he too must one day leave his own body (Platts pp. 175–176; Barker p. 359) |
| What follows | Not recorded in the report | The father "lost all desire for the world" (Platts) / "became a Bairāg" (Barker's rendering, per report §8.5; the AI reading of the printed Hindi is *bairāg huā*) |
| Closest relatives | Ś 22 (same Viśvapura setting; yogī weeps and laughs); KSS 23 in its riddle's subject (the ascetic himself) | KSS 23 in setting (Kaliṅga) |

KSS 23 differs from both: Kaliṅga, the city Śobhāvatī, the brahman Yajñasoma and his son Devasoma who dies at sixteen; the ascetic weeps at leaving the old body that had served him so long and **dances** for joy at gaining youth (12.30.42–47). The report concludes that XXIV is "a variant with a new riddle subject, not a duplicate" (§8.5). **That conclusion is about the difference between XXIV and the others. It is not a finding under D-035**, which asks a different question (a different *dilemma* or a *substantially different story*).

**Shared plot elements (established; corrected after the full reading in [HINDI_XXII_XXIV_COMPARISON.md](HINDI_XXII_XXIV_COMPARISON.md)).** An aged man who knows the art of entering another body takes a younger one. The *dead boy at a cremation ground* is established only for KSS 23 and Hindi XXIV; in Hindi XXII (and Ś 22) the youth's death is not mentioned, and XXII's entrant is an aged brahman householder who then announces he has become a yogī.

**Different essential events and dilemma (partly established).**

- The riddle's *subject* changes: the brahman in XXII, an onlooking father in XXIV.
- The *reasoning* changes: per the full reading, XXII's brahman wept recalling his mother's love and his youth and from attachment to the body, and laughed at succeeding in his art and entering a new body (Platts p. 167; Barker p. 343); XXIV's father laughed at seeing the art succeed and wept at the thought of his own mortality. **Note:** both answers turn partly on the art's success. The report's prose in §8.5 generalised the "young body" reasoning to XXII, which the Hindi does not support (corrected in the report; see the comparison §9). XXII and XXIV may be closer in dilemma than the riddle subjects alone suggest.
- XXIV has events XXII lacks (the boy's death, funeral and revival, the father's renunciation, *bairāg*, narrated in one clause), and XXII has events XXIV lacks (the announcement "I am a yogī", a long sermon, a pilgrimage). Full comparison: [HINDI_XXII_XXIV_COMPARISON.md](HINDI_XXII_XXIV_COMPARISON.md).
- The *setting* and *characters* differ.

**What cannot be established with the present evidence.**

1. *(Resolved by the full reading.)* Both Hindi tales have now been read in full in Platts and in Barker's printed Hindi (page images, AI reading). Forbes's Hindi, the 1805 print and any Braj text remain unread.
2. *(Resolved.)* XXIV narrates no weeping or laughing by the ascetic; only the father reacts.
3. Whether the **dilemma** in XXIV differs from XXII's in a way a reader would feel as a different problem, or whether it is a second answer to the same enigma. In both, a person in the story reacts in two ways to the same event, and the answer explains the pair; the explanation concerns different people and different thoughts.
4. The **reason** the Hindi tells it twice (PROVISIONAL; report §8.5 suggests that it keeps Śivadāsa's XXII and adds a Kaliṅga-setting variant as XXIV, bringing its narrated tales to 25).
5. Whether Hindi XXIV's wording reads as the report's AI reading suggests (a specialist question).

**Possible ways to see it (as framed before the decision; the user chose the first).**

- **One tale (TC-023), XXIV treated as a variant.** The plot is the same, and XXIV supplies an alternative riddle subject and answer, which D-034 would let the project use or combine.
- **Two tales.** The riddle's subject and the reflection it draws (the ascetic's own farewell to his past against an onlooker's recognition of his own mortality) might be judged distinct dilemmas, although both answers also turn on the art's success. Costs: a closely related plot in a 25-tale collection that needs variety (D-035). Benefit: it would let the 25 be filled entirely from the 24 core families plus this one, without relying on the unread J-only tales (§2.3, §6.4). Another cost, if kept as one tale: the pool shrinks to 27 and the margin to two.
- **Either way, the XXIV material is available as a recorded variant** if the user chooses one tale.

**Evidence (done):** the full reading and comparison are in [HINDI_XXII_XXIV_COMPARISON.md](HINDI_XXII_XXIV_COMPARISON.md). A specialist Hindi/Braj reading of XXIV and XXII (and a Sanskritist's check) remains recommended for the variant record, not for the classification.

### 6.1 Reaction riddles ("why did X weep, laugh or dance?")

**Other frame witnesses.** Ryder renders KSS's frame (names in English; he says twelve years where the e-text reads ten). BKM's frame was not read; its manuscripts call the king Vikramasena and the edition Trivikrama° (Emeneau). The Newari, Tibetan and Bhaviṣya Purāṇa frames were not examined.

**What this means for the catalogue.** The frame choice affects the king's identity, the ascetic's motive, the Vetāla's backstory, the closing theology, and also **how the tales' own details fit together**: the report cautions that frames and tales "were shaped together" (§13, Option 5). For example, the Hindi Vetāla's warning in the prehistory and the KSS Vetāla's warning after TC-024 are different structures. The catalogue does not resolve this. It only flags that any tale's use under D-034 must be checked against the frame chosen.

**Research gaps for the frame.** J's frame beyond the passages read; BKM's frame; the unresolved relationship of Uhle's and Emeneau's descriptions of J's ending; the penalty and cemetery details in Ś and J (report §15).

## 9. Assessing the balance of the eventual collection (PROPOSED)

> **Status: PROPOSED working method, not an approved decision.** The user has not approved a weighting system, a selection formula or a priority order, and none is introduced here. Nothing below scores or ranks a candidate. The lenses are suggestions for how to read the catalogue. If the user wants any of them adopted, that is a decision for DECISIONS.md.

D-035 requires "meaningful variety across dilemmas, characters, conflicts, emotions and forms of storytelling". The catalogue's job is to make *excessive repetition* and *gaps* visible before the user chooses.

### 9.1 Proposed lenses

| Lens | What to record for a candidate (in plain words, not a score) | What the pool allows now |
|---|---|---|
| **A. Type of central dilemma or riddle** | The question form (for example: a superlative judgement "who was most…"; a "why did X react so?" riddle; an allocation "to whom should…"; a relational or kinship puzzle; an unanswered riddle; a judgement of blame) and what the answer turns on. | The report quotes a question for only six core tales (TC-010, 014, 016, 021, 023, 024) and records an answer, or its absence, for about fifteen (TC-007–011, 013–019, 021, 023, 024). Question forms seen: superlative or comparative (TC-007, 010, 016, 021); "why did X react?" (TC-014, 023); allocation (TC-019, by title); blame (TC-013); kinship, unanswered (TC-024). Incomplete. **N1:** the KSS question and answer are now recorded for all 24 core families ([SOMADEVA_TALE_EVIDENCE.md](SOMADEVA_TALE_EVIDENCE.md) §2 and §4.1). |
| **B. Characters and relationships** | Who the central figures are (rank, calling, gender) and the relationship that creates the dilemma (spouses, lovers, rival suitors, master and servant, parent and child, sovereign and subject). | By titles only. The report notes recurring types (kings, ministers, merchants, brahmans, thieves, courtesans, Vidyādharas, ascetics; women often drive the dilemma, §9). |
| **C. Conflict and stakes** | What is at risk (life, honour, love, fortune, the soul) and what drives the conflict (loyalty, desire, duty, deceit, fate). | By titles only. |
| **D. Emotional experience** | The experience the reader may have (wonder, dread, humour, grief, tenderness, unease, delight), described as an observation, not a promised effect. | The report notes humour and play in TC-008 and TC-015 only. The rest is unassessed. |
| **E. Narrative form and structure** | A single plot, a tale inside a tale, a long journey or a short scene, a riddle with a twist, an answer, no answer. | Verse lengths in KSS give a crude scale of length (TC-004: 132 verses; TC-012: 171; TC-016: 207; TC-009: 39) and say nothing about quality. |
| **F. Setting and atmosphere** | Where and when it happens (court, forest, city, cremation ground, sea, a Vidyādhara realm) and the atmosphere (the experience's atmosphere is story-led under D-026; settings here are the tales' own). | Only a few settings are recorded (Kaliṅga, Viśvapura, the sea in TC-007, Benares in TC-001's opening). |
| **G. Variants that change how a character or event is understood** | Where a variant changes the reader's understanding of a person or act (a rejected wife; a hero's practice grounded in caste; a witness who renounces). | Recorded for TC-010, 016, 021, 023, plus minor ones (§5.C). |

### 9.2 How the catalogue could be used for this (a possible process, not a mandate)

1. **Complete the facts first.** For each candidate, record the recorded question, answer and essential events (N1), so that lenses A to G are filled from evidence and not from titles.
2. **Tag, in words.** For each candidate, write a short plain-language tag per lens. No numbers, no weights.
3. **Lay the pool out.** Arrange all 27 in a grid by lens. Clusters of near-identical tags show **repetition**; empty regions show **gaps**.
4. **Test repetition against D-035.** Where two candidates share a tag on lenses A and B and C, ask whether their central dilemmas are genuinely different. §6 starts that list.
5. **Treat variants as options.** Where a candidate has variants (§5.C), record how each variant would change its tags. This is where D-034 widens the choice.
6. **Present options, not a verdict.** The user decides which 25, and whether any gap justifies stepping outside the pool (Q-411, D-001, D-002).

### 9.3 What the pool can and cannot show today

- **It cannot yet show balance.** For most candidates only a title and a position are known. Any claim about variety at this stage would repeat the titles.
- **It can already show where to look first**: the reaction riddles (§6.1), the self-sacrifice cluster (§6.3), and the suitor pair (§6.2).
- **A pool of 27 against 25 leaves little room to remove repetition without removing variety.** If research shows several candidates overlap (for example if TC-020 proves to be a reaction riddle like TC-014 and TC-023), the margin may shrink and the user may have to look beyond the examined pool (§7).
- **Gender portrayal** is a collection-level matter the report raises: many tales treat women as the source of the dilemma and some carry gendered generalisations (§3 item 10 of the report). That is a lens the user may want to add (it touches D-006), and the catalogue does not rule on it.
