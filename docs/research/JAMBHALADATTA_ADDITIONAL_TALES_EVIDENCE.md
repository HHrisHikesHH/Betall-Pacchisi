### 1.2 Method

1. Each tale was read in Emeneau's translation from the Vetāla's return to the tree to the colophon, with the facing Sanskrit and the apparatus, before its record was written. The Sanskrit was used to check the question, the answer and any phrase on which a record turns; it was **not** independently translated in full.
2. **Page references** give the edited Sanskrit text ("T") and Emeneau's translation ("Tr") separately. Text pages are even, translation pages odd.
3. Emeneau's translation is quoted only in short phrases. Everything else is an original summary. Jørgensen's German is summarised, with at most a few words quoted.
4. Fact and interpretation are separated as in N1: "Essential events", "Vetāla's question" and "Vikrama's answer" state what the text says; interpretation is labelled and kept to the later rows.
5. Nothing is rated, ranked or recommended. Repetition observations (§6) are prompts for the user's D-035 judgement.

### 1.3 Reading limitations that apply to every record

- An **AI model**, not a Sanskritist or a Newari specialist, did the reading.
- Emeneau's printed text is the **Bengali recension** (manuscripts H, O, P and the 1873 print V). **J 22 and J 23 stand in the long lacuna of H and O** (H omits J 22, J 23 and the beginning of J 24, and leaves blank the rest of the page after J 21; O, a copy of H, has the same gap), so **for them Emeneau's only manuscript is P**, a Devanagari copy made in 1852–53 of a Calcutta Sanskrit College manuscript, with V as a printed witness (Intro. §3, pp. xii, xiv; §5). J 21 rests on H, O and P.
- The Nepali manuscript **W** contains all three tales (concordance, p. xv), but Emeneau collated W's narrative only for the introduction and story 1; **for J 21–23 he reports W's proper names only**, plus one longer W reading in J 23 (p. xiii; the notes). W's narrative differences in these tales are therefore **not known** from this edition.
- The OCR of Emeneau's romanised Sanskrit loses diacritics. Sanskrit words cited here were normalised by the reader and are PROVISIONAL where the OCR is unclear.
- The Newari evidence is **Jørgensen's German translation**, which he himself marks as uncertain in several places (each noted where relevant), of **one manuscript** (Berlin, Ms. Or. fol. 600, dated Nepal era 921 = 1801 CE, a copy of an older manuscript; pp. 213–214).

## 2. TC-025: J 21, the four brothers and the courtesan

Emeneau's title: "How the Four Brothers fared with the Harlot". This record uses "courtesan" for the Sanskrit *veśyā*, except in quoted titles.

| | |
|---|---|
| **Identification** | Emeneau story 21: T pp. 108–124, Tr pp. 109–125. Opening T p. 108 / Tr p. 109; question and answer T p. 124 / Tr p. 125; colophon T p. 124 (*ekaviṃśatitamo vetālakathāprabandhaḥ*). Manuscripts: H, O, P and V (V numbers it 20, because V omits story 18). Also in W (as 21) and the Newari version (as 21) (concordance, p. xv). Emeneau's note: not in the other versions except the Newari (Tr p. 109 n. 1). |
| **Essential events** | (1) In the south, under King Vikramabāhu, in the town of Puṣkaravatī, lives a caravan merchant of vast wealth, Nidhipatidatta, with four wives and four sons. Each son masters one art: Ratnadatta music and dance, Maṇidatta arms, Kumāradatta "all the sciences", Kanakadatta political wisdom (*nīti*) (Tr pp. 109–111). (2) After the father's death the brothers, though of one mind, separate because their wives quarrel. The wealth is divided amicably, but three divine jewels remain in common; the father had ordered that, if the brothers separated, the jewels should go to whichever son his friend the king chose (Tr p. 111). (3) The king, marvelling at the jewels, decides to give them to the cleverest son and sets a test: in Kusumapura lives a courtesan, Rūpavatī, who takes a lac for one night and will not take the same man a second night for any price; whoever stays with her two nights wins the jewels. They go in order of seniority (Tr pp. 111–113). (4) **Ratnadatta** sends an envoy with gifts, spends a night with her and displays his art; she weeps that she cannot have him again; he offers three lacs for a second night, and she answers that not even a thousand lacs would move her, citing a vow; he returns home stunned (Tr pp. 113–115). (5) **Maṇidatta** takes service with the king of Kusumapura, defeats an invading king, spends a night with her, and is refused a second (Tr p. 115). (6) **Kumāradatta** spends a night with her and argues for a second because of the jewels; refused (Tr p. 115; Emeneau notes the brevity of this episode, Tr p. 111 n. 7). (7) **Kanakadatta** reasons that wealth and valour have failed; he goes with a little money and one servant to learn how she lives. From a slave-girl he learns that Rūpavatī regularly visits her teacher, a female ascetic, Puṇyaśarīrā, at a Śiva temple on the edge of the city (Tr pp. 115–117). (8) He presents himself to the ascetic as a prince from another country, serves her for six months, always slipping away when Rūpavatī comes. Pleased, she offers him a wish; in private he asks the reason for Rūpavatī's one-night rule. She arranges for him to hide in a corner of her house while she asks (Tr p. 117). (9) Rūpavatī, after making the ascetic promise secrecy, tells her story (Tr pp. 119–121): in a former life she was the daughter of Vicitradatta, minister of peace and war to King Pracaṇḍasena of Padmapura; for her devotion the goddess Gaurī granted that she would be fortunate, find a fitting husband and **remember her former births**; she married Prince Pratāpasena, who loved her more than life, took her to war, and fled in fear, abandoning her; captured by the enemy king, and fearing for her fidelity, she died pining for him. (10) At each death she saw a creature and was reborn as it: a cow-elephant, a doe, a *cakravāka* bird. In each life she refused all mates until she recognised Pratāpasena, reborn as the same kind of animal; in each, he fled a danger (an elephant-catcher, a forest fire, hunters) and left her to captivity or death. Dying as a *cakravāka*, caught at a lake in Kusumapura, she saw a courtesan bathing there and was born a courtesan. Concluding that men are cruel and loveless, since Pratāpasena had abandoned her in four births, she vowed never to take a man for a second night (Tr pp. 119–121). (11) Kanakadatta, in disguise, goes to every courtesan's house except Rūpavatī's, sleeps with none, and tells them all the same story told from Pratāpasena's side, as if it were his own (that he abandoned her each time and is now a man pining for her alone); he has it painted (or written) on a cloth and given to all the courtesans (Tr p. 123). (12) Rūpavatī hears the story at second hand, brings him home, learns "the whole affair", clings to his neck weeping loudly before an astonished public, gives up her vow, believing she has regained Pratāpasena's love, and lives with him. He takes her and all her wealth to Puṣkaravatī and **secretly** tells the king everything (Tr p. 123). (13) The king, marvelling that he succeeded where many clever men failed, gives Kanakadatta the jewels and makes him his minister of peace and war (Tr pp. 123–125). |
| **Vetāla's question** | In every birth she knew her husband; how, remembering her former births, did she **not** recognise Kanakadatta for what he was, since his fraudulent story succeeded? (T p. 124 *sā jātismarāpi kathaṃ kanakadattaṃ na jñātavatī*; Tr p. 125). A "why did she fail to recognise?" question. |
| **Vikrama's answer** | In each earlier birth she had union with her own husband, and by that merit (*puṇya*) she recognised him. Born into a courtesan's family she became "very wicked" (*pāpiṣṭhā*); through union with other men she forgot her own husband, although she still remembered her former births (T p. 124; Tr p. 125). The Vetāla, having made the king break his silence, returns to the tree. |
| **Dilemma (plain language)** | A woman who remembers all her past lives, and who recognised her lost lover in every animal shape, is taken in by a stranger who simply tells her own story back to her. Why did her gift fail? The answer locates the failure in her present life as a courtesan. Strictly this is an explanatory puzzle about the plot, not a choice between claimants. |
| **Characters and relationships** | The four brothers (rivals for an inheritance; Kanakadatta the youngest and the successful trickster); King Vikramabāhu (the father's friend, arbiter and tester); the courtesan Rūpavatī (the test, and the teller of her own life); the female ascetic Puṇyaśarīrā (her teacher and confidante, used as a channel); Rūpavatī's past-life husband Pratāpasena (who abandons her four times; he never appears in the present life); the king of Kusumapura (Maṇidatta's patron). |
| **Stakes and emotional character** | Textual: an inheritance of three jewels; Rūpavatī's tears at Ratnadatta's art (Tr p. 113); a woman's story of four abandonments and four deaths pining for the man who left her (Tr pp. 119–121); her anger, put into words, at men's cruelty (Tr p. 121); a public scene of weeping recognition (Tr p. 123); the king's wonder and reward (Tr pp. 123–125). *Interpretation:* the tale combines a trickster contest, in which the reader watches three gifted brothers fail before the patient youngest succeeds, with a melancholy rebirth romance that becomes the instrument of the trick. The reader knows the recognition is false; the text does not say whether Rūpavatī ever learns the truth (the king is told "secretly", Tr p. 123). |
| **Narrative features** | A four-step contest in order of seniority, the youngest succeeding by inquiry rather than by wealth, art or valour. A life story told in the first person by its subject, overheard by a hidden listener, and then **retold falsely from the man's side** as bait. A chain of rebirths each determined by the last thing seen at death, and a pair of lovers reborn in parallel forms (Emeneau identifies both as known motifs, Tr p. 109 n. 1). A long tale: on Emeneau's pages (pp. 108–124, text with facing translation and notes) it is the longest single story in the edition (contents, pp. v–vi); this says nothing about quality. *Observation, no inference drawn:* Kanakadatta is rewarded with the post of minister of peace and war, the title also given to Rūpavatī's past-life father and, in one colophon, to Jambhaladatta himself (report §4.D). |
| **Bengali text and W** | Emeneau reports W's proper names only: Nidhidatta (for Nidhipatidatta); the wives Kāmasenā, Vasantasenā, Vāsavadattā, Kusumavatī (HOPV: Kāmasenā, Vāsavadattā, Kṣamāvatī, Campāvatī); Suvarṇadatta for Kumāradatta; **Mālatī for Rūpavatī**; Padmanagara for Padmapura; and puṣkarāvatī, which Emeneau follows (printing Puṣkaravatī) against HOPV's Puṣpavatī, citing the name's later occurrence in the tale (T p. 108 n. 2; Intro. p. xvi; T pp. 110–118 notes). He argues that where W and the Newari agree on names against the Bengali manuscripts, they are closer to Jambhaladatta's text (p. xvi). No narrative difference in W is reported. |
| **Newari parallel** | **Present, as Newari 21** (Jørgensen pp. 214, 219–223 text, 226–232 translation). A textual parallel at the level of plot, names and question; see §5.2 for the differences, of which the most notable are the third brother's art, the cause of the dispute, and a different false story told by Kanakadatta. Same answer in substance. |
| **Evidence and uncertainty** | Textual fact: all events above, read in Emeneau's translation with the facing text. *Uncertainties:* whether Kanakadatta's cloth is painted or written (*paṭe … likhitvā*, T p. 122; Emeneau "painted"); whether Rūpavatī learns the truth (not stated); whether the king's test intends the brothers to find out her reason (not stated: he says only that whoever stays two nights wins). The question's wording, "for what he was", is Emeneau's rendering of a plain "did not know Kanakadatta". |

## 3. TC-026: J 22, Mūladeva wins a bride for Śaśideva

Emeneau's title: "How Mūladeva obtained a Bride for Śaśideva".

| | |
|---|---|
| **Identification** | Emeneau story 22: T pp. 124–132, Tr pp. 125–133. Opening T p. 124 / Tr p. 125; question and answer T p. 132 / Tr p. 133. **Manuscripts: P and V only** (H and O omit it; V numbers it 21); also in W (as 22) and the Newari version (as 22) (p. xv). Emeneau: not in other versions except the Newari, and "a new addition to the cycle of Mūladeva stories" (Tr p. 125 n. 1). |
| **Essential events** | (1) In Kuṇḍinapura, under the pious King Śvetaketu, the king's close friend, the merchant Śaṅkaradatta, has married Anaṅgasenā, daughter of the merchant Maṇidatta. He entrusts his household to the king, leaves her guarded by soldiers, and goes abroad to trade; twelve years pass without his return, and she wastes away (Tr p. 125). (2) At the festival of the love-god her mother sends her to worship for her husband's return. Śaśideva, "the great rascal", sees her on her way home and falls in love; he goes to the city of Padmāvatī and tells his friend Mūladeva (Tr p. 127). (3) Śaśideva objects that she is guarded by soldiers, her parents and the king. Mūladeva proposes a bawd, Kāmikalpalatā, able to "disturb even Arundhatī" (the model of wifely fidelity; Tr p. 127 n. 9). She agrees: the two men are to stay hidden while she builds a reputation (Tr p. 127). (4) With two servant women she goes to Kuṇḍinapura, worships Śiva, gains magic power and the name of an all-knowing ascetic. At Anaṅgasenā's prompting, her mother consults the "ascetic" about the absent son-in-law; the bawd asks that the daughter be brought. Next day both parents bring her; the bawd tells them Śaṅkaradatta has married a beautiful girl abroad (Tr p. 127). (5) The parents beg for a remedy, threatening that their daughter will die, they after her, and the guilt will be the ascetic's. She names a charm with one hard condition and tells the mother privately: in a hidden bower, Anaṅgasenā must whisper the charm while looking at a man brought to her, and make love with him daily; when he takes on Śaṅkaradatta's appearance, the husband will return. The father agrees, builds the bower and orders the guards to let the ascetic come and go (Tr p. 129). (6) The bawd draws Anaṅgasenā out: since her husband left she has not seen "even the shadow of a man", and the man at the charm reminds her of love with her husband. The bawd gives the man to her; Anaṅgasenā asks for secrecy. **The man is Śaśideva**; the two become lovers, and she forgets Śaṅkaradatta. The bawd keeps visiting on the pretext of the charm (Tr p. 129). (7) Mūladeva decides they must leave with her. Anaṅgasenā agrees to come out in a litter. Mūladeva, "in the guise of Śaśideva" with a hundred servants, seizes her by her clothes at the king's gate, calling her his runaway wife. The guards tell her father; the father tells the king; the king rebukes the stranger as shameless, threatens to kill him and swears on his sacred thread (Emeneau suspects corruption here, Tr p. 131 n. 12). The stranger says he mistook her for his wife after a long absence and promises to bring his real wife to clear his name (Tr pp. 131–133; Emeneau supplies in brackets a phrase "not represented in the text", Tr p. 131 n. 13). (8) Mūladeva's plan: the bawd and Śaśideva carry a woman's corpse, perfumed, into Anaṅgasenā's house in a litter; the bawd dresses it in Anaṅgasenā's ornaments and lays it on her bed; Śaśideva sets the house on fire and escapes with Anaṅgasenā, so that no one will say she ran off with another man. Śaśideva and Mūladeva take her to their own country (Tr p. 133). (9) The parents lament; the king, out of friendship, sends "her" bones to various places of pilgrimage; the bawd laments her "dear pupil" and leaves for Padmāvatī (Tr p. 133). (10) Later Mūladeva and Śaśideva bring Anaṅgasenā before the king as Mūladeva's wife, "who looks like Anaṅgasenā", explaining the earlier mistake. The king exclaims that she is Anaṅgasenā in person, shows them favour and lets them go. Her parents, seeing her, give her all "Anaṅgasenā's" jewels and weep (Tr p. 133). |
| **Vetāla's question** | Who showed the greater intelligence (*mahatī buddhiḥ*): Kāmikalpalatā or Mūladeva? (T p. 132; Emeneau "more ready wit", Tr p. 133). A superlative comparison of two tricksters. |
| **Vikrama's answer** | **Mūladeva**, the great rogue (*mahān dhūrtaḥ*), because his wit was ready on every occasion (*sarvatrotpannabuddhitvāt*) (T p. 132; Tr p. 133). No further reasoning. The departure formula says the Vetāla had thus "deceived the king" (into speaking). |
| **Dilemma (plain language)** | Two tricksters together steal a guarded wife from her family, her absent husband and a king, and erase every trace by faking her death. Whose cleverness was greater: the bawd who won the family's trust and turned a "charm" into a seduction, or the strategist who planned the escape and the false death? The answer prefers the one whose wit never failed at each turn. |
| **Characters and relationships** | Anaṅgasenā (wife of an absent merchant; guarded, lonely, then a willing lover); Śaśideva (the lover; Mūladeva's friend); Mūladeva (the strategist, "prince of rogues"); Kāmikalpalatā (the bawd, posing as an all-knowing ascetic); her parents Maṇidatta and his wife (anxious, deceived, complicit without knowing it); Śaṅkaradatta (the absent husband; never returns in the narrative); King Śvetaketu (the husband's friend and protector, deceived twice). |
| **Stakes and emotional character** | Textual: a wife wasting from twelve years' separation (Tr p. 125); parents who threaten that their own deaths will be on the ascetic's head (Tr p. 129); a king's threat to kill (Tr p. 131); a staged death and cremation with the parents' grief (Tr p. 133); the king's astonishment (Tr p. 133). *Interpretation:* comic intrigue in which the deceived are the virtuous parties (parents, king, absent husband) and the tricksters triumph; the final scene, in which the parents hand their own daughter "Anaṅgasenā's" jewels and weep, has an edge of pathos, and its irony depends on whether they recognise her, which the text leaves open (see "Evidence and uncertainty"). |
| **Narrative features** | A chain of deceptions: a false ascetic, a false oracle about the husband, a false charm, a false claim of mistaken identity, a false death with a substituted corpse, and a "lookalike" wife presented to the king. The mistaken-identity claim is planted early so that the final presentation can succeed (Mūladeva's own explanation of the plan, Tr p. 133). The question compares two helpers, not the lovers. |
| **Bengali text and W** | Emeneau reports W's names only: **Maṇikuṇḍala** for Maṇidatta (p. xvi; T p. 124 n. 4) and **Śaśin** for Śaśideva "in the only place where I have been able to find the name" (Tr p. 125 n. 1; T p. 126 n. 20). No narrative difference in W is reported. Because H and O lack the tale, the Bengali text rests on **P and V** (§1.3). |
| **Newari parallel** | **Present, as Newari 22** (Jørgensen pp. 214, 223–226 text, 233–236 translation). Same plot outline, but with **a different answer: the bawd** (§5.3). |
| **Evidence and uncertainty** | Textual fact: as above. *Uncertainties:* (a) the parents at the end: the text says they saw "her, their daughter" (*tāṃ duhitaram eva ālokya*) and gave her the jewels and wept; Emeneau renders this as the narrator's statement that she was "really their own daughter" (Tr p. 133); whether the parents themselves knew her is **not stated**; (b) why Mūladeva takes Śaśideva's appearance at the gate is not stated; (c) the king's oath and Mūladeva's excuse rest on a passage Emeneau thinks corrupt or incomplete (Tr p. 131 nn. 11–13); (d) the husband Śaṅkaradatta's fate is not told. |

### 3.1 J 22 compared with J 13 (the Mūladeva tale of TC-015)

J 13 is Emeneau's "How Manaḥsvāmin became a Woman through Mūladeva's Magic" (T pp. 78–86, Tr pp. 79–87), Jambhaladatta's form of the core family TC-015. Emeneau notes that in both J 13 and J 22 Mūladeva's friend is called Śaśideva, where the other versions of J 13 and W call him Śaśin (Tr p. 81 n. 7; Tr p. 125 n. 1).

**J 13 in brief (Emeneau's translation).** The bard's son Manaḥsvāmin and Princess Śaśiprabhā fall in love at a river, where he carries her off when a must-elephant scatters her guards. Śaśideva and Mūladeva take him in. Mūladeva gives him a charm (*vidyā*, not the pill of the other versions, Tr p. 81 n. 9) to change sex; disguised as an old brahman, Mūladeva leaves the "girl" in the princess's apartment, saying his son has gone to Ujjayinī. Manaḥsvāmin becomes the princess's lover. The minister's son Vijayasena falls for the "girl" and is given her; she sends him to Ujjayinī, and Manaḥsvāmin seduces Vijayasena's bride Mṛgāṅkavatī and takes her to Mūladeva. Mūladeva returns as the old brahman with Śaśideva disguised as his son and, threatening to die so that the guilt falls on the king, extracts Śaśiprabhā for "the son". Śaśideva and Manaḥsvāmin then quarrel over her. Question: whose wife is she? Answer: Śaśideva's, because her father gave her to him; what Manaḥsvāmin did is forbidden in this world and the next (Tr p. 87).

| Element | J 13 (TC-015) | J 22 (TC-026) |
|---|---|---|
| **The woman** | An unmarried princess, in love with the lover first | A merchant's wife, married twelve years, husband absent |
| **The lover** | Manaḥsvāmin, a bard's son | Śaśideva himself |
| **Śaśideva's role** | Co-helper, disguised as the "son"; ends as the **rival claimant** | The **lover** who wins her |
| **Mūladeva's role** | Gives the sex-changing charm; disguises himself as an old brahman twice; extracts the princess from the king by threat | Proposes the bawd; impersonates a husband at the king's gate (in Śaśideva's guise); plans the false death; presents her as his "lookalike" wife |
| **Other trickster** | None | The bawd Kāmikalpalatā, posing as an all-knowing ascetic |
| **Means** | Change of sex; a woman placed in the palace; a threat of brahman-suicide | A false oracle and a false charm; a mistaken-identity claim; a substituted corpse and a fire |
| **Who is deceived** | The king and his minister | The parents, the king and (in absence) the husband |
| **Shared devices** | A disguised claim before a king about a bride or wife; Mūladeva and Śaśideva as a pair of rogues; a king deceived into giving up, or failing to keep, a woman | Same |
| **Question form** | Allocation: **whose wife?** | Superlative comparison: **who was cleverer?** |
| **Answer basis** | Law: the father's gift against a secret union | Wit: readiness at every turn |
| **Narrative function** | A dispute between two men whom the trick has made rivals | A celebration of the trick itself; no dispute |

**What is shared.** The rogue pair Mūladeva and Śaśideva; a trick by which a woman is removed from her guardians; a disguised claim made to a king; the king's deception. **What differs.** The woman's status and the moral situation (a princess's secret love against a married woman's adultery); Śaśideva's role (rival claimant against lover); the presence of a second trickster; the means; the dilemma (a legal claim to a wife against a comparison of cleverness); and the answer's basis. *Observation:* J 22 is **not a repetition of J 13** at the level of events or dilemma; it is a second, independent Mūladeva intrigue with overlapping devices. Whether its dilemma is "genuinely different" under D-035 is the user's judgement.

## 5. Newari parallel evidence

### 5.1 What Jørgensen's article contains

- **Scope.** A Newari grammar sketch built on a connected prose text: **two tales from a Newari recension of the Vetālapañcaviṃśati**, edited in the Newari with a German translation (pp. 213, 219–236). The manuscript is Berlin Ms. Or. fol. 600, 82 leaves, dated Nepal era 921 (1801 CE), and is a copy of an older manuscript (pp. 213–214).
- **The two tales are Newari 21 and 22**, which Jørgensen identifies with Jambhaladatta's 20 and 21 **in Vidyāsāgara's 1873 print (V)**, the edition of Jambhaladatta he used (p. 214 n. 1; Emeneau: "as the author knew it in V", p. xvi). Since V omits story 18, **V 20 and 21 are Emeneau's J 21 and J 22**. Emeneau confirms the identification (pp. xv–xvi).
- **His concordance (p. 214, page image)** gives the Newari order against V, Uhle's *f* and Śivadāsa. Newari 21 and 22 have no equivalent in *f* or Ś. **Newari 23 corresponds to *f* 14 and Ś 13 (the thief who wept and laughed, TC-014), and the Newari has no equivalent of V 22, Emeneau's J 23.** Newari 24 corresponds to V 23, Emeneau's J 24 (Jīmūtavāhana). Emeneau's own table (p. xv, page image) agrees: Newari column "21, 22, —" for J 21–23, with "23 = S.K. 14, Ś. 13".
- **A discrepancy in Jørgensen's prose (p. 214, page image):** he writes that the Newari lacks "Ja 23", while his table shows that it lacks Ja 22 and has Ja 23 as its 24. The table and Emeneau agree; the prose appears to be a slip. Recorded, not resolved further.
- **His view of the relationship (pp. 214–215).** A quick comparison shows that **no known Sanskrit recension can be the Newari's direct source**; Jambhaladatta's is the closest; *f* shows some agreements; Śivadāsa stands further off. He lists shared features with J (the king's two names; the sign language in tale 1; the brahman-ogre in tale 10; a *vidyā* instead of a pill in tale 13; Newari 24 agreeing essentially with J 24) and notes that the **wording mostly differs**, sometimes fuller in one, sometimes in the other, with **no verbatim agreement in longer passages**, and that tale openings and closings differ. He also notes that in the Newari the king fetches the Vetāla 25 times and the frame's conclusion is not counted as a tale, and that the Vetāla's account of his own history (in J's Bengali conclusion) is absent.
- **Emeneau's view (pp. xvi–xvii).** The Newari is "obviously based on" Jambhaladatta's version because it contains J 21 and 22; it agrees with W in the order of stories 7 and 8 and in proper names; it goes back to a 27-tale archetype; where it and W agree on names against the Bengali manuscripts, they represent Jambhaladatta's text more closely. As the report already notes (§4.F), the two views are compatible.

**Kind of parallel: textual** in plot outline and names, with substantial differences of detail, and a **different answer**.

**Material difference.** **The answer is reversed.** In J the strategist wins; in the Newari the go-between wins, because her deception made all the rest possible. The Newari also redistributes the actions (Śaśideva plans the escape and fakes the death; Mūladeva only impersonates a brahman), so that the answer fits its own telling. This is the second recorded case, after TC-010 (catalogue §5.C), of a J-related telling whose answer differs from another tradition's.

### 5.4 What can and cannot be established

- **Established:** the Newari version has close parallels to J 21 and J 22 (same plots, names and question type), and none to J 23 (Jørgensen's table; Emeneau's table and p. xvi).
- **Established:** the Newari answers differ in reasoning for J 21 and in **substance** for J 22.
- **Not established:** whether these differences go back to the Newari translator, to the Sanskrit source used, or to the one late manuscript; whether other Newari manuscripts (Jørgensen mentions Cambridge and St Petersburg, p. 214 n. 2) agree; the Newari wording itself (not read here). Otter's modern work on the Newari (report S12) remains inaccessible.
- **Not established:** whether J 23 was ever in the Newari tradition. Its absence from one manuscript of one recension is not evidence that it never existed there (catalogue §1.1).

### 6.2 Characters, relationships and motifs

- **Brothers with different accomplishments (TC-025).** Emeneau links J 21's brothers to J 5 and J 19 (TC-008, the fastidious brothers; TC-022, the brothers who revive a lion or tiger) and, with variation, to J 2, 6, 9 and 11 (TC-002, 005, 009, 011) (Tr p. 109 n. 1). In those tales the different skills feed the **riddle**; in J 21 they only set up a failed contest, and the riddle concerns the courtesan. The catalogue's title-level flag on TC-022 and TC-025 ("four brothers") therefore marks a shared opening device, not a shared dilemma.
- **A courtesan at the centre (TC-025).** In N1's records courtesans are incidental (TC-008, TC-019). J 21 is the only candidate in which a courtesan is the central figure and tells her own story. J 23's ogress poses as one.
- **Rebirth and memory of former births (TC-025).** The pool has former births as background (TC-016's Jīmūtavāhana and TC-020's boy remember or are shaped by them, N1) and illusion and nested lives (TC-018), but no other tale is **built on a chain of rebirths**.
- **Trickster intrigue to win a woman (TC-026).** Closest are **TC-015** (J 13; §3.1) and **TC-001** (a prince's clever friend decodes signs, fakes a witchcraft charge and brings about a woman's banishment and her parents' deaths). TC-001 and TC-026 share a schemer-helper, a deceived king and deceived parents, and a staged falsehood that removes a woman from her family; their questions differ (blame for the parents' deaths against comparative cleverness). The catalogue's title-level TC-001/TC-026 flag is thus partly borne out at the level of devices, not of dilemma.
- **A woman go-between (TC-025, TC-026).** J 21's female ascetic is a genuine teacher whose trust is used; J 22's bawd is a false ascetic. Compare TC-001's bribed nurse and TC-021's confidante (N1).
- **Faked death with a substituted corpse (TC-026).** Not found in N1's records; TC-001 has a staged accusation, not a staged death.
- **A daily human tribute by pact and a weeping mother (TC-027).** **TC-016** has the same elements in KSS: Garuḍa eats one Nāga a day by agreement, and Śaṅkhacūḍa's mother weeps for her son whose turn has come (N1). In Jambhaladatta's own sequence, **J 24** (TC-016) begins immediately after J 23 with a weeping serpent-mother whose son Garuḍa will eat (Tr p. 141; seen in passing, not read for N3). TC-020 also has a man-eating brahman-ogre who demands a victim from a king. The dilemma in J 23 (blame) differs from TC-016's (who was more courageous) and TC-020's (why did the boy laugh).
- **Ogres.** Rākṣasas or brahman-ogres appear in TC-005, 012 and 020 (N1) and in J's own TC-010 variant (report §8.1). J 23 is the only candidate in which ogres are the main antagonists of a whole city.
- **King and helper.** J 23's king fails to honour the man who saves him; compare the core king-and-servant tales TC-004, 007 and 017, where the question weighs a king's act against a servant's. J 23 does not ask that question, but its plot turns on the same relationship from the other side.

### 6.3 Emotional range and narrative form

- **TC-025:** pathos (four abandoned lives) inside a trickster comedy; a recognition scene the reader knows to be false. Of the core families, TC-014 and TC-023 also centre on a strong emotional reaction, but none uses a false recognition.
- **TC-026:** comic intrigue with a dark undertow (a staged death, deceived parents). Close in tone to TC-015 and, more distantly, TC-001 (N1 §4.4 records humour and irony in TC-008, 011, 015, 023, 024).
- **TC-027:** horror and the uncanny (an ogress who changes shape and eats her lovers), public catastrophe, and a closing act of generosity. In the core pool horror is present (TC-002's child in the fire, TC-020's sacrifice), but no core tale sets a monster against a whole city.
- **Narrative devices not prominent among the 24 (N1 §4.5):** a story overheard and then retold falsely (TC-025); a chain of rebirths (TC-025); a corpse substitution and fire (TC-026); a creature's belly yielding a living girl (TC-027); shape-shifting into a bee (TC-027).

### 6.4 Gendered and moralising material (for N9)

- **TC-025:** Vikrama's answer calls the courtesan "very wicked" and attributes her failure to her life with other men (T p. 124); Rūpavatī's own verdict is that men are cruel and loveless (Tr p. 121). Both are in the text; the second is a woman's voice against male abandonment, which N1 found rare.
- **TC-026:** the bawd's power to disturb "even Arundhatī" (Tr p. 127) and the married woman's adultery are presented without judgement in J; the Newari bawd also claims that mantras succeed in proportion to intercourse.
- **TC-027:** an ogress who poses as a courtesan and eats her lovers; Vidyādhara's affair and his wife's insistence.

## 7. Source limitations and uncertainties

- **AI reading.** No Sanskritist or Newari specialist checked these readings.
- **Emeneau's text is the Bengali recension.** For J 22 and J 23 it rests on **one manuscript, P** (a 19th-century copy), plus the 1873 print V, which Emeneau calls a poor edition (§1.3). W, which Emeneau considers closer to the original but very corrupt, was collated only for names in these tales.
- **The Newari is read only in Jørgensen's German translation** of one manuscript of 1801; Jørgensen marks several renderings as uncertain (for example the first death, p. 230; Śaśideva's disguise, p. 235). The Newari text was not read.
- **OCR.** Emeneau's romanised Sanskrit and Jørgensen's German were read through OCR; the key passages (Emeneau p. xv concordance and p. xvi; Jørgensen pp. 214, 232, 236) were checked on page images, and the three J question-and-answer passages were cross-checked in the second Emeneau scan.
- **Copyright.** Emeneau 1934 carries a copyright notice (report §11); Jørgensen 1921's status was not checked. Only short phrases are quoted here; all summaries are original.
- **Passages recommended for specialist verification** (recommendations, not requirements): J 21's question and answer (T p. 124: *na jñātavatī*, *pāpiṣṭhā*, *anyapuruṣayogāt*); J 22's answer (T p. 132: *sarvatrotpannabuddhitvāt*) and the corrupt passage at T p. 130; J 22's closing sentence on the parents (T p. 132: *tāṃ duhitaram eva ālokya*); J 23's question and answer (T p. 138) and Mahābala's pun on the king's name (T p. 138, Tr p. 139 n. 17); Jørgensen's Newari for the answers of tales 21 and 22 (pp. 223, 226).

## 8. Findings relevant to eventual collection selection

These are findings, not recommendations. The choice of the 25 (Q-411) is the user's.

1. **All three tales are complete, coherent stories with a question and an answer.** None is a fragment, and none merely repeats a core family.
2. **TC-025 (J 21)** brings material not otherwise in the pool: a courtesan at the centre, a chain of animal rebirths, a woman's own account of repeated abandonment, a trick that turns her story against her, and a question about the failure of memory. Its answer is moralising about courtesans (N9). It has a Newari parallel with the same answer and a different false story.
3. **TC-026 (J 22)** is a trickster intrigue sharing its rogue pair and some devices with TC-015 (J 13) and, more loosely, TC-001, but **its dilemma (who was cleverer?) differs from both**. Its answer **differs between J (Mūladeva) and the Newari (the bawd)**, so the tradition offers two answers, as TC-010 does. Its Bengali text rests on one manuscript.
4. **TC-027 (J 23)** is the darkest of the three: a city devoured by ogres, with a blame question that falls on the king. It shares the tribute-and-weeping-mother motif with TC-016 and the "the king is to blame" answer with TC-001, but its dilemma (guilt for a chain of causes) is its own.
