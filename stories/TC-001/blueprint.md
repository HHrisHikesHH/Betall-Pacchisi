## 3. Adaptation premise and central dilemma

**Premise (framing of the approved question and answer, D-060).** Two young people's love affair becomes, through the planning of one clever friend, a lie told to a grieving king; the lie becomes a sentence; after it, her parents die believing their daughter has been eaten in the forest. What, if anything, they were told after the expulsion is not stated. Each act in the chain was somebody's choice.

**The dilemma, with both dimensions kept alive.**
- **The prince and the minister's son have their own culpable acts**: the drink she was instructed to be given (154) and found motionless from liquor (160), the iron, the theft, the false account to authority. No ground (service to a master, passion, "their own aim") cancels them (§9).
- **The king held political authority and the duty to examine before he punished.** He made two real checks on the evidence, formed his conviction, and then took the sentence from the accuser himself. None of his checks tested the accusation independently. His sentence is the crucial recorded link between the lie and the parents' loss, though KSS does not make it the sole established cause of their belief (§4, §10).
- **The deceivers are in the chain around the sentence too.** The minister's son's plan already aimed at carrying her off (150); the sentence the king adopted on his word became the route by which that happened (INF), and the two men then took her, alive, to their own kingdom (183–184). The tale keeps that visible (§4.1, §10).
- Examining the accomplices **must not erase** the king's responsibility, and the king's responsibility **must not erase** theirs. The tale is not "all equally guilty" and does not rank the accomplices' wrongs against the king's. The question it asks is narrower: on whom *these deaths* rest. **Whether Vikram's answer keeps KSS 190's "not these three" for the deaths, or proposes shared responsibility for them, is the user's decision** (§9.5 item 5, §9.8).
- **The answer stays the king.** Betal's objection (the three were the doers) must be something a thoughtful reader can accept.
- The tale fails if the king is merely the obvious fool, or if the accomplices are merely monsters, or if all four are collapsed into one verdict (§10).

**9.5 Reasoning (APPROVED, D-060 A; composed; outline only, not wording for the tale).**
1. **The prince's and the minister's son's acts are wrongs of their own**, whatever their motives: the drink, the iron, the theft, the lie told to authority. Neither service to a friend nor love cancels any of them. (Replaces 192–193.)
2. **Padmāvatī is measured by the same standard of conduct and the same standard of evidence** (A3 F6). The narrator reports only that the dog died after eating (146). The poison and her motive are **alleged by the minister's son** (148–149, CHAR), not confirmed by the narrator, and the same man later invents the account he gives to authority (172–177). The allegation is never tested with her: the prince acts on it without asking her (IC-14), and the king never hears her. The reasoning therefore **does not assign her guilt on the word of an untested accuser while faulting the king for doing the same.** It neither clears nor condemns her; the poison stays unresolved (C8). If Vikram's reasoning touches the food at all, it says no more than that the charge came from her accuser and was never put to her. Her presence in the question is never treated as implying a share in her parents' deaths.
3. **The question asks about the deaths; what the reasoning must face** (A3 F1). The evidence states are set out in §4.1 (the SRC / INF / UNV table) and are not repeated as fact here.
   - **The strongest alternative, at full strength.** The minister's son's plan already aimed at carrying her off (150, SRC). The king took the punishment from him and expelled her on his word (180–181 *tadgirā*, SRC), so the sentence the reasoning lays on the king became the route by which the accuser's aim was achieved (INF). The two men then took her, alive, to their own kingdom (183–184, SRC), which may have made it harder for her parents to learn she lived and may have contributed to the conditions in which their fatal belief persisted (INF). On this view the deaths come at the end of the deceivers' scheme, and the deceived king was its instrument. **This needs no claim that the men knew what the parents believed, foresaw the deaths or withheld a message, and makes none** (UNV; IC-19).
   - **The reply available to Vikram (APPROVED, D-060 A; contestable).** A judge owns the sentence he adopts. Taking the punishment from the accuser, the only source of the charge, is itself the fault the source names (194–195: no inquiry; ignorant of rogues). The parents' grief begins at the expulsion (181), and the belief that killed the father, that she was eaten in the forest (185), is a belief about what a sentence of expulsion would bring. The accuser's design and the removal stay in the chain; on this reasoning they do not transfer the sentence away from the man who pronounced it.
   - **What the reasoning does not claim.** That the sentence was the sole established cause of the parents' belief; that the accuser's design or the removal is irrelevant; that his reply defeats the alternative. **The reply is an argument a reader may reject**, and the tale must leave the alternative standing in its events (C4, F5).
4. **The duty to examine was the king's office** (A3 F2, F9). He made **two real checks bearing on guilt**: he recognised the necklace (178) and received the trusted old woman's report of the mark (179). His conviction formed from them (180 *utpannaniścayaḥ*). **Only then** did he go to the "ascetic", and that visit was about her punishment, not her guilt (180–181). Both checks examined things the scheme had itself produced; neither tested the accusation independently. The narrative records no hearing of Padmāvatī, no inquiry of her parents, no independent test of the "ascetic's" explanation, and no examination of his son's death. (A robbery report existed (167); who made it and whether he knew of it is not recorded, OQ-21; it is an unknown and is not counted among his omissions.) The fault proposed is not that he made no checks. It is that his checks did not test the accusation independently, and that he then took the sentence from the accuser. The source's own grounds (194–195: no inquiry, ignorant of rogues and signs) are kept.
5. **The scope of the answer: four distinct things** (A3 F3).
   - (a) **The answer target:** the king (D-048; §9.4). Not open.
   - (b) **The question answered:** on whom the parents' deaths rest (186–187).
   - (c) **The accomplices' other acts:** the drink, the iron, the theft and the lie to authority are their own wrongs, which the adaptation does not excuse (item 1; C3). Saying so is **not** a statement about the deaths, and the tale does not rank who is worst. "The king's fault does not clear them" means (c) only.
   - (d) **The allocation of the deaths (APPROVED, D-060 B: Option A).** **Option A:** for these deaths, not the three, but the king (KSS 190's two parts kept; the form the brief §4 and SLATE_AUDIT §5 describe: "the *deaths* belong to the one with the duty to inquire"). **Option B** (the king, with others sharing responsibility for these deaths) was **considered, not adopted** (D-060 B). As worded here it would have shared the deaths with "the three", which includes Padmāvatī (A3 re-check N-1); that wording is not used. **Selected: Option A (APPROVED, D-060 B).** Betal's objection argues against Option A directly. The reasoning in items 1–4 is written so that it does not presuppose either option.
6. **The prince, too, acted without asking her (B10, IC-14).** The same failure to inquire recurs, first privately, then by a king with the power to destroy. This is a structural echo the writer may use through events, not a speech.

## 10. Honest-difficulty test (D-041; Charter §3.5)

**Status.** The researcher's first version of this test claimed no PASS. **The A3 pre-review (AI) found the dilemma honestly hard: PASS, conditional on finding 1's correction** reaching this section and §9.5 (A3 report §4.6). This revision applies that correction. **Whether it does so adequately is for the focused A3 re-check; no PASS is claimed here.** The question is whether a thoughtful reader could defensibly reach a verdict other than Vikram's.

**Strongest case for the king's responsibility.**
1. **Office.** Only he had the authority and the duty to examine before he punished (194–195).
2. **Two checks, both on what the scheme produced.** He recognised the necklace (178) and received his trusted woman's report of the mark (179), and his conviction formed from them (180). Neither tested the accusation independently: the scheme had produced both, and only the account supplied the *ḍākinī* reading. The narrative records no hearing of Padmāvatī, no inquiry of her parents, no independent test of the "ascetic's" explanation, and no examination of his son's unexplained death. (A robbery report existed (167); who made it and whether he knew of it is not recorded, OQ-21: an unknown, not an omission.) ("Not recorded" is not "did not" as a source fact; making these omissions visible is the adaptation's choice, IC-09.)
3. **He took the sentence from the accuser.** After his conviction formed, he asked the "ascetic", the only source of the accusation, what the punishment should be, and expelled her on his word (180–181 *tadgirā*). The one who brought the charge decided the sentence.
4. **No hearing.** Her parents lament as she is expelled (181) and are not recorded as asked. That they were present at the deliberation is not stated (INF at most), so the tale does not claim they could have been questioned before the sentence (C5).
5. **The sentence and its foreseeable effect.** Expelling a young woman from the city, which in the tale means the forest (182), makes her parents' grief and their fear of her death predictable. Their fatal belief, "eaten in the forest" (185), is a belief about what such a sentence would bring (INF). KSS does not narrate how the belief arose.
6. **Grief explains him; it does not excuse him.** A duty to examine is heaviest when feeling is strongest.

**Strongest counter-case (could lead a thoughtful reader to answer otherwise).** The king is not made blameless here; the case is that the deaths rest more on the deceivers than on him.
1. **The sentence served the accuser's design.** The minister's son's plan aimed at carrying her off (150 *haraṇe yuktim*, SRC). The expulsion the king imposed came from the accuser's word (181 *tadgirā*, SRC) and became the route by which that aim was achieved (INF). The king's "making it real" was the scheme succeeding through him. (The source does not say the minister's son planned this exact sentence before the king asked him; the link is an inference.)
2. **The removal, and what it made foreseeable.** The two men found her alive and took her to their own kingdom (183–184, SRC). The narrative does not show anyone else learning that she lived; that no one else knew is an inference from that silence, not a recorded fact (INF). Taking her to another kingdom may have made it harder for her parents to learn that she lived, and may have contributed to the conditions in which their fatal belief persisted (INF). **This argument needs no claim that the men knew what the parents believed, foresaw their deaths or withheld a message; the source establishes none of these, nor that any message would have reached or saved the parents** (UNV; IC-19).
3. **The deception was built to be undetectable.** The false account, told in private to the city chief (172–177), offers a prince's heart to a deity, matching his own loss (IC-10), and describes a trident mark. The scheme had made that mark (160); the account then described it; and his own trusted woman looked and found it (179). A checkable detail was checked and found true, which is the strongest kind of corroboration a judge ordinarily gets. To see through it he would have had to suspect that the holy man himself had arranged the mark, and nothing in front of him pointed that way.
4. **He did check, and he did not kill.** His two checks on the evidence were real (178–179), and the sentence was expulsion, not death. J's king resolves to kill her (not adopted); KSS's does not.
5. **"But for."** Without the minister's son's and the prince's acts there is no mark, no story, no sentence, no removal (INF). The mark, the false account and the removal are theirs (SRC); the sentence was the king's, given on the account they had staged (181, *tadgirā*); and how the parents' fatal belief arose is not narrated (185). Their acts are a necessary condition of the deaths, not the whole of their cause. A deceived man is a victim of deceivers, which is exactly Betal's rice and geese.
6. **The standard may be too high.** Spies, polity and a reading of rogues are an ideal ruler's skills; blaming the deceived for being deceived may be a harsh test.

**Does Betal's objection stay forceful?** It does only if the tale meets these conditions:
- **C1** The king's evidence is genuinely persuasive inside the tale (the account is not absurd; its mechanics are kept, §11.1 R7).
- **C2** The king's checks are shown accurately: **two real checks on the evidence** (178, 179), a conviction formed from them (180), then a consultation of the accuser **on punishment** (180–181). The consultation is not shown as a third check on guilt, and he is shown neither as making no checks nor as independently verifying the accusation.
- **C3** The accomplices' wrongs are fully visible, not softened by love or service, so "they were the doers" carries moral weight.
- **C4** The tale keeps the king's sentence central to the deaths **while letting the two strongest alternative points stand in its events**: that the sentence came from the accuser and served his aim (150, 181), and that the removal of a living daughter to another kingdom may have helped the parents' false belief persist (183–185). The removal is **neither presented as a proven second cause nor reduced to an omission that depends on what the men knew**. Nothing is added about a message (IC-19).
- **C5** The king's omissions are specific, as the adaptation makes them visible (IC-09), not a generic "he should have investigated"; the tale does not claim that every possible witness was certainly within his reach at the moment of judgment.
- **C6** No narrator or character states the thesis. A fix that only works by having Vikram state it fails (Charter §3.5).
- **C7** The answer never turns on the accomplices being lesser or innocent, and its scope is stated (§9.5 item 5).
- **C8** The poison stays unresolved, so no reader can settle the weighing by deciding she was "a poisoner" or "only a victim".

**Failure signs (the proposal has failed if any appear).** F1 The king is obviously guilty and the accomplices irrelevant. F2 The accomplices are obviously monstrous and the king blameless. F3 All four are collapsed into "equally guilty". F4 Padmāvatī is a flawless victim or a villain. F5 Betal's objection is a straw man.

**What must be preserved or left open.** The poison; the cause of the king's son's death; whether anyone could have told the parents; whether she consented to rite, drink or removal; the guilt of the accomplices *compared with* the king's. **The blueprint cannot guarantee C1 to C8; the draft, the critique and the verification must test them** (A3 marked C1, C2, C4, C6 and C7 at risk; they are tested again in Draft 2's ethics section and at Stage C). The focused A3 re-check decides PASS, BLOCK or ESCALATE on this revision.

## 12. Emotional and thematic possibilities

Possibilities, not prescribed morals, and none for narration to state.
- **Complicity and public responsibility:** one adviser's word is accepted by the prince, then by the king.
- **The cost of manufactured evidence:** each clever step has a person on the other side.
- **Padmāvatī's constrained position:** an artisan's daughter, her own purposeful acts, then a plot that treats her body as evidence. The tale can hold her signs and her silence together.
- **A house that dies believing its daughter dead:** the loss of the parents, felt through the setting the tale built early, not through their thoughts.
- **Signs and readers of signs:** the minister's son reads signs well; the king, faulted at 195, cannot. A reader may notice that the lie was also a kind of sign.
- **Grief and judgment:** the king's loss makes him easy to deceive, and it does not excuse him.
- **Contentment bought elsewhere:** the prince's ease at the end.
