## 0. Reading conventions

**Evidence labels** (used in every table; they are never merged):

| Label | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **SRC** | The source text itself says it, in the narrator's voice or as plain narration of an event. |
| **NAR-U** | The narrator *reports* something (usually through a character's account) **without confirming** it. |
| **CHAR** | A character alleges, interprets or decodes. Not a source fact about the world of the tale. |
| **INF** | The researcher's inference from the text. Never a source fact. |
| **UNV** | Unverified, or not checked in this session. |

**Quotation.** Phrases are quoted as they stand in the source (KSS: as in the e-text, with its sandhi); single words are given in the stem or pausal form usual in citation.

**Locations.** KSS: verse as `12.8.N` (GRETIL numbering, Durgaprasad–Parab 1915). Uhle (Ś): printed page and, where given, printed line (the line numbers in the margin; ±1). Barker (Hindi): printed page (= PDF image index 0-based minus 15; every Barker page of Story I, pp. 31–64, was inspected as a page image). Platts: printed page. Emeneau (J): printed page; **the printed Sanskrit text is on the even pages (14, 16, … 26) and the English translation on the odd pages (15, 17, … 27); footnotes at the foot of each**. **Emeneau's PDF image index (0-based) = printed page + 23** (1-based PDF page = printed + 24); the images actually inspected are listed by printed page and kind in §3. ## 1. Material findings in brief (findings, not decisions)

Everything here is argued in the sections cited. None of it changes D-048, the brief or any open question.

1. **The witnesses do not share one plot.** The comparison counts **four main narratives read in full**: KSS, Ś 1 (Uhle), the Hindi (Barker's printed Hindi, read directly in Devanagari on all 34 pages, is the text read; Platts is a **separate English translation of Forbes's Hindi**, and **the relationship between Forbes's underlying text and Barker's text is not established in this record**, so no common textual lineage is claimed) and J 1. **A fifth, the anonymous recension U-f, was read only once, in OCR, to separate it from Ś; it is not analysed to the same depth and is mentioned only where stated.** All four main narratives have the lake meeting with lotus signs, a decoding by the minister's son, a go-between, a union, food that is tested on an animal (in **KSS the dog dies, but poison and motive are alleged only by the minister's son; in Ś, the Hindi and J the narrator identifies the food as poisoned**; §6.1), a trident-like mark and the stolen ornaments, a false "witch" story, the king's expulsion of Padmāvatī, and the answer "the king". They differ on almost everything else (§§4–9; [chronology.md](chronology.md) §5).
2. **Padmāvatī's origin varies by witness.** KSS: daughter of a *dantaghāṭaka* named Saṅgrāmavardhana, in King Karṇotpala's Kaliṅga. Ś: daughter of Dantaghāṭa, **called a king** (*rājñaḥ putrī*), and "*rājaduhitā*", in Karṇakubja. Hindi (Barker): *rājkanyā*, daughter of **king** *Dantbāṭ*, in Karnāṭak. J: daughter of the *saṃdhivigrahika* (minister of peace and war) Dantāghāta, under King Karṇotpala. §4. **The artisan origin is attested only in KSS**, and even there it rests on one reading of *dantaghāṭaka* that Tawney's own note shows was disputed (§4.2). **The occupational sense of *dantaghāṭaka* is not specialist-confirmed. The approved D-048 adaptation direction, and the brief's safeguard on her origin, are not changed by this record.**
3. **Whether Padmāvatī poisoned anyone is resolved differently by each witness.** KSS alone leaves it unconfirmed by the narrator (the dog's death is narrated; the word *viṣa* appears only in the minister's son's speech, 12.8.148). Ś, Hindi and J each have the narrator name the food as poisoned; J also reports her thought and motive in the narrator's voice (§6.1).
4. **Drink, drug, sleep and the marking differ in kind**, and "drunk", "drugged" and "sedated" are **not** interchangeable (§6.2): KSS = **the minister's son instructs** the prince to make her drink until unconscious (12.8.154–155, *pānamada*); **the narrator reports her as motionless from liquor** when she is marked (160, *madya*); **the act of giving her the drink is not narrated**; the mark is a fire-heated trident-spike on the hip/loins; Ś = no drink, "extreme helplessness" (*vaikalya*) after union, a trident-shaped mark made **with fingernails** on the left *jaṅghā*; Hindi = natural sleep, a trident on the left thigh; J = a sweet made with thorn-apple (*dhustūra*), then a three-line mark on the right thigh with an iron spike.
5. **KSS 12.8.182cd may report, in the narrator's voice, that Padmāvatī supposed the minister's son had devised a stratagem** (*upāyaṃ mantriputreṇa taṃ saṃbhāvya tathā kṛtam*); N1 and the audit do not mention the verse. **That is the narrowest defensible reading, and it is not settled**: the syntax and the causal link to her not giving up her body are for a Sanskritist (§5.1, §11.1–11.2). **The verse does not establish what she inferred, how much of the deception she understood, that she knew of the prince's part, or what outcome she expected.** It does not give her consent to the removal, which no witness records. Tawney's translation reads the verse as a supposition about an "artifice devised by the minister's son".
6. **The parents' deaths are absent from the Hindi** (Barker pp. 63–64; Platts pp. 28–29). They are present in KSS (a single verse, 12.8.185), Ś (p. 12), J (p. 24) and the anonymous recension. The Hindi question also does not concern a death (§8).
7. **The crow-and-geese objection (12.8.191) exists only in KSS** among the witnesses read. The "love's arrows" and "served his master" exemptions (192–193) are in KSS and, in compressed form, in the anonymous recension (Uhle f). Ś, J and the Hindi give no exemption for the prince or the minister's son; the Hindi gives different ones for the minister's son, the kotwāl and the princess, and never names the prince (§9).
8. **In Ś and the Hindi the judging king appears to be Padmāvatī's own father** (no second king is introduced; INF, §4.3). In KSS and J the king (Karṇotpala) is a different man from her father.
9. **N1 and the dossier say the branding is on the "thigh". KSS says *kaṭītaṭa* (155) and *jaghana* (160, 176, 179)**, the hip/loins region. "Thigh" is right for the Hindi (left) and J (right) and probably for Ś (*vāmajaṅghā*; §6.3).
10. **KSS shows the king doing some checking** (recognising the necklace, an old woman sent to look at the mark, a personal consultation with the "ascetic", 12.8.178–181) and **not recorded** doing other things (hearing Padmāvatī, her parents, testing the ascetic's story). "Not recorded" is not "did not" (§7).
11. **Two textual echoes of *avicāra* ("lack of examination") in KSS** (159cd, 193c, 195) are INF only (§8.4).
12. **Betal's penalty formula** is at the end of the tale in KSS (head), Ś (heart) and the Hindi (hell), but in J it stands in the prologue **before** the tale (p. 14) and is not repeated at the question (p. 26). Stated for the frame record only; D-057B is not touched.

### 4.2 What "*dantaghāṭaka*" means in KSS: an unsettled point for a Sanskritist

- N1, the audit and the brief use "ivory-carver". That is also Emeneau's gloss of KSS ("a *dantaghāṭaka*, an ivory-carver", p. 17 n. 8).
- **Tawney translates "dentist" and adds a note** (vol. 2, p. 236, n. †): one Pandit "thinks that the word *dantaghāṭaka* must mean 'dentist'", "the Petersburg lexicographers take it to mean 'a worker in ivory'", and another Pandit "thinks that *dantaghāṭa* is a proper name; if so, *saṅgrāmavardhana* must mean prime minister". (OCR reading; the page is 236.)
- The KSS e-text itself gives the father's name and the epithets above but **no stated trade apart from the compound** and the pun-sign *dantaracanā*. Emeneau glosses *dantaracanā* as "cleaning of the teeth" (p. 17 n. 8); Tawney has her make a lotus into a "tooth-leaf" ear-ornament (*dantapatra*, p. 236) and **his text reads that word, not *dantaracanā*** (a possible textual variant, **UNV**).
- **So the artisan reading of KSS is the most natural one for a Sanskritist to confirm, not a proven fact.** The brief's safeguard ("preserve KSS's ivory-carver's daughter") is therefore a choice of KSS's tradition and of one reading of its word; this A1 records it and does not change it. **The occupational sense of *dantaghāṭaka* is not specialist-confirmed; D-048 and the brief are unchanged.**

### 8.2 KSS 12.8.191: the crow-and-geese objection (re-read; working translation)

> *vetālo 'py āha rājñaḥ kiṃ te hi tatkāriṇas trayaḥ / kākāḥ kim aparādhyanti haṃsair jagdheṣu śāliṣu*

| Element | Reading | Status |
|---|---|---|
| *vetālo 'py āha* | "The Vetāla in turn said" | Plain |
| *rājñaḥ kiṃ* | "(what) of the king?" The noun ("sin", "fault") is **understood**, not written | **Elliptical; the supplied noun is mine** |
| *te hi tatkāriṇas trayaḥ* | "for those three are the doers of that" (*tatkārin*). **What "that" is is not stated** (the deaths? the whole deed? the deception?) | **UNV: antecedent of *tat*** |
| *kākāḥ kim aparādhyanti* | "do crows offend/do wrong?" (rhetorical *kim*; *aparādhyanti*, 3 pl. of *apa-√rādh*). Tawney: "Are the crows in fault…?" | Plain grammar; **the rhetorical force is the reading everyone follows** |
| *haṃsair jagdheṣu śāliṣu* | "when the rice (*śāli*) has been **eaten** (*jagdha*) by **geese** (*haṃsa*; also "swans")" (locative absolute with instrumental agent). Tawney: "swans eat the rice" | Plain |

**Working translation (mine, tentative):** "The Vetāla said: 'What [guilt] of the king? Those three were the doers of it. Are crows at fault when the rice has been eaten by geese?'"

**Sense, with the limits stated.**
1. The line is a rhetorical question. The proverbial logic, on every reading found (Tawney, N1, the audit), is that **the party who did not eat the rice is not at fault when another has**.
2. **The text does not assign the birds to persons.** The natural mapping is crows = the king (the innocent bystander) and geese = the three doers, from the immediately preceding clause ("what of the king? the three are the doers"). **That mapping is the reader's inference, supported by the preceding clause; it is not stated.** The opposite mapping would make the line support Vikrama and is not read by anyone, since the line opens with *vetālo 'py āha* and *rājñaḥ kiṃ*.
3. Whether the proverb carries an extra nuance (crows as the lowly usual suspects, geese as the noble who are the real culprits) is **not settled** and is a Sanskritist's question.
4. The audit's formulation that the objection "carries the accomplices' case" (SLATE_AUDIT §5.0) is a **fair reading of what Betal says** (the three are the doers), not a statement in the text about the accomplices' innocence.
