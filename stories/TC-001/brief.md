## 1. Source beats
1. Vārāṇasī: King Pratāpamukuṭa, his son Vajramukuṭa, the minister's son Buddhiśarīra ("dearer than his body"); they hunt and reach a forest lake. [KSS 59–67]
2. Padmāvatī, of divine beauty, comes to bathe; both are struck. Under cover of play she makes signs to tell her country: a lotus at the ear, a lotus on the head, a hand on the heart (D-061: three signs, no teeth sign). [KSS 68–76]
3. The prince cannot read them; Buddhiśarīra decodes: Karṇotpala's realm, the name Padmāvatī, "my life is in you"; she is the ivory-worker's (Saṅgrāmavardhana's) daughter in Kaliṅga. [KSS 77–86]
4. They travel and lodge near her house; an old woman, paid, carries messages. [KSS 87–99]
5. One "rebuke" (report unreliable, never confirmed) which Buddhiśarīra reads as an invitation. [KSS 100–114, compressed to one exchange, ours]
6. Padmāvatī herself honours the old woman and has a rope-seat let down from her window; the elephant outcry is left unexplained. [KSS 115–121]
7. The prince is drawn up; they are united by the gāndharva rite; no scene. [KSS 122–131]
8. She asks who read her signs, calls the friend her "brother" and lets the prince go to him. [KSS 132–139]
9. Food reaches the friend; he tests it on a dog, which dies; he alleges poison and her motive; the narrator confirms only the dog's death. The prince acts without asking her; the friend plans to "carry her off". [KSS 142–151]
10. A cry: the king's young son has died (cause unstated). Buddhiśarīra sees his opening and has a trident forged. [KSS 152–157]
11. The prince goes by night; she is "motionless from liquor" (the drink was his planned incapacitation); he brands her with the iron and takes her ornaments. A violation, his hand visible; nothing sexual narrated. [KSS 154–161]
12. Buddhiśarīra poses as an ascetic; the prince sells the necklace; guards seize him; the "ascetic" tells the city chief a false story (a drunk yoginī marked, a prince's heart offered). [KSS 162–177]
13. The king recognises the necklace; a trusted old woman reports the mark; his conviction forms; only then does he ask the "ascetic" her punishment (180–181). [KSS 178–181]
14. On the "ascetic's" word she is expelled; her parents lament; no word of hers. [KSS 181]
15. In the forest she does not give up her life; she supposes the minister's son devised a stratagem. [KSS 182, CA-02, ours, narrow]
16. At day's end the two find her grieving, console her and take her to their kingdom; the prince lives there content. No word of hers. [KSS 183–184]
17. Her father, believing her eaten in the forest, dies of grief; his wife follows him (manner unstated). [KSS 185]
18. Betal's question and Vikram's answer (penalty not in the tale, D-057B). [KSS 186–195]
Ours: early scene of the parents' household (CA-01, D-060 G). Variants: Ś, Hindi and J agree the answer is the king; Padmāvatī is a king's daughter in Ś and the Hindi, a minister's daughter in J; the Hindi has no deaths, Ś has the mother on the pyre (not adopted); the crow-and-geese objection and the two exemptions exist only in KSS.

## 2. Riddle and verdict
- Betal's question (KSS 186–187): on the parents' deaths, what is the sin of the minister's son, of the prince, of Padmāvatī? The king is not named among them.
- Source answer: none of the three; the sin is King Karṇotpala's (190). Betal: "those three were the doers; are crows at fault when geese have eaten the rice?" (191). Reasons (192–195): master's business; passion and own aim; the king uninformed and acting without inquiry.
- Our answer: **king kept; reasoning changed; APPROVED (D-060 A, B, C)**, the composed verdict is APPROVED. Exemptions removed: a dilemma change (D-060 C). Reasoning outline (blueprint §9.5): the accomplices' acts are their own wrongs; Padmāvatī is held to the same standard of evidence (poison never tested, unresolved); a judge owns the sentence he adopts, and taking the punishment from the sole accuser is the named fault; the accuser's design and the removal stay in the chain but do not move the sentence off the king; no ranking of guilt.
- Honest-difficulty: the planners did the visible harm, their aim was her removal, and the sentence became the road to it; a reader may well say the deaths rest on them.

## 4. Charter points
- Branding, drugging and public witch-accusation of a woman (§3.3.3, §3.3.6, gender H): a violation, never cleverness; no spectacle; R6 (no sexual contact that night); the lie is shown as a lie and mirrors what was done to her (R7); the narration never confirms a ḍākinī.
- No invented consent (§3.3.6): R5 keeps three separate evidence states (her initiative at the window, the rite as a label, grief and no recorded consent at the removal); R2 forbids relief or welcome at the removal.
- Poison unresolved and the same standard of evidence for her (§3.3.1); never her word *dhūrtā* as a "women's wiles" trope (R3).
- Artisan family (§3.2.4, 3.2.5a, 3.6): craft as skill, no jāti, household substantial not poor (R4); the asymmetry is political power.
- Loyalty examined (§3.4.5): "served his master" is not an excuse; the king examined and judged, not worshipped (principle 6); the straw-man test: Betal's objection stays full strength (D-060 E).
- Mother's death: no devotional or sati-like diction, never virtue (§3.4.3; R1; item (h) deferred).

## 5. Voice plan
- Hidden layer: heard as a clever cipher-and-romance tale, it is about what happens when one person's account is taken as the whole truth, and who then pays.
- "And you too": (a) after the prince acts on the poison story without asking her, turn to the reader's own habit of acting on a trusted friend's word; (b) after the king's sentence, what we sentence on one voice; (c) optional, at the question, the reader's own answer (device 10).
- Odd details with a human motive: why a paid old woman's report is unreliable; why the king goes to the "ascetic" (grief seeking an answer); why the friend wants the prince to himself.
- Plot object: the necklace/iron-mark (evidence that looks like proof); the rope-seat (her own act).
- Callbacks: none.
