## 3. Findings

Ranked most severe first. "Required" means a blueprint correction before the gate passes. "Drafting constraint" means the blueprint must carry the rule, but the risk is tested in the draft, the critique and the verification.

### Finding 1 — MAJOR. The counter-case and the composed reasoning leave out the strongest alternative argument

- **Where:** blueprint §9.5 item 3 ("The deceivers made the sentence possible; the king made it real"), §9.6, §10 counter-case items 1–5, condition C4; IC-19; §4.1 rows "The sentence" and "Taking her away".
- **Evidence:**
  - **SRC, KSS 150:** right after alleging the poison, the minister's son tells the prince to drop his anger at her and says he will devise a means for *carrying her off* (*haraṇe yuktim*). The scheme was aimed at removing her, not at punishing a poisoner.
  - **SRC, KSS 180–181:** the king goes to the "ascetic", asks about her punishment (*nigraha*) and expels her **on his word** (*tadgirā*). So the sentence the reasoning lays on the king was prescribed by the accuser, and it achieved the accuser's stated aim.
  - **SRC, KSS 183–185:** the two men take her, alive, to their own kingdom. The prince lives there content. "But" (*tu*) her father, believing her eaten in the forest, dies of grief, and his wife follows him.
- **What the blueprint does:** it uses 181 *tadgirā* only on the king's side (§10, king's case 3: "consulted the accuser's side"). It never states it as the alternative's best point: the sentence was the deceiver's design, and the king was its instrument. It treats the removal only as a possible omission (a failure to tell the parents) and makes even that **conditional on the two men knowing what the parents believed** (§10 counter-case 3; C4). The king's route is not held to the same knowledge condition: he is not shown to know that the parents would think her dead either. His route rests on his office and on what his sentence was bound to cause. **The asymmetry is in the analysis, not in the source.** Reasoning by foreseeable effect applies to both routes. Whoever secretly takes a banished daughter to another kingdom makes it foreseeable that her family cannot learn she lives. That claim needs no knowledge and no deliberate withholding (which the source does not establish and this review does not assume).
- **Consequence:** the dilemma stays hard in the events, because a reader sees B16–B19 in KSS order and will raise these points. But the reasoning the user is asked to approve (D-041) has no answer to them, and the A3 packet would misrepresent the balance. Vikram's answer then looks as if it ignored the obvious rebuttal. That is the gap between "an honestly hard dilemma" and "Vikram is missing a reason". Conditions C4 and F5 are at risk (§4.3).
- **Charter and decision clauses:** D-041 (honest difficulty; the user approves composed reasoning); Charter §3.5 (Betal argued at full strength); D-048 (the king's duty must not erase the accomplices' responsibility).
- **Minimum required action (blueprint revision; the answer and the direction are unchanged):**
  1. Add to §10's counter-case, as its strongest items, (a) that the sentence was the accuser's prescription and served his stated aim (150, 181 *tadgirā*), and (b) a foreseeability statement about the removal (183–184) that does not depend on the men's knowledge and does not claim deliberate withholding.
  2. Restate C4 so that the removal is neither a proven second cause nor reduced to a knowledge-conditional omission.
  3. In §9.5 item 3 and §9.6, set out for the user the reply available to Vikram: the judge owns a sentence he adopts; taking the punishment from the accuser is itself the fault (194–195); the parents' grief begins at the sentence (181), and their fatal belief is about a girl expelled into the forest. Present it as an argument that can be contested, not a settled one.
  4. Record the stronger counter-case in §9.8 as part of what the user approves.

  **Do not** settle the question in the tale's narration, and do not add a message, a refusal or a statement that none was sent (IC-19 stands).

### Finding 2 — MODERATE (required correction). The king's visit to the "ascetic" is counted as a check on guilt; KSS places it after his conviction, and it concerns punishment

- **Where:** §9.5 item 4 ("He did make checks: … and consulted the 'ascetic' himself"); §10 king's case 2 ("personally consulted the ascetic") and counter-case 2 ("He did inquire … 178–181"); C2. (§2.3 row "King's checks", B15 and §5.5 describe it correctly as a consultation "on punishment".)
- **Evidence (SRC, AI reading):** KSS 178 (he recognises the necklace) → 179 (he hears the trusted old woman's report of the mark) → 180 *grastaḥ suto me ḍākinyā tayety utpannaniścayaḥ* ("certainty arose in him: 'my son was seized by that *ḍākinī*'") → only then does he go himself to the "ascetic" → 181 he asks about her punishment and acts on his word. **Two checks bear on guilt (the necklace's identity and the mark). The visit comes after his conviction and is about the sentence.**
- **Consequence:** counting it as an inquiry inflates the king's diligence in the counter-case and contradicts the king's case (where the same act is a fault). C2 ("checks shown as real checks") can only be met if the checks are described accurately. This also bears on finding 1, since the sentence came from that visit.
- **Minimum required action:** correct §9.5 item 4, §10 (both lists) and C2 to: two real checks on the evidence, a conviction formed from them, then a consultation of the accuser on punishment. The verse sequence is also a fair, specific way to make the king's error visible through events (OQ-14).

### Finding 3 — MODERATE (required clarification; user decision). The scope of the adapted answer is ambiguous

- **Where:** §3 ("does not rank their guilt at all. It asks on whom *these deaths* rest"); §9.4 ("The king"); §9.5 item 5 ("The answer does not say their guilt is less"); §9.8.
- **Evidence:** KSS 190 has two parts: "not the sin of these three; but the sin is King Karṇotpala's" (SRC). The brief §4 and the slate audit fix the direction: "the adviser and the prince did their own wrongs; the *deaths* belong to the one with the duty to inquire" (approved direction).
- **Problem:** the blueprint does not say whether Vikram's adapted answer keeps the first part, that **for these deaths** the three bear no sin, while the reasoning condemns their other acts. "The answer does not say their guilt is less" reads as no ranking even on the question asked. Read one way, the adapted answer becomes "the king as well" (failure sign F3) and quietly changes the dilemma. Read the other way, it is "the king, for these deaths", which is the fixed direction. Betal's objection works differently in each case.
- **Minimum required action:** state explicitly that the answer allocates **these deaths** and makes no comparative claim about the accomplices' other wrongs, or put the alternative to the user as a possible dilemma change. Add the choice to §9.8.

### Finding 4 — MODERATE (record in the blueprint; drafting constraint). "His wife followed him" (185 *anujagāma*) may carry the idiom of a widow's death

- **Where:** IC-12; §2.3 row "Parents"; §4 B19; §7.3; §8.1 note to 185.
- **Evidence:** KSS 185 *bhāryā cānujagāma tam* (SRC). *Anu-√gam* ("follow") is a recognised idiom for a widow following her husband in death (cf. *anugamana*). **Whether 185 carries that sense is for a Sanskritist (INF/UNV).** Other witnesses make the mother's death explicit: in Ś she mounts the funeral pyre (p. 12), and in J the father "died with his wife" (p. 25). That makes the reading plausible, not proven.
- **Problem:** the blueprint rejects Ś's pyre under Charter §3.3.4 and §3.4.3. It does not notice that KSS's own verb may carry the same practice. "Manner left unstated" is restraint. But a Hindi rendering with a devotional "following" idiom (for example अनुगमन, सहगमन, or "पीछे-पीछे चली गई" in a tone of wifely devotion) would present the death as wifely virtue without naming it. Removing all trace would, if the sense is there, erase a real harm (Charter §3.2.5, "never erase a real injustice").
- **Minimum action:** record the lexical possibility in IC-12 and the open questions (Sanskritist). Add a drafting constraint: no devotional or *sati* diction, and the death is not framed as virtue. Record, as a lead or user decision (not decided here), whether the possibility stays unstated or becomes visible as harm.

### Finding 5 — MODERATE (drafting constraint). CA-02 placed next to B18 can read as her expecting, and accepting, the removal

- **Where:** CA-02, IC-11, B17–B18, §5.1, §11 ("whether the silence in B18 reads as acceptance").
- **Evidence:** KSS 182 joins "she did not abandon her body" with *upāyaṃ mantriputreṇa taṃ saṃbhāvya tathā kṛtam*. The natural reading, and Tawney's, takes the second as her supposing a stratagem of the minister's son, possibly as her reason (INF/UNV; OQ-4). At 183 the two men arrive and find her **grieving** (*śocantīm*, SRC); they console her and take her.
- **Problem:** the narrowest form of CA-02 is sound. But told just before the men arrive, the supposition invites the reader to conclude that she understood a rescue plan and went willingly. That would turn source silence into consent (Charter §3.3.6). Read the other way, as recognising betrayal, it adds knowledge the verse does not give. The blueprint forbids saying it is "why she lived" but does not control the sequence, which carries the same implication.
- **Minimum action (drafting constraint):** CA-02 must not resolve into B18 as an anticipated rescue. Her grief at the men's arrival (183) stays visible, with no relief, welcome or recognition added. The supposition is not described as hope or as betrayal. If a Sanskritist rejects the reading, CA-02 comes back to A3 (as the blueprint already says).

- **Evidence:** KSS 146–149: the narrator reports only that the dog died after eating. Poison and motive are alleged only by the minister's son (CHAR). By B14 the reader knows he invented the account given to authority.

Put the wording to the user as part of the composed reasoning.

### Finding 7 — MODERATE (drafting constraint). The narrator's epithet *dhūrtā* and the "women's wiles" trope

- **Where:** §5.1; §8.3 (echoes the writer "may use as structure"); §11 row "Padmāvatī as flawless heroine or villain".
- **Evidence:** the narrator calls her *dhūrtā* at 134 and calls the minister's son *dhūrta* at 172 (SRC). Verse 195 faults the king for not knowing *dhūrtacarita*.
- **Problem:** in Hindi, a narrator's "cunning" (धूर्त, चालाक) applied to a woman evokes a long-standing trope about women's guile. Voiced by the narrator, it is a judgement, it feeds failure sign F4 (the villain reading), and Charter §3.3.1 bars collective judgements about women. The blueprint marks the echo as usable and imposes no limit.
- **Minimum action (drafting constraint):** if the echo is used, it is never applied to her alone, never as the narrator's verdict, and never in a form that evokes the trope. Her intelligence (she identifies the sign-reader, 134–138) is shown as intelligence.

### Finding 8 — MODERATE (drafting constraint; one line in §6). The family's standing: the asymmetry is political power, not poverty or worth

## 4. Honest-difficulty test (D-041; Charter §3.5)

### 4.1 Strongest case for the king's responsibility

1. **Office and power.** Only he could sentence, and the duty to examine came with that power (194–195, SRC).
2. **His checks were real but not independent.** The necklace was hers (178), and his trusted woman confirmed the mark (179). Both were **products of the scheme the account explains**; only the account supplied the *ḍākinī* reading, and he formed his conviction from those things (180).
3. **He sentenced on the accuser's word.** After his conviction formed he asked the "ascetic", the only source of the accusation, what the punishment should be, and adopted it (180–181). That is the deepest procedural fault: the one who brings the charge decides the sentence.
4. **No hearing.** Nothing records that she was heard, and her parents lament at the expulsion (181) without being asked. A robbery report on record (167) contradicted the account (whether he knew of it is not recorded).
5. **The sentence and its foreseeable effect.** Expelling a young woman from the city, which in the tale means the forest (182), makes the parents' grief and their fear of her death predictable. Their fatal belief, "eaten in the forest" (185), is a belief about the sentence's natural result.
6. **Grief explains him but does not excuse him.** A duty to examine weighs most when feeling is strongest.

### 4.2 Strongest alternative case: the deaths rest on the minister's son and the prince

The king is not made blameless. He erred.

1. **They wrote the sentence.** The scheme's aim was to carry her off (150). The expulsion the king imposed was the accuser's own prescription (181 *tadgirā*), and it worked exactly as designed. The king's "making it real" was the scheme succeeding through him.
2. **His error was manufactured to be undetectable.** The account predicted a mark on her loins, and his own trusted woman found it. A confirmed prediction is the strongest corroboration a judge ordinarily gets. To see through it he would have had to suspect that the holy man himself had branded her, and nothing in front of him pointed that way. Blaming the deceived for being deceived is Betal's crows and geese.
3. **They held the truth that would have saved the parents.** The parents died of a false belief. The only people the tale shows knowing she was alive took her secretly to another kingdom (183–184). That makes it foreseeable that her family could not learn she lived. This needs no claim that the men knew what the parents believed, or that they deliberately kept them uninformed.
4. **But for.** Without the drink, the iron, the theft and the lie there is no mark, no story, no sentence, no removal. Every link in the chain except the sentence is theirs, and the sentence was their design.
5. **The deaths come at the end of their scheme, not of a royal policy.** The king acted once, as the scheme intended. They acted throughout.

### 4.3 Can a thoughtful reader genuinely defend the alternative?

**Yes.** Points 4.2.1 and 4.2.3 rest on recorded events (150, 181, 183–185), not on feeling. A reader who holds that whoever designs and steers a harmful outcome bears its consequences more than the deceived instrument has a real argument. **Can a reader still defend Vikram?** Also yes, through 4.1.3 and 4.1.5: a judge owns the sentence he adopts, and the fatal belief is a belief about that sentence. **The dilemma is honestly hard.** The defect is that the blueprint states the alternative more weakly than this (finding 1), and the reasoning the user will approve answers neither 4.2.1 nor 4.2.3.

**Other checks required by the task.**
- **Persuasive yet not independent evidence:** shown correctly in §10 counter-case 1 (the necklace and the mark come from the same scheme). Holds, subject to finding 13.
- **Recorded checks and unrecorded inquiries:** mostly fair. The consultation is miscounted (finding 2), the robbery report is missing (finding 9), and the parents' "presence" is overstated (finding 10).
- **The accomplices' wrongdoing:** visible in B10–B14 and §4.1, with mixed motives (§5.4) and no relish. They carry moral weight and are not caricatures.
- **Expulsion, removal and deaths kept apart:** the blueprint is careful in §4.1, B19 and IC-19 not to merge sequence with cause. It errs only in applying a knowledge condition to one route and not the other (finding 1).
- **Does the reasoning make the answer obvious or weaken Betal?** It does not make the answer obvious. Because it does not engage Betal's strongest form, it leaves Vikram's answer exposed rather than the dilemma balanced. It does **not** reduce the tale to condemning the schemers: the king remains the target, and the accomplices' guilt does not carry the answer (C7).
