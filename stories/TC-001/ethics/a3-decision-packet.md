## The tale in five lines (for orientation)

A prince and his friend, the minister's son, find Padmāvatī, an ivory-worker's daughter in another king's realm. She signals; they reach her house; she and the prince are united. After a dog dies eating food from her household, the minister's son **alleges** she tried to poison him (the narrator never confirms it) and plans to "carry her off". The minister's son instructs the prince to make her drink until she is motionless (KSS 154); the narrator reports her "motionless from liquor" and marked with a trident (160); **the act of giving the drink is not narrated**. The minister's son, disguised as an ascetic, tells the city chief **in private** a story of a circle of *yoginīs* in the cremation ground: a prince's heart offered to Bhairava, a drunk *yoginī* who tried to snatch his rosary, a trident mark he gave her, a necklace he took (172–177). The chief informs the king; the king recognises the necklace and his trusted woman looks and finds the mark (178–179); **the king himself** concludes that his son was seized by that *ḍākinī* (180), asks the "ascetic" what her punishment should be, and **expels her on his word** (181). (Records: [chronology.md](../research/chronology.md) K28–K34; [sources.md](../research/sources.md) §6.2.) The two men collect her from the forest, alive and grieving, and take her to their own kingdom. Her father, believing her eaten in the forest, dies of grief, and her mother "follows him". Betal asks whose guilt the parents' deaths are: the minister's son's, the prince's or Padmāvatī's. In every witness, Vikram's answer is **the king**.

**Already fixed, not asked again:** the answer is the king (D-048); Betal's objection stays at full strength; the accomplices' wrongs are not excused; the branding is shown as a violation; the poison stays unresolved; no penalty formula in the tale (D-057B).

## A. The composed reasoning (blueprint §9.5), PROPOSED

**Why you are asked:** the reasoning is the project's own composition, not the source's (KSS's reasons, 12.8.192–195, excuse the accomplices). D-041 makes composed reasoning your decision.

**What you would approve (outline; not wording for the tale):**
1. **The prince's and the minister's son's acts are wrongs of their own**: the drink, the iron, the theft, the lie told to authority. Neither service to a friend nor love cancels them. (This replaces KSS 192–193.)
2. **Padmāvatī is measured by the same standard of conduct and of evidence.** The poison charge comes only from her accuser, was never put to her, and is not treated as proof. She is neither cleared nor condemned. If Vikram mentions the food at all, he says no more than that the charge came from her accuser and was never tested. Her being named in the question never implies a share in her parents' deaths.
3. **Facing the strongest alternative** (set out in full in item F), Vikram's **proposed reply** is:
   - a judge owns the sentence he adopts;
   - letting the only accuser set the punishment is the very fault the source names (194–195: no inquiry; ignorant of rogues);
   - the parents' grief begins at the expulsion (181), and the belief that killed the father, "eaten in the forest" (185), is a belief about what that sentence would bring;
   - the accuser's design and the removal stay in the chain, but they do not move the sentence away from the man who pronounced it.
4. **The duty to examine was the king's office.** He made **two real checks** (he recognised the necklace and received his trusted woman's report of the mark). His conviction formed from them, and **only then** did he ask the "ascetic" about her punishment. Both checks examined what the scheme itself had produced. Nothing records a hearing of her, an inquiry of her parents, an independent test of the account, or an examination of his son's death. (Separately, a robbery report existed (167); who made it and whether the king knew of it is not recorded, OQ-21. It is an unknown, not an omission.)
5. The prince also acted on one adviser's word without asking her. This is a structural echo, shown through events, never a speech.

**What the reasoning does not claim:** that the sentence was the only cause of the parents' belief; that the accuser's design or the removal is irrelevant; that the reply defeats the alternative.

**Please note before you approve.** The reply answers the first counter-argument (the accuser wrote the sentence) directly. It answers the second (the men held the truth that she lived) only by locating the origin of the parents' belief in the sentence. It does not refute the point that the belief persisted while the two men knew otherwise. That is deliberate: it keeps the dilemma honestly hard (D-041). But it means a reader may finish the tale disagreeing with Vikram.

## C. Confirming the removal of both source exemptions, and whether that is a dilemma change (§9.2, §9.6), PROPOSED

**The source:** after answering "the king", KSS's Vikram clears the others: the minister's son served his master's business (192); Padmāvatī and the prince were "scorched by Kāma's arrows" and set on their own aim (193). Only KSS has these exemptions and the crow-and-geese objection.

**The proposal:** do not inherit either exemption. D-048 directs this ("the king's duty to inquire must not erase their responsibility"). Service and passion would otherwise excuse a violation done to a woman.

**Effect:** KSS's dilemma is "the king against nobody", because the others are cleared in advance. The adaptation makes it "the king against people who really did wrong". That makes the case against Vikram stronger and the dilemma harder, not easier.

**Your two answers:**
1. **Confirm the recorded direction (default: remove both exemptions), or change it?** (Keeping them would conflict with D-048 and the charter, so the reviewer sees no ethical alternative to removal.)

Either way the reasoning is composed and covered by item A. The classification decides how the change is recorded and described. It does not change the tale.

## F. The strongest counter-case, as you would accept it (§10; §9.8 (f)), PROPOSED

You are asked to approve the reasoning **knowing what it must answer**. This is the alternative at full strength. Labels: **SRC** narrated in KSS; **INF** inference; **UNV** not established.

**The case that the deaths rest more on the deceivers than on the king** (he is not made blameless):
1. **The sentence served the accuser's design.** The minister's son's own words set the aim: a means for carrying her off (150, SRC). The king asked him what her punishment should be and expelled her on his word (181 *tadgirā*, SRC). The sentence was the accuser's prescription and delivered his aim (INF). The source does not say he planned this exact sentence beforehand (UNV).
2. **The removal.** When her parents died believing her eaten, the two men had her **alive** in their own kingdom (183–185, SRC). The tale shows no one else learning she lived (INF from silence). Their taking her to another kingdom may have made it harder for her parents to learn the truth (INF). **This needs no claim that the men knew what the parents believed, foresaw their deaths or withheld a message, and makes none.** The source establishes none of these, and does not say the removal was secret.
3. **The deception was built to be undetectable.** The scheme made the mark (160); the false account then described it (172–177); and the king's own trusted woman looked and found it (179). A checkable detail was checked and found true, which is the strongest kind of corroboration a judge ordinarily gets. Nothing in front of him pointed at the holy man as its maker.
4. **He did check, and he did not kill.** His two checks were real; the sentence was expulsion, not death.
5. **"But for."** Without their drink, iron, theft and lie there is no mark, no story, no sentence and no removal. Their acts are a necessary condition of the deaths, not the whole cause. A deceived man is the deceivers' victim, which is Betal's rice and geese.
6. **The standard may be too high.** Spies, polity and reading rogues are an ideal ruler's skills.
7. *(Unresolved reading, added by the re-check, N-2.)* KSS 150's first half, *bandhutyāgān mahātmanaḥ*, is read by Tawney as telling the prince to **persuade her to leave her relations**. On that reading, separating her from her family was part of the plan from the start. The project's own AI reading differs ("because of the abandonment of kin"), and a Sanskritist has not settled it. **The tale and the reasoning will not rely on either reading** until one does.

**The case for Vikram's answer:** only the king could sentence, and the duty to examine came with that power. His checks examined only what the scheme produced. He let the accuser set the sentence. No one heard her or her parents. Expelling a young woman into the forest makes her parents' fear of her death predictable (INF), and their fatal belief is exactly that fear. Grief explains him; it does not excuse him.

**Re-check result:** a thoughtful reader can defend either verdict on the events. Honest-difficulty test **PASS**.
