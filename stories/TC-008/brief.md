# TC-008 brief: The three fastidious brothers

Status: brief · Written 2026-11-27 · User look required: no (D-081)

## 1. Source beats
1. At the brahman settlement Vṛkṣaghaṭa in Aṅga, the wealthy sacrificer Viṣṇusvāmin sends his three sons to the sea to fetch a turtle for a sacrifice he has begun [KSS 12.15.3–5].
2. Each refuses to touch the slimy turtle: eldest fastidious about food, second about women, youngest about beds. The eldest warns that failing the sacrifice means hell; the others laugh that he knows duty only for others [KSS 6–14].
3. Quarrelling out of pride (*abhimāna*), they go to King Prasenajit to have their claims judged [KSS 15–18].
4. Food: the eldest will not eat the fine rice, which smells of corpse-smoke; it was grown beside a village cremation ground [KSS 19–26].
5. Women: a courtesan is sent to the second; he nearly faints and says she smells of goat; she says she was reared on goat's milk as an infant [KSS 27–37]. **Ours:** her upbringing is known in town, and she is given the scene and the joke.
6. Bed: a bed of seven mattresses; the youngest rises crying after half a watch; a red curved mark on his side; one hair under all seven [KSS 37–45].
7. The king rewards each; they stay on, forgetting the turtle, "having earned the sin of obstructing their father's sacrifice" [KSS 46–47].
8. Betal asks (48–49); Vikram answers (50–51); Betal returns (52).

Variants: Ś 23 and Hindi XXIII give the same answer with no reason (Hindi: hair "in the seventh fold"). Only KSS gives the evidential reason. J 5 not documented (Emeneau calls it careless). Position varies (KSS 8, Ś and Hindi 23, J 5).

## 2. Riddle and verdict
- Betal's question: of these three, who is the most fastidious (KSS 12.15.49)?
- Source answer and reasoning: the bed-connoisseur; the hair's imprint was seen with one's own eyes on his body, while the other two could have learned their facts beforehand (12.15.50–51).
- Our answer: **same**.
- Honest-difficulty: knowing about a thing beforehand is suspicion, not proof, and a sense that detects a hidden cause (smoke in rice) is no less delicate. The mark was seen by the king's men, so even the bed test depends on others' eyes.

## 4. Charter points
- Satire target: the brothers' own *abhimāna*, refinement turned into rank, and their contradiction: disgust so fine it abandons the duty it serves (§3.2.5a, §3.2.8). It is *these three men's* vanity, never a birth group's. Counterweights: their father is an earnest sacrificer left with an unfinished rite; the king tests coolly.
- The corpse-smoke rice carries the purity-pollution idea. Show the people who farm and work by the burning ground briefly as people, so the disgust visibly lands on someone, with no commentary; the telling must not invite aversion to cremation-ground workers (§3.2.3, §3.5).
- The courtesan: keep her; give her the scene and the joke, so the laugh lands on the brother's vanity, not on her body (§3.3.2, §3.4.1). Her origin is exposed at her own pace; she is not disposable.
- Do not endorse any brother's purity claim; the king's verdict rests on evidence (§3.2.5b).
- The narrator's "sin" line (47) is the source's own; tell the brothers' forgetting of the turtle as irony in events, without a sermon.

## 5. Voice plan
- Hidden layer: heard fast, a comedy of fussy brothers; heard slowly, it asks what we call delicacy when it excuses us from a duty.
- "And you too": (a) at the turtle, what you too would not touch, and who then has to; (b) at the seven mattresses, the smallest thing you could not ignore.
- Odd details needing motive: why a father sends sons for a turtle (source silent; do not invent ritual claims); why the king tests them (curiosity, a ruler's amusement, 26, 37, 44); why the courtesan was reared on goat's milk (separated from mother and nurse as a baby, 27–37).
- Plot object: the single hair under seven mattresses; the smallest thing felt most.
