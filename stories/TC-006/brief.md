# TC-006 brief: The exchanged heads

Status: brief · Written 2026-11-27 · User look required: no (D-081)

## 1. Source beats
1. In Śobhāvatī (King Yaśaḥketu) stands a great temple of Gaurī with a sacred lake and an annual bathing festival [KSS 12.13.4–6].
2. Dhavala, a young washerman, sees Madanasundarī, a washerman's daughter, bathing; lovesick, he stops eating; his father arranges the marriage [KSS 7–17].
3. Her brother invites them to a festival at her father's house; the three set out [KSS 18–22].
4. At the Gaurī temple Dhavala goes in alone; moved "by fate" (*vidhipreraṇa*, 28) he takes a sword left by pilgrims, ties his hair to the bell-chain and beheads himself [KSS 23–31].
5. The brother goes in to look, and, "deluded" (*vyāmohita*, 33), beheads himself [KSS 32–33].
6. Madanasundarī finds both, reproaches the goddess, and prepares to hang herself, praying for the same husband and brother in every birth [KSS 34–43].
7. A voice from the sky says the goddess is pleased with her courage (*sattvotkarṣa*, 44): join each head to its body and both will live [KSS 43–45].
8. In joyful haste she joins each head to the wrong body [KSS 46–47]. Both rise; they tell each other what happened and walk on; she is bewildered [KSS 48–50].
9. Betal asks; Vikram answers; Betal returns [KSS 51–54].

Variants: Ś 6 and Hindi VI give the same answer (verse "the head is chief"; Hindi cites a "book of law"). J 8 unread; bodies of Ś and Hindi not read.

## 2. Riddle and verdict
- Betal's question: of the two mixed men, who is her husband (KSS 12.13.51)?
- Source answer and reasoning: the one on whom her husband's head is set; the head is chief of the limbs and identity (*pratyabhijñā*, "recognition") resides in it (12.13.53).
- Our answer: **same**, with the full KSS reasoning (SLATE_AUDIT §5). The Hindi "book of law" citation is not used (charter §3 principle 7).
- Honest-difficulty: a reader may say that the body she married, held and knows, or the man's memory and character, make someone else her husband. The tale gives no account of what the men remember or of how they live afterwards.

## 4. Charter points
- Two self-beheadings and a hanging attempt, ending in divine reprieve (§3.4.3): do not present self-sacrifice as virtue. Keep "fate" and "delusion" as an uncanny compulsion at the shrine; invent no loving vow (SLATE_AUDIT §5). Dread, not gore.
- Her despair is despair, not virtue. The goddess's answer is mercy, not an endorsement of the offerings.
- Her haste is joy in darkness, not "female flightiness" (§3.3.1, §3.3.5). The error is human, and the men's passivity is not her fault to answer for alone.
- The characters are washers (*rajaka*). Keep as their work and community, never their worth or birth-duty (§3.2.4, §3.2.5a); a working-class trio is the protagonists (SLATE_AUDIT §5.3).
- Keep her present at the end: she must live with the answer. She does not choose or ratify (§3.3.6).
- Honest-difficulty (§3.5): the head principle is argued as a reading, not a law; avoid a body-hierarchy tone.

## 5. Voice plan
- Hidden layer: a comedy that is funny only after you have been frightened; heard slowly, it asks what a person is.
- "And you too": (a) at her hanging-rope, how little we know of what we would do when everything is lost; (b) at the swap, a time joy made you hurry into something you could not undo.
- Odd details: why Dhavala goes in alone and why the brother follows (source gives only fate and delusion; leave it unexplained and say so honestly, no invented motive); why a sword lies in the shrine (left by pilgrims, KSS 24–25).
- Plot object: the two heads and the sword; wisdom grows from the thing she holds in her hands.
- Callbacks: none.
