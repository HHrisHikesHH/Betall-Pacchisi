# TC-025 brief: Four brothers and the courtesan (J 21)

Status: brief · Written 2026-11-27 · User look required: yes (D-081: verdict reasoning is our own composition, D-041)

## 1. Source beats
All [source: J 21, Emeneau Tr pp. 109–125] unless marked. Base is J: KSS, Ś and Hindi lack the tale (bucket §3).
1. Merchant Nidhipatidatta of Puṣkaravatī has four sons, each master of one art: music and dance, arms, "all the sciences", *nīti*.
2. After his death the brothers separate because their wives quarrel; three divine jewels stay common, to go to whom the king chooses.
3. The king sets a test: the courtesan Rūpavatī of Kusumapura takes a man one night only; whoever stays two nights wins.
4. The three elder brothers each get one night and are refused a second.
5. Kanakadatta reasons that wealth and valour failed. He serves her teacher, the ascetic Puṇyaśarīrā, six months under a false identity; hidden in a corner, he overhears Rūpavatī's story.
6. Her story: a minister's daughter, goddess-granted memory of past births, abandoned by Prince Pratāpasena in battle; reborn as cow-elephant, doe and *cakravāka*, each time recognising him in an animal of the same kind, each time left; reborn a courtesan, she vows never to take a man twice.
7. Kanakadatta tells every courtesan the same story from the man's side, as if he were Pratāpasena pining for her; it is put on a cloth.
8. Rūpavatī hears it, brings him home, weeps in public, gives up her vow. He takes her and her wealth home and secretly tells the king everything; he gets the jewels and the ministry.
Variants: Newari 21 (German only) differs in the third brother's art, cause of dispute, the false story (adds events), and has an explicit marriage; same answer in substance. The cloth may be painted or written (N3 §2 "Evidence and uncertainty"). Whether she ever learns the truth is not stated.

## 2. Riddle and verdict
- Betal's question (source): "she, though remembering her births, did she not recognise Kanakadatta" (T p. 124 *sā jātismarāpi kathaṃ kanakadattaṃ na jñātavatī*; N3 gloss: "A 'why did she fail to recognise?' question").
- Source answer (verbatim as the files give it): "In each earlier birth she had union with her own husband, and by that merit (*puṇya*) she recognised him. Born into a courtesan's family she became 'very wicked' (*pāpiṣṭhā*); through union with other men she forgot her own husband, although she still remembered her former births (T p. 124; Tr p. 125)." Note: the repo holds N3's rendering of Emeneau, not his full English; check Tr p. 125 before quoting further.
- Our answer: **changed (D-041)**; question kept. Audit recommendation (SLATE_AUDIT §5, TC-025): MAJOR REWRITE, L–M, "Moral worth is set by birth into a courtesan family... Victim-blaming, while the fraudster is made minister." Do not endorse *pāpiṣṭhā* (D-048). §6 item 3 ranks this the third most serious problem.
  - **PROPOSED A (audit's candidate, needs the user's approval; no DECISIONS entry approves any composition for this tale):** "Her gift let her know a body and a deed in every life. This time she was given only words, the one story she had waited four lives to hear. Memory of past births tells you who you were, not who is lying."
  - **PROPOSED B (alternative, ours):** she was not deceived by want of sight but by what the story gave her: four abandonments taught her that his return mattered more than whether it was true. Grief, not guilt; no verdict on courtesans.
- Source position at full strength (audit: not a strawman): the life she chose or was born into changed how she reads men; a woman who has read a stranger's face every night for years should have seen through him.
- Honest difficulty: a reader can hold that she should have known (the story was too neat, she had four lives of evidence), or that no one tests the one story she most needs.

## 4. Charter points
- Verdict by birth/occupation (§3.2.2 by analogy, §3.3.3 victim-blaming): replaced by composed reasoning, labelled ours in every record (§3.5, D-041).
- Deception for sex and gain is rewarded (§3.3.3): show the cost to her; no "she was really happy" gloss; Kanakadatta not a hero.
- Her humanity and agency (D-048): her vow is a reasoned response to four abandonments, not a flaw; agency through her story and interiority, no invented consent (§3.3.6).
- Men as a group: her "men are cruel" is her belief after Pratāpasena, not the tale's (§3.3.1, 3.3.5); Kanakadatta and the king must not stand for "men".
- Female ascetic Puṇyaśarīrā is a real teacher whose trust is used; religious figure kept unmarked (§3.2.8).
- Courtesan pattern (SLATE_AUDIT §5.3): central here; not object, predator or "fallen".
