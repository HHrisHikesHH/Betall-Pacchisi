## 1. Narrative premise and point of view

### 1.1 Premise
A king who has accepted ten years of costly gifts without asking why agrees, out of that unexamined debt, to help a stranger with a night rite. He is sent to carry a hanged dead man across a cremation ground. The dead man is inhabited by Betal, who tells him tales and puts questions under a penalty: if the king knows the answer and stays silent, his head will burst. Each correct answer costs the king his task, because speaking sends Betal back to the tree. At the one question the king cannot answer, the pattern breaks. Betal, who has deceived him all night, tells him that the man he is carrying the body to intends to kill him, and shows him how to strike first. The king must decide on the word of the least trustworthy voice he knows. He kills; the frame leaves what that was open.

One night. One appointment. One long walk with a repeated structure, and an ending that arrives because of a failure.

### 1.2 The causal chain (every link is source-based)
| Link | Cause in the source | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Why the king goes | Ten years of daily fruit with a gem hidden in each; he consents to help before he knows what for (KSS 12.8.23–36) | Patronage-debt. The loyalty the charter wants examined (§3.4.5). |
| Why he must keep answering | Betal's penalty for knowing and staying silent (12.8.188–189) | The king's virtue (honest judgment) keeps undoing his errand. |
| Why he is warned | He fails the last question; the text says he did not know (12.31.57–62) | Not a wise refusal. His ignorance, not his wisdom, saves him. |
| Why he kills | Betal's plan and instruction, with a reward attached (12.31.65–73) | The monk never says he will kill; his only words to the king describe the prostration as winning "success" (12.32.15–16). The plan is known **only** from Betal (A1 C7). |

### 1.3 Register
Mysterious, wry, increasingly tense. Entertainment first (North Star); the charter works through events. Comedy at the start (the monkey and the gem), dread from the cremation ground onward, sharper dialogue in the middle, a long held breath at the end. The frame never explains itself.

### 1.4 Point of view and voice
**Knowledge discipline (DEFAULT, drafting rule).** The frame is anchored on what Vikram sees and learns. The reader is never told more about the monk's intentions than Vikram is. The monk is seen only from outside. Betal is seen only from outside, with one exception noted below. This is what makes the late warning a shared surprise, and it keeps the monk's intent a matter of testimony, not fact.

**Betal's mind (R2 sub-question).** KSS enters Betal's reasoning at 12.31.61–64 in the narrator's voice. Default: show his private laugh (attested) and let his reasons arrive only as his own speech, which the reader cannot verify.

**Voice (R8).** Q-005 is open and the proposal withdrew "an unnamed narrator tells the frame" because it would half-decide Q-005. The drafting rule is therefore only a **voice-neutrality rule**: no first person, no address to a listener or reader, no narratorial wisdom, no stated persona or gender, no "it is said". This is not a claim that the voice is "an unnamed narrator"; it is an instruction to leave the seams open.

**Seam list (places where Q-005 would change the text):**
1. The first line of the frame.
2. The line that opens each tale ("who says Betal began").
3. The narrator's gloss that Betal's returns are "to gauge the king's firmness" (12.8.196), which the default omits.
4. Access to Betal's mind (12.31.61–64).
5. The reader-facing blessing and the closing image (B30, B35).
6. KSS's own embedding: the frame is told inside Mṛgāṅkadatta's story, relayed from an old brahman (12.8 intro; Tawney only, Sanskrit unread; medium confidence). Dropping it is a reduction.

## 2. Opening and transition into the frame

The frame has **two crossings**: from the collection's introduction into the court (B01–B02), and from the day-world into the night-world (B06–B07, sequenced in §3 because they belong to the arrangement).

**Entry.** Chronological, not a cold open on the tree. Reason: the source's first hook is not the corpse but a question: why does a man bring one fruit a day for ten years? The monkey and the gem answer *what*, not *why*. A cold open on the tree would spend the debt, which is the cause of everything. An optional one-line image of the tree (ADAPT) may precede B01 if the drafting session wants an earlier hook; it must not reveal the corpse speaks.

"Ten years": Ś/J give 12 in places; not used. King and city names: §13 G4. | Establishes ease, power, and **taking without asking**. ## 3. The ascetic's arrangement with Vikram

The arrangement is the most important thing the source does not give. **It has no terms.** The king asks for a reason only after ten years; he then agrees to an unnamed rite; he goes to the appointment; he receives a one-line errand. No danger is named, no silence rule is stated by the monk (A1 C4), no penalty, no reward, no intent. Everything the king later knows about what the monk wants comes from Betal. This thinness is deliberate in the source's design (the gifts built the obligation so the terms never had to be negotiated), and the blueprint keeps it.

## 4. The corpse's repeated returns and Betal's storytelling

### 4.1 The loop, in its attested order
| Step | What happens | Basis | Who controls it |
|---|---|---|---|
| L1 | The king reaches the tree and cuts the hanged man down. | KSS 12.8.49 | King |
| L2 | The corpse cries out, then laughs. The king infers a Vetāla lives in it. It is back in the tree. | KSS 12.8.50–54 (H); inference at 12.8.54 | Betal |
| L3 | The king climbs again and carries the body **in silence** on his shoulder. | KSS 12.8.57 | King |

| L5 | The tale ends in a question, with the penalty: if he knows and is silent, his head bursts. | KSS 12.8.188–189 (penalty first stated after tale 1) | Betal |
| L6 | The king answers; speaking is how he keeps from dying. | KSS 12.8.196 | The penalty |
| L7 | Betal goes back to the tree by illusion. | KSS 12.8.196 | Betal |

**What the loop shows about power.** Neither party is a captive. Betal holds a real **death-threat** (the penalty); the king holds the sword and the body. The monk's leverage is the debt. Three coercions (debt, penalty, steel) run through one night, and none of them is clean. No tale-verdict is a free answer: each is a judgment under pressure (proposal §8.3).

### 4.3 The elided middle (B12–B14: movement beats)
The loop is shown **fully twice** (here, and at the climax, where it breaks) and **elided in between** (proposal §8.3). Each tale gets short bracketing segments (half to two pages). Each segment varies one or two of: the walk; the weather or hour; a fragment of Betal's experience; a reaction to the last answer; a hook to the next tale. Nothing repeats as a formula. The beats below are **families**, not fixed scenes; they attach to whichever tales ship (see §11).

| ID | Movement beat family | Basis | What it does |
|---|---|---|---|
| B12 | **Contract.** The opening tales. Mechanics, irritation, humour. The king answers fast and sure. Betal needles and objects (an attested device: Betal's objections in KSS 1, 4, 17). | Proposal §8.3; objections KSS per proposal; the rest ADAPT | Establishes the game and the king's competence. His confidence is the thing the night will test. |
| B13 | **Friction.** Middle tales that turn on rulers. Walks shorten, exchanges sharpen. People of the burning ground and the road are seen in passing as people. The king's answers begin to cost him something. | Proposal §8.3; the people: ADAPT | The night gets long. The king's fatigue is physical, not announced. |
| B14 | **Doubt.** Late tales. The king asks Betal questions back. A few deniable oddities about the night (Betal's slowing, his interest in what the monk has given) seed the later claim that the obstacles had a purpose. | Questions-back: proposal §8.3. Seeds: ADAPT; a Ś tale-9 remark is a provisional OCR lead (§13 G8), **not** used | Gives the late warning a retrospective fairness without spending it. Whether the king changes is left to the reader. |

**Rules for the middle:**
- No announced growth. The king is not "improved". The reader may feel the night, but no beat says what it did to him.
- Betal never lies about the rules; he deceives only in play (flight, laughter, misdirection). This is **ADAPT**. It is what makes the king's late trust intelligible without sentiment, and it is the one invention the frame's emotional logic leans on hardest (§5.3).
- Placement echoes, not themes (proposal §8.3, **not re-verified against the tale files**): a tale where a king lets a child die for his safety (TC-020) sits shortly before the reveal that the ascetic means to sacrifice *Vikram*; TC-022's aside (he answers knowing it frees Betal) marks the turn into Doubt; TC-001 and TC-027 both answer "the king is guilty". No tale is rewritten to fit the frame.

## 5. The relationship between Vikram and Betal

The source gives almost nothing here beyond its endpoints: Betal says he will ease the road (start), and Betal praises the king's courage and the king trusts his word (end). Everything between is **ADAPT**, hung on those two attested endpoints. It must reach "trusts the word of the one who tormented him" without making them friends.

### 5.1 Movements
| Movement | Vikram | Betal | What the reader feels |
|---|---|---|---|
| Contract | Confident, practical, a little annoyed | A trickster setting terms; a performer | Play; a puzzle-box |
| Friction | Tired, less sure; answers cost more | Sharper; contests answers at full strength (no strawman: charter §3.5) | The game turns cold |
| Doubt | Asks questions back; begins to answer strategically | Less theatrical; slows the pace; probes | Unease: the game is about something |
| Silence (TC-024) | Does not know | Stays; does not return to the tree | The pattern breaks |

### 5.2 What the relationship is not
Not friendship, not mentor and pupil, not captor and prisoner, not teacher and student. It is a **negotiation between parties who each hold something over the other**: the king carries the body and the sword; Betal holds the penalty and the pace. It ends at the same place it started in one respect: Betal owes the king nothing, and neither asks the other for kindness.

### 5.3 Why the king can trust the warning
Not warmth. **Betal has kept his terms all night.** Every tale was a real question. The penalty was applied the same way each time. The only deceptions were play. The king's trust at B21 is the trust one gives an adversary who has never cheated on the rules, and it is *still* a gamble: Betal incites the killing (A1 §4 item 3) and attaches a reward to it. The reader should be able to suspect the warning is itself a trick. (ADAPT, built on KSS 12.31.65–73.)

### 5.4 If R5 becomes "freed"
A "freed" ending needs a captive Betal. KSS shows none (A1 §3.1); the captive-Betal strand is J/H only and attaches a victim backstory (oilman) the proposal excludes as caste-coded. It would require earlier beats that make his presence in the corpse feel like confinement, and a release that no source supplies. That is why the default is departure.

### 5.5 Betal's fragments
Betal's "past stays a mystery he reveals on his own terms" (proposal §8.2 item 2). Blueprint rule: any fragment (ADAPT) is **experiential, never genealogical**: what it is like to be in a hanged man, the tree, the cold, the smell of the ground. No caste, occupation, victim story, or origin. Whether any fragment is *true* is left unresolvable.

## 6. TC-024 as the fixed climax

TC-024 is the tale of the exiled king and the cross-generation marriages ([CANDIDATE_TALE_CATALOGUE.md](../../docs/research/CANDIDATE_TALE_CATALOGUE.md); evidence in SOMADEVA_TALE_EVIDENCE.md, entry 024, KSS 12.31.1–75). The tale is written in its own file. This section covers what the **frame** does around it.

| ID | Beat | Basis | What it does |
|---|---|---|---|
| B15 | The lead-in: Betal says he has one **great question**, not another tale. | KSS 12.31.4 (the Vetāla announces "one great question", *mahāpraśna*) | Signals weight without announcing the end. Must be written **without counting tales**, so insertions do not break it. |
| B16 | After the tale, the question. The king thinks it over in many ways. He does not know. He walks on in silence. | KSS 12.31.57–60 (H). The text states ignorance (*nājñāsīt*), not choice. | The one failure. It must not read as wisdom, dignity, or a "wise refusal" (A1 §4; N1 caution). |
| B17 | **The loop breaks.** Betal does not return to the tree. | B18 | Betal laughs, privately, on the king's shoulder. | KSS 12.31.61 (H). The reasons that follow at 12.31.61–64 are R2-gated: default is **laugh only**. | The reader sees the first sign that Betal has decided something. |

**Fixed ordering:** nothing is inserted between B16 and B19. New tales go before B15. The lead-in (B15) and the segment after the preceding tale are adjacent and may be fused when a tale is added; any change goes through the frame's revision cycle (proposal §8.5).

**What the tale echoes (not themes, not rewrites):** two lines of footprints followed; a king killed defending his family; a woman's coerced remarriage stated plainly (KSS 12.31.50); a father who must be *pressed* by his son. A frame that wants one echo may use the **following of traces** (the king follows the ascetic's path as the hunters followed footprints). Optional (ADAPT). **Flag for the TC-024 session, not resolved here:** the robbers in the tale are forest people (*śabara*, Bhil); the charter pattern audit (§3.2.9) applies.

## 7. Betal's late warning and its consequences

### 7.1 What the warning contains (all attested together: KSS 12.31.65–73)
He praises the king's endurance and courage; says he is leaving the body, "carry it"; the monk will call him into it and honour him, then ask the king for an eight-limbed prostration to offer him; the king should say "show me first" and behead the monk as he bows; then the power the monk wants will be the king's ("enjoy the earth through that offering"); if the king does not, the monk will make the king the offering; "for this purpose I put obstructions in your way for so long."

It gives the king **plan, method, reward and a retrospective reason**. It is also **instigation to kill** with a prize attached.

| ID | Beat | Basis | What it does |
|---|---|---|---|
| B19 | Betal speaks the warning. | KSS 12.31.65–73 (H for content; M for the reading of 12.31.73, A1 §3.3). Motive: R2. Prize: R3. | The reader learns what the monk may intend **only on Betal's word**. R3 default: the prize stays in the warning (attested, and it is the incitement) but is never shown delivered. |
| B20 | Betal leaves the corpse. The king carries an empty body. | KSS 12.31.74 | The weight changes: he has carried a companion all night; now he carries cargo. A new silence. First time the body is only a body. |
| B21 | The king, trusting what he has heard, goes to the mendicant as to an enemy, carrying the body. | KSS 12.31.75 ("*ahitam*" is the narrator's word) | He is now **concealing** and **calculating**. The act he is about to commit is a choice he can still decline. The reader can see the alternatives (refuse the rite, ask openly, leave) without anyone naming them. |

### 7.2 What the warning costs
- **The king** loses his one source of attested honesty (he had been answering truthfully all night) and walks into a ruse. He will lie to a man who has been courteous to him.
- **Betal** gives up the game, his hold on the king, and the pace of the night. He also commits himself to a bet: if the king fails, he has helped the monk. His private reasoning, if shown, would be the one place the frame breaks its outside-only view (R2).
- **The monk** is not yet touched by it; everything the warning predicts is still in the future.

### 7.3 Betal's possible stakes (attested pieces only)
The monk "will not stop" (12.31.63, compact; A1 D4: a *reading*, not a clear statement). The monk calls Betal by spells and worships him (12.32.12–14). Betal in fact receives the monk's head and heart from the king (12.32.19). Whether he would have received the king as the monk's offering is **inference**: the sources read for A1 say the king is to be "offered up" in a rite Betal presides over, not explicitly to whom. These are facts about the situation, not a stated motive. R2 is where they are combined.

## 8. The final confrontation

The confrontation is short in time and long in dread. KSS gives it in few beats; the blueprint keeps that brevity. Tension lives in **what the king knows that the monk does not**, and in **what the reader cannot confirm**.

| ID | Beat | Basis | What it does |
|---|---|---|---|
| B22 | The circle: bone-dust and blood, fat-lamps, fire, the monk ash-smeared in corpse-garments, alone. He praises the king's steadfastness. | KSS 12.32.1–8 (H). **Skull-cup and sectarian iconography omitted by D-053.** R1 label. | The rite's setting. The monk's warmth is unforced; nothing in it confirms or denies Betal's account. |
| B23 | He bathes and garlands the body, calls Betal into it with spells, and worships him with offerings. | KSS 12.32.9–14 (H) | The ascetic's only shown craft. Betal is now back in the corpse and **silent**: the first time he has not spoken since the first loop. Whether the spell binds him is shown, not explained. |
| B24 | The monk tells the king to prostrate himself. His own words frame it as the way to the king's desired success. | KSS 12.32.15–16 (H) | Matches Betal's prediction at one point. It does not match it at the other: the monk says nothing about killing. |
| B25 | The king says he does not know how and asks to be shown. | KSS 12.32.17 (H) | The king's ruse, against a man who has just praised him. A lie told to a trusting man. |
| B26 | The monk prostrates. The king beheads him. | KSS 12.32.18 | The act. No dying words (see below). |
| B27 | The king tears out the heart and gives head and heart to Betal. (R4) | KSS 12.32.19 (H; corrects F4 in proposal §8.6) | The king completes the monk's own rite with the roles reversed. The sharpest honest-difficulty beat in the frame. **No applause, no host.** |

**No dying words.** KSS gives the monk none. Adding a confirming line ("I meant to kill you") would clear Vikram; adding an innocent one ("it would have made you a king") would condemn him. Both are ADAPT and both end the ambiguity D-043 wants kept. The monk dies in the act of doing what he asked, and the reader is left with exactly what the king knew.

**The unspoken debt.** The king's treasury was filled by the monk's ten years of gifts; the king now kills his donor. The blueprint wants this **felt, not stated**: the lamps still burning, the circle half-drawn, the ten-year cost. (Source-derived irony; no beat says it.)

## 10. The closing coda and transition to the collection's end

### 12.2 Religious stereotyping, in more detail
- **Rule:** the frame objects to the **plan**, not the **practice**. Nothing says the rite is wicked in itself. What is wrong is, if Betal is believed, an intent to kill a man who trusted him. That separation is the central safeguard.
- **Specialised knowledge is Essential; sect is not** (charter §3.6, proposal §8.2 item 1).
- **Kept:** ash, lamps, circle, fire, a corpse called by spell, offerings. **Cut:** skull-cup and other Kāpālika-coded objects. **Soft:** "human teeth, eyes, flesh" in KSS 12.32.13–14 is a macabre inventory; default is to drop the inventory and keep that the offerings are dreadful, so the human-parts detail does not do the sectarian work.
- **Honest disagreement.** One view: any rite that ends in a sacrificial plan is the stock figure, label or no label, and only a sensitivity reader can say whether the stripped version still reads that way. Another: a fully generic villain with no craft is a worse, flatter character and a missed chance for a specific, patient, costly ambition. The blueprint takes the second view cautiously and defers to a reader.

### 12.3 Caste, power, human dignity
- No caste marker is placed on the ascetic, Betal, the hanged man or the king. Charter §3.2.5(c): idle tags may be dropped **after** the slate-level pattern audit (§3.2.9); that audit for the frame is pending.
- Burning-ground people: historically caste-coded. If used, they appear working and unharried; no "polluted" coding (§3.2.3). If the drafter cannot do this without a marker, cut them.
- The treasurer and servants are persons (§3.4.1). The king's secrecy (B06) leaves his own household unaware: the powerful accountable for what they do unseen.

**Weak points:** the long middle (24 tales) sags if the movements are announced or formulaic; the deniable seeds in B14 are the main defence. The late warning is the single biggest bet; it is source-faithful and the right structure.

### 12.5 Honest disagreements to preserve
1. **Is leaving the killing open evasion?** A reviewer may say that with the plot rewarding him, neutrality is not neutrality. Another that any resolution (justified or condemned) is invented. Unresolved.
2. **Is the king's silence at TC-024 a missed chance for wisdom?** The source says he did not know. Making it a choice would be a modern arc. Held as in source.
3. **Is Betal's offering-receipt a moral stain on Betal, or on the king, or on the rite?** The frame shows it and does not rank.
4. **Seeding clues early (B14) versus keeping the warning a pure surprise.** The seeds preserve fairness; a purist would call them spoilers. Default is deniable seeds only.
