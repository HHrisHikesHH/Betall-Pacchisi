## 1. Narrative premise and point of view

### 1.1 Premise
A king who has accepted ten years of costly gifts without asking why agrees, out of that unexamined debt, to help a stranger with a night rite. He is sent to carry a hanged dead man across a cremation ground. The dead man is inhabited by Betal. The corpse escapes once, as the king speaks to it (KSS states no cause either way); on the first carry Betal provokes the king into speaking, and the king's speech sends him back to the tree, so the king learns by use that speaking costs him his burden (D-057). Betal then tells tales and puts questions under a penalty: if the king knows the answer and stays silent, his head will burst. Each answer sends Betal back to the tree. At the one question the king cannot answer, the pattern breaks. Betal, who (in KSS's own narration) decides he can no longer go on deceiving this king, tells him that the man he is carrying the body to intends to kill him, and shows him how to strike first. The king must decide on the word of the least trustworthy voice he knows. He kills on Betal's instruction, and goes further in his own acts; the frame leaves what that was open.

One night. One appointment. One long walk with a repeated structure, and an ending that arrives because of a failure.

The plan is known **only** from Betal (A1 C7). The heart-tearing and the offering (12.32.19) are the king's own further acts, not Betal's attested instruction (D-058 item 3). |

### 1.3 Register
Mysterious, wry, increasingly tense. Entertainment first (North Star); the charter works through events. Comedy at the start (the monkey and the gem), dread from the cremation ground onward, sharper dialogue in the middle, a long held breath at the end. The frame never explains itself.

### 1.4 Point of view and voice
**Knowledge discipline (DEFAULT, drafting rule).** The frame is anchored on what Vikram sees and learns. The reader is never told more about the monk's intentions than Vikram is. The monk is seen only from outside. Betal is seen only from outside, with one exception noted below. This is what makes the late warning a shared surprise, and it keeps the monk's intent a matter of testimony, not fact.

**Betal's mind (R2 sub-question).** KSS enters Betal's reasoning at 12.31.61–64 in the narrator's voice. Default: show his private laugh (attested) and let his reasons arrive only as his own speech, which the reader cannot verify.

**Voice (R8).** Q-005 is open and the proposal withdrew "an unnamed narrator tells the frame" because it would half-decide Q-005. The drafting rule is therefore only a **voice-neutrality rule**: no first person, no address to a listener or reader, no narratorial wisdom, no stated persona or gender, no "it is said". This is not a claim that the voice is "an unnamed narrator"; it is an instruction to leave the seams open.

**Seam list (places where Q-005 would change the text):**
1. The first line of the frame.
2. The line that opens each tale ("who says Betal began").
3. The narrator's gloss that Betal's returns are "to gauge the king's firmness" (12.8.196), which the default omits.
4. Access to Betal's mind (12.31.61–64).
5. The reader-facing blessing and the closing image (B30, B35).
6. KSS's own embedding: the frame is told inside Mṛgāṅkadatta's story, relayed from an old brahman (12.8 intro; Tawney only, Sanskrit unread; medium confidence). Dropping it is a reduction.

## 2. Opening and transition into the frame

The frame has **two crossings**: from the collection's introduction into the court (B01–B02), and from the day-world into the night-world (B06–B07, sequenced in §3 because they belong to the arrangement).

**Entry.** Chronological, not a cold open on the tree. Reason: the source's first hook is not the corpse but a question: why does a man bring one fruit a day for ten years? The monkey and the gem answer *what*, not *why*. A cold open on the tree would spend the debt, which is the cause of everything. An optional one-line image of the tree (ADAPT) may precede B01 if the drafting session wants an earlier hook; it must not reveal the corpse speaks.

## 3. The ascetic's arrangement with Vikram

The arrangement is the most important thing the source does not give. **It has no terms.** The king asks for a reason only after ten years; he then agrees to an unnamed rite; he goes to the appointment; he receives a one-line errand. No danger is named, no silence rule is stated by the monk (A1 C4), no penalty, no reward, no intent. Everything the king later knows about what the monk wants comes from Betal. This thinness is deliberate in the source's design (the gifts built the obligation so the terms never had to be negotiated), and the blueprint keeps it.

**Two returns, not one.** The first return (L2) is KSS. KSS has the king address the corpse (12.8.54) and the corpse seen back on the tree as he speaks (12.8.55), and it **does not explicitly state whether the speech causes it**: speech and return coincide in the narrated sequence, and the text links them in time and neither states nor denies a causal link. The blueprint asserts neither causation nor its absence as a source fact (D-057, source statement corrected 2026-11-23). The return at L3a is an adaptation mechanic in which **Betal deliberately provokes the king to speak** and a return then visibly follows, shown by use and never stated as a rule, so that the reader has seen a return follow speech before any tale. The deliberate provocation, and a speech-then-return staged as its own loop before the first tale, are the adaptation. KSS itself narrates Betal's departure after the king breaks silence at later tale ends and has the king recognise it (G1), but formulates no opening rule; the KSS sequence at L2 is not presented as the first instance of such a rule. The returns at L7 are KSS's: they follow an answer, and KSS gives the narrator's gloss that they test the king's firmness (12.8.196). **No one states a silence rule** at the opening: KSS has none (A1 C4), and the blueprint does not attribute one to it.

**What the loop shows about power.** Neither party is a captive. Betal holds a **death-threat the king cannot test** (the penalty); the king holds the sword and the body. The monk's leverage is the debt. Three coercions (debt, penalty, steel) run through one night, and none of them is clean. No tale-verdict is a free answer: each is a judgment under pressure (proposal §8.3).

- No announced growth. The king is not "improved". The reader may feel the night, but no beat says what it did to him.

## 5. The relationship between Vikram and Betal

The source gives almost nothing here beyond its endpoints: Betal says he will ease the road (start), and Betal praises the king's courage and the king trusts his word (end). Everything between is **ADAPT**, hung on those two attested endpoints. It must reach "trusts the word of the one who tormented him" without making them friends.

### 5.1 Movements
| Movement | Vikram | Betal | What the reader feels |
|---|---|---|---|
| Contract | Confident, practical, a little annoyed | A trickster setting terms; a performer | Play; a puzzle-box |
| Friction | Tired, less sure; answers cost more | Sharper; contests answers at full strength (no strawman: charter §3.5) | The game turns cold |
| Doubt | Asks questions back; begins to answer strategically | Less theatrical; probes | Unease: the game is about something |
| Silence (TC-024) | Does not know | Stays; does not return to the tree | The pattern breaks |

### 5.2 What the relationship is not
Not friendship, not mentor and pupil, not captor and prisoner, not teacher and student. It is a **negotiation between parties who each hold something over the other**: the king carries the body and the sword; Betal holds the penalty and the pace. It does not end in friendship, a declaration of affection, a self-sacrifice or an apology (D-056). KSS has Betal acknowledge the king's trouble and offer a boon (12.32.20–29); that is as far as the source goes and as far as the frame goes.

### 5.3 What the king has to go on (replaces the earlier "why the king can trust the warning")
**The frame does not say that the king can trust Betal, or why.** The earlier version rested the trust on "Betal has kept his terms all night"; that was an invention and is removed (D-058 item 2). No other guarantee of Betal's honesty replaces it.

What the source gives the king is narrower, and it is source, not invention:
- **Specific predictions come true before he acts.** Betal predicts that the ascetic will call him into the corpse, honour him, and ask the king for an eight-limbed prostration (12.31.67–70, the range reviewer C gave; A1 gives 12.31.65–73). The ascetic does all three (12.32.9–16: B23, B24), and the king acts only after them (12.32.17).
- **These corroborate only those three things.** They do not show that the ascetic means to sacrifice the king, and they do not show why Betal acted. The intent stays Betal's report alone (A1 C7); the monk's own words are consistent with a benign or a lethal reading.
- **Betal's offer is also a prize.** He incites the killing and attaches a reward (12.31.72), and his claim that he made the obstacles for this (12.31.73) is his own claim. The reader should be able to suspect the warning is itself a trick.

The king's act is therefore still a gamble on one unreliable voice, partly checked and not settled. The narrator's word for his stance after the warning is *ahitam*, "unfriendly" (12.31.75).

### 5.4 If R5 becomes "freed"
A "freed" ending needs a captive Betal. KSS shows none (A1 §3.1); the captive-Betal strand is J/H only and attaches a victim backstory (oilman) the proposal excludes as caste-coded. It would require earlier beats that make his presence in the corpse feel like confinement, and a release that no source supplies. That is why the default is departure.

### 5.5 Betal's fragments
Betal's "past stays a mystery he reveals on his own terms" (proposal §8.2 item 2). Blueprint rule: any fragment (ADAPT) is **experiential, never genealogical**: what it is like to be in a hanged man, the tree, the cold, the smell of the ground. No caste, occupation, victim story, or origin. Whether any fragment is *true* is left unresolvable.

### 5.6 Betal's arc: a source ledger (D-056)
The arc keeps KSS as its structure. J is supplemental for **one** element. Everything else on the adaptation side is labelled.

**What stays true of Betal.** Mischievous and strategic. He incites the killing by a ruse and offers a prize. His claim that he made the obstacles to protect Vikram is his own claim, not independent proof of his earlier motives. **Not invented here:** a declaration of friendship or affection, a heroic self-sacrifice, a complete moral conversion, a sweeping apology, a claim that he first acted from sympathy, a claim that KSS makes self-preservation his first motive.

**How this meets the other rules.**
- **R2 is unchanged.** The narration shows his laugh and not his reasons; whether the self-preservation inference appears at all is a drafting choice, and if it does it arrives through his behaviour or his own speech, never as narrated fact (§1.4).
- **The self-preservation inference is fenced too.** Until B19, nothing Betal does to protect himself may point to the ascetic or the rite as the danger: that would be foreshadowing (D-058 item 1) and would break the knowledge discipline (§1.4). B10a carries no such hint. If the inference appears at all, it arrives at B18–B19, in his own speech or behaviour.
- **The growth is not a seed (D-058 item 1).** It concerns Betal and the king. It must not become planted evidence about the ascetic's intent, about Betal slowing, or about an interest in the monk's gifts, and it must not be announced (§4.3 rules).
- **Nothing beyond KSS's last scenes.** The explicit admiration, the end of the deception and the protective counter-plan are KSS and arrive at B18–B19; the earlier growth is the only invented part.
- **Pending a Sanskritist:** the scope of 12.31.61–64 and 12.31.63 (§13 G7).
- **Verse numbers.** The ledger's verse numbers are those of the approved decision text (D-056). A1 (chronology §1 rows 15, 20) gives the ranges 12.31.65–73 and 12.32.20–29; attributions inside them (12.31.72 for the beheading ruse and the prize, 12.31.67–70 in §5.3 for the three predictions, "acknowledges the trouble" in 12.32.20–29) were not checked verse by verse by A1 or by a specialist.

## 7. Betal's late warning and its consequences

### 7.1 What the warning contains (all attested together: KSS 12.31.65–73)
He praises the king's endurance and courage; says he is leaving the body, "carry it"; the monk will call him into it and honour him, then ask the king for an eight-limbed prostration to offer him; the king should say "show me first" and behead the monk as he bows; then the power the monk wants will be the king's ("enjoy the earth through that offering"); if the king does not, the monk will make the king the offering; "for this purpose I put obstructions in your way for so long."

It gives the king **plan, method, reward and a retrospective reason**. It is also **instigation to kill** with a prize attached.

| ID | Beat | Basis | What it does |
|---|---|---|---|
| B19 | Betal speaks the warning. | KSS 12.31.65–73 (H for content; M for the reading of 12.31.73, A1 §3.3). Motive: R2, with the ledger in §5.6. Prize: R3. | The reader learns what the monk may intend **only on Betal's word**. R3: the prize stays in the warning (attested, and it is the incitement) but is never shown delivered. Betal's praise and his claim that he made the obstacles "for so long" (12.31.73) are **his own claim**, not proof; in the minimum release the reader has seen only B09 and B10a, so the claim is not corroborated and the drafting must not pretend otherwise. What Betal instructs is **the ruse and the beheading**; the heart-tearing and the offering are treated, per D-056 and D-058 item 3, as the king's own further acts (§8, B27; whether "that offering" in 12.31.72 reaches them needs a Sanskritist, §13 G13). |
| B20 | Betal leaves the corpse. The king carries an empty body. | KSS 12.31.74 | The weight changes: the voice that spoke on his shoulder is gone and he carries cargo. A new silence. First time the body is only a body. |
| B21 | The king, trusting what he has heard, goes to the mendicant as to an enemy, carrying the body. | KSS 12.31.75 ("*ahitam*" is the narrator's word) | He is now **concealing** and **calculating**. The act he is about to commit is a choice he can still decline. The reader can see the alternatives (refuse the rite, ask openly, leave) without anyone naming them. |

### 7.2 What the warning costs
- **The king** acts on the word of a voice he has no means to check, and walks into a ruse. On Betal's instruction he will deceive a man who has spoken courteously to him (12.32.6–8).
- **Betal** gives up the game, his hold on the king, and the pace of the night. He also commits himself to a bet: if the king fails, he has helped the monk. His private reasoning, if shown, would be the one place the frame breaks its outside-only view (R2).
- **The monk** is not yet touched by it; everything the warning predicts is still in the future.

### 7.3 Betal's possible stakes (attested pieces only)
The monk "will not stop" (12.31.63, compact; A1 D4: a *reading*, not a clear statement). The monk calls Betal by spells and worships him (12.32.12–14). Betal in fact receives the monk's head and heart from the king (12.32.19). Whether he would have received the king as the monk's offering is **inference**: the sources read for A1 say the king is to be "offered up" in a rite Betal presides over, not explicitly to whom. These are facts about the situation, not a stated motive. R2 is where they are combined.

## 8. The final confrontation

The confrontation is short in time and long in dread. KSS gives it in few beats; the blueprint keeps that brevity. Tension lives in **what the king knows that the monk does not**, and in **what the reader cannot confirm**.

| ID | Beat | Basis | What it does |

He praises the king's steadfastness. **Staged with restraint, from the source-attested details of 12.32.1–8 only; nothing sect-coded or grotesque is added** (D-058 item 6). | KSS 12.32.1–8 (H). **Skull-cup and sectarian iconography omitted by D-053.** R1 label. | The rite's setting. **No dying words.** KSS gives the monk none. Adding a confirming line ("I meant to kill you") would clear Vikram; adding an innocent one ("it would have made you a king") would condemn him. Both are ADAPT and both end the ambiguity D-043 wants kept. The monk dies in the act of doing what he asked, and the reader is left with exactly what the king knew.

**The unspoken debt.** The king's treasury was filled by the monk's ten years of gifts; the king now kills his donor. The blueprint wants this **felt, not stated**: the lamps still burning, the circle half-drawn, the ten-year cost. (Source-derived irony; no beat says it.)

**The killing has two layers (D-058 item 3).** (1) *Betal's instruction:* say "show me first", behead the monk as he bows, with a prize attached (12.31.72). (2) *The king's own further acts:* the heart and the offering (12.32.19), which Betal's instruction does not cover (G13). The king remains the agent of both layers: he delivers the lie and the beheading Betal instructed, and he chose the rest. Neither the instruction nor the prize settles whether the king was right. The frame does not present the night as heroic triumph, and no voice, including Betal's, gives a verdict.

## 10. The closing coda and transition to the collection's end

KSS: Śiva appears with gods and praises the king for killing the *kūṭatāpasa* "greedy for the Vidyādhara empire"; says the king was first born as Vikramāditya to destroy asuras born as *mlecchas*, and now as Trivikramasena to put down an evildoer; gives the sword Aparājita; promises Vidyādhara sovereignty and then union with Śiva (12.32.31–38). ## 11. Segment architecture and insertion rules

### 11.1 Spine and slots
- **Spine** (always present, independent of the tale slate): B01–B10, **B10a, B10b**, B15–B35. This is the frame's own story. The frame-only loop (B10a) is assumed to be a **permanent** spine beat so that adding tales never edits it; D-057 does not say so in terms, and the user is asked to confirm (§13 G14).
- **Slots** (one per shipped tale, before B15): B11 for the first tale, B12–B14 as movement families for the rest.
- TC-024 is B15–B18 plus its own tale text.

### 11.2 Minimum cut (D-054: frame and TC-024 only)
**Decided (D-057).** The earlier mitigation ("Betal states both rules at B10, in speech") is withdrawn: it contradicted B10 and §13 G1, and no rule is voiced at the opening. The minimum release reads, in order:
1. **B09**: the corpse's first escape (KSS; the king speaks to it, the return comes during his speech, and KSS does not state whether the speech causes it).
2. **B10, B10a, B10b**: the king carries him in silence; Betal provokes him to speak; he speaks; Betal returns to the tree; the king retrieves him; on the next carry Betal opens the first tale (ADAPT loop, no tale).
3. **B15 (fused with the close of B10b)** and **TC-024**: Betal announces one great question and tells the tale.
4. **B16**: the question, **with the penalty stated** (KSS 12.8.188–189 position), then the king, who does not know, falls silent.
5. **B17** onward as written: Betal does not return to the tree.

What the minimum-release reader has then seen: the KSS corpse escape (the return comes during the king's speech; KSS does not state whether the speech causes it) and the adaptation loop at B10a, in which a return visibly follows the king's speech after Betal has provoked it. The "answer, then return" loop of KSS (L5–L8) is **not shown** in this release, and B11, B12–B14 do not exist. The climax is still weaker than with earlier tales. This design answers reviewer C's BLOCKER C1 **on paper only**: whether a one-tale frame reads as complete is a judgment for a Hindi literary reader, and the prose is not yet written. D-054 is unchanged: the complete frame and TC-024 still need approval in Hindi and English before the first release.

### 11.3 Insertions after launch (proposal §8.5)
Each new tale is inserted **before B15**. Its bracketing segments are written through the normal workflow. The only existing text that may change is a neighbouring transition line, via the frame's revision cycle with user approval. The movements are loose, so pacing is re-checked at each addition rather than re-planned.

**One known exception, not decided here.** If the penalty is stated with TC-024's question in the minimum release (§11.4), the first tale added before it takes over that statement (KSS position), so the segment carrying the penalty at B16 changes. That is a spine revision through the frame's revision cycle with the user's approval (FRAME_PROPOSAL §8.5). To keep the change small, draft the penalty as **its own short frame segment**, not inside TC-024's tale text (§13 G15).

### 11.4 Where the penalty and rules are stated (decided, D-057B)
The penalty appears **with the first shipped tale's question**, after the tale, as in KSS (12.8.188–189). If TC-024 is the only tale, it appears with TC-024's question (B16), **before the king falls silent**. No silence rule and no return rule is stated at the opening: the return that follows speech is shown by use at B10a (§4.1). Later KSS narrates returns after the king's silence breaks and has the king recognise the practice (G1), but the frame-only loop and its placement before the first tale are ADAPT.

### 12.2 Religious stereotyping, in more detail
- **Rule:** the frame objects to the **plan**, not the **practice**. Nothing says the rite is wicked in itself. What is wrong is, if Betal is believed, an intent to kill a man who trusted him. That separation is the central safeguard.
- **Specialised knowledge is Essential; sect is not** (charter §3.6, proposal §8.2 item 1).
- **Staging (D-058 item 6):** restraint, and only source-attested details from 12.32.1–14; nothing sect-coded or grotesque is **added**. How much of the attested kit (bone-dust, blood, fat-lamps, corpse-garments) a restrained staging keeps is not settled: reviewers disagreed (A2, A3 against C6) and a Tantra-studies reader and a sensitivity reader are needed.
- **Kept:** ash, lamps, circle, fire, a corpse called by spell, offerings. **Cut:** skull-cup and other Kāpālika-coded objects. **Soft:** "human teeth, eyes, flesh" in KSS 12.32.13–14 is a macabre inventory; default is to drop the inventory and keep that the offerings are dreadful, so the human-parts detail does not do the sectarian work.
- **Honest disagreement.** One view: any rite that ends in a sacrificial plan is the stock figure, label or no label, and only a sensitivity reader can say whether the stripped version still reads that way. Another: a fully generic villain with no craft is a worse, flatter character and a missed chance for a specific, patient, costly ambition. The blueprint takes the second view cautiously and defers to a reader.

### 12.3 Caste, power, human dignity
- No caste marker is placed on the ascetic, Betal, the hanged man or the king. Charter §3.2.5(c): idle tags may be dropped **after** the slate-level pattern audit (§3.2.9); that audit for the frame is pending.
- Burning-ground people are **not used** (D-058 item 5). They were historically the most caste-coded figures the frame could add, and the source has none.
- The treasurer is a person (§3.4.1). The king's secrecy (B06) leaves his own household unaware: the powerful accountable for what they do unseen.

### 12.5 Honest disagreements to preserve
1. **Is leaving the killing open evasion?** A reviewer may say that with the plot rewarding him, neutrality is not neutrality. Another that any resolution (justified or condemned) is invented. Unresolved.
2. **Is the king's silence at TC-024 a missed chance for wisdom?** The source says he did not know. Making it a choice would be a modern arc. Held as in source.
3. **Is Betal's offering-receipt a moral stain on Betal, or on the king, or on the rite?** The frame shows it and does not rank.
4. **Seeding clues early versus keeping the warning a pure surprise.** *Resolved by D-058 item 1:* no seeds. The cost is accepted: the warning is less "fair" in the puzzle sense, and its credibility is left to the reader's own judgment of Betal.
