# TC-021 brief: Anaṅgamañjarī and the three who died of love

Status: brief · Written 2026-11-27 · User look required: no (D-081 list). Answer unchanged; framing choice below is a craft call.

## 1. Source beats
1. In Viśālā, merchant Arthadatta marries his only daughter Anaṅgamañjarī to Maṇivarman of Tāmraliptī but, doting, keeps the couple at home. [KSS 12.28.3–8]
2. She finds her husband hateful "like bitter medicine"; he loves her more than life. [KSS 9–10]
3. While he visits his parents, she sees Kamalākara, the chaplain's son, from her window; love at a glance. [KSS 11–24]
4. In despair she prays at the tank to Caṇḍikā for him in another birth and ties a noose; friend Mālatikā cuts it. [KSS 25–34]
5. Mālatikā finds Kamalākara equally lovesick and arranges a tryst in the garden. [KSS 35–66]
6. She runs and embraces him, "Where are you going? I have you!", and dies of joy. He faints, revives, embraces her body, his heart bursts. [KSS 67–78]
7. At dawn the kin find them; the narrator's aside that bad women disgrace families. [KSS 79–81; aside moved to a character, ours]
8. Maṇivarman returns, sees his wife dead with another man, and, still loving her, dies. [KSS 82–84]
9. Caṇḍī, petitioned for her devotee Arthadatta, orders all three to live "with passion stilled"; they rise as from sleep; Kamalākara goes home ashamed; Arthadatta takes daughter and son-in-law home and celebrates. [KSS 85–91]
10. Question and answer. [KSS 92–96]

Variants: question wording only: KSS "deluded by passion" (*moho*), Ś 20 "blind with desire" (*kāmāndha*), Hindi XX *adhik kāmī* (Barker p. 332; Hindi wording provisional, one AI reading). Answer is the husband in all three. Whether Ś and Hindi narrate the revival was not checked. J 18 unread.

## 2. Riddle and verdict
- Betal's question: among these three, deluded by passion, whose delusion was greater? [KSS 12.28.93]
- Source answer and reasoning: Maṇivarman. The lovers' love was mutual and ripened, and let that be; he, seeing his wife dead with another, at a time for anger, still died of love: "excessively deluded". [KSS 94–96]
- Our answer: same. Per SLATE_AUDIT, "a time for anger" is Betal's provocation, not Vikram's premise; no reason invented for her aversion (the unchosen marriage is enough).
- Honest-difficulty: the husband's love is steadiness to some, folly to others; the lovers died for someone who loved them back, he for someone who never did, but his was the longest love.

## 4. Charter points
- Aside against women (§3.3.1, §3.2.6): remove from the narrator; a kinsman may say it in shame; the tale's structure must not depend on it.
- Unchosen marriage (§3.3.3, §3.3.6): keep it visible as unchosen; do not blame her for revulsion or invent a reason.
- Husband unloved but loving (§3.3.5): neither a fool nor a villain; no collective judgement of men.
- Suicide attempt (noose): staged with restraint, no method detail beyond the source; the rope cut by a friend, not fate.
- Revival "with passion stilled" (§3.3.3): treat as uncanny loss, not a reset the reader is meant to cheer.
- Chaplain's-son label: an idle tag (§3.2.5c); drop, pending slate pattern audit.

## 5. Voice plan
- Hidden layer: a love story told as if it were a joke; heard slowly, it asks what you keep loving after you ought to stop.
- "And you too": (a) at the husband's arrival: whom do you still wait for; (b) at Caṇḍī's reset: what would you give back to be rid of a longing.
- Odd details: why Arthadatta keeps the couple at home (he cannot let her go: a doting father's motive); why Mālatikā helps (friend loyalty, not intrigue for its own sake); why Caṇḍī answers the father not the lovers.
- Plot object: the noose on the aśoka tree; the garden where all three end.
- Melodrama risk (SLATE_AUDIT): handle through restraint and a dry narrator, not pathos.
- Callbacks: none.
